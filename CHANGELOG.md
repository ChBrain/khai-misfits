- a114e66: Add the misfit Best Day for It: a staging of the market-value decline of variable renewables, met in the literature as cannibalisation or the falling value factor. Bright, blowing hard, and the whole fleet is running: it is the best day of the month for generating and the worst day of the month for getting paid, and those are the same fact. - 80b8ede: Add the misfit Clear from Here: a staging of hindsight bias, or creeping determinism, where knowing how something turned out reorganises how foreseeable it appears, so a decision taken under genuine uncertainty is evaluated against a reconstruction in which the actual outcome was always the likely one. Fischhoff gave people accounts of historical episodes and asked how probable each possible ending had been; told which ending had actually occurred, they rated it as far more probable than people judging the same account without that knowledge, and did not notice. Fischhoff and Beyth then had people forecast the outcomes of Nixon's trips to China and the Soviet Union and afterward recall their own predictions, and the recalled forecasts drifted toward what had happened, diverging from what those same people had written down. They were not lying; they could not get back to what they had thought before they knew. This is what makes it a trap rather than a lapse: it is not produced by carelessness and is not cured by care, since people warned of the bias and instructed to disregard the outcome show it regardless, the outcome having already reorganised what the record looks like. The three warning signs that were three signals among two hundred are now the three that pointed at it all along, and the reviewer is seeing that pattern rather than inventing it, because it is genuinely there once the ending is known. The consequence runs both ways: a sound call with a bad result reads as a warning ignored, and a reckless call that came off reads as judgement, so luck is scored as skill and skill as luck in the same motion. Nobody behaves badly. The harmed are entitled to an account and a serious failure met with no inquiry would be worse, the reviewer reads honestly and reaches what the record now supports, and the decider is not being framed, so every part of the process works and arrives at a wrong answer about a right decision. The deeper cost is that the review was convened to teach something and hindsight replaces the lesson with a culprit, since a finding that someone missed an obvious sign yields the instruction to notice obvious signs, which is no lesson at all. A contemporaneous sealed decision log hands the inquiry the fog rather than a reconstruction, and costs upkeep across decisions that will never be reviewed, is composed by someone who knows it may be read at an inquiry, and can be furnished afterward. - ba38175: Add the misfit Fair Warning: a staging of the green paradox, where a credibly announced future cut in demand lowers the expected future price of an exhaustible resource, so the owner brings the extraction forward and the harm arrives sooner than if nothing had been said. There are two things everyone knows about announcing a phase-out and both are true. You announce it early, because a rule that lands overnight strands the workers, the towns and the investors who built their lives around the thing being ended. And you make it credible, because a deadline nobody believes in changes nobody's plans. Neither is a compromise with the other: fairness wants the notice long and effectiveness wants it believed, and the announcement has to be both. The owner of a seam is not deciding whether to sell, since the stuff does not spoil, but when, and the only reason to leave it in the ground another year is that it will be worth more next year net of the cost of lifting it. So an announcement that demand will be cut in fifteen years is an announcement that the price in fifteen years will be lower, the return to waiting has fallen, and the field goes into production in the year the date is set. Not in defiance of the policy but in correct answer to it, by an owner doing the arithmetic any holder of a depleting asset does and citing the announcement openly in documents anyone can read. Nobody is a villain. The legislator chose fifteen years because five would be cruel and thirty would mean nothing. The campaigner pressing for a firmer date and an earlier one is pressing for exactly the right thing, since vagueness is how phase-outs die quietly. And the owner is not defying the policy, it is believing it, which is what the policy asked to be. The cruelty is unusually exact: the standard cure for a policy nobody heeds is to make it more credible, which is correct almost everywhere else and is the accelerant here, so the lever a government can actually reach for is the lever that makes it worse, and an owner who had disbelieved the government would have left the carbon in the ground longer. The corrections that reach the mechanism act on supply rather than demand, buying and retiring reserves, taxing the stock rather than the flow, or assembling a demand cartel with no unregulated buyer left, and each requires paying the owner for wealth the policy is extinguishing, a transfer to precisely the party the public believes should bear the loss. - 02d2f66: Add the misfit Good Enough to Hold: a staging of the regulatory licence view of credit ratings. A rating starts as an opinion, sold to whoever wants it, worth exactly what being right has been worth. Then a rule points at it. The capital requirement says investment grade, the fund mandate says investment grade, the eligibility schedule says investment grade, and at that moment the grade stops being a forecast anybody weighs and becomes a threshold somebody must clear. Nothing changed in the rating and nothing changed in the agency: what changed is what the grade now does. An issuer below the line cannot be held by the institutions that hold most of the money, so it must buy its way above the line, and what it is buying is legibility to the rulebook rather than an assessment of itself, so demand rises as informational content falls, because a permission does not have to be right to be necessary. The rule does a second thing that gets less attention: it decides who may issue the permission, so the designation is itself a licence, the incumbents' pricing power belongs to the regulation rather than to any record, and a more accurate newcomer cannot take business that was never about accuracy.
