Add the misfit Long Enough to Qualify: a staging of immortal time bias, where being counted as treated requires surviving until the treatment occurs, so the waiting cannot contain a death and crediting it to the treated arm manufactures a survival advantage out of the definition. Most bad studies go wrong through something absent or something crooked, a confounder nobody measured, a group that never entered the sample, a number somebody shaded, and the remedies are the obvious ones: measure more, sample better, check the arithmetic, audit the analyst. This one goes wrong with every subject present, every measurement correct and every arithmetic step exact. A cohort opens and exposure is defined by an event that takes time to arrive, the organ becoming available, the prescription finally written, the award finally won, and between entry and that moment the subject cannot die, not by luck but by construction, since a subject who died there would never have been recorded as treated and would sit in the other column. The record carries one field per person rather than one per stretch of a person's life, so a patient untreated in March and treated in September is written down as treated, once, and the intervening months travel into the treated column alongside them by a decision nobody made. The comparison then runs between a group whose person-time contains a stretch in which nobody could die and a group whose does not, and the treated win by the length of their own queue, so the measured benefit moves with the logistics of delivery rather than with anything the treatment does. Nobody is careless: the physician treats at the first moment the treatment can be given, the registrar records who was treated because that is what a register is for, and the analyst compares the only two groups the data offers and computes correctly on them. The fault lives in the step from a record to a denominator, which nobody experiences as an inferential choice, so a hostile reanalyst working the same way returns the same number and every check the field routinely applies confirms the result. The loop closes on the people who made it, the physician acting on a finding built from his own scheduling with no vantage joining the ward to the round. Both corrections cost: counting time rather than people removes the bias and returns an analysis harder to explain to anyone who must act on it, since the wrong one compared two groups of people and the right one compares two kinds of time; and the landmark alternative buys its correctness by discarding every event before a fixed date, which is the early and highest-hazard period, on a date with no correct value, so one chosen after the curves are seen readmits the selection it was brought in to close. Cut to the bookkeeping vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from The Survivors, where the units that never appear are the whole problem and the remedy is to reason about the absent, whereas here every subject is present and counted and the error is in how each observed subject's time is apportioned; from Better in Both, where people move between categories, whereas here nobody moves at all and it is their time that is filed under the wrong heading; from The False Trade-Off, which needs a common effect conditioned on, whereas this needs no collider, no confounder and no conditioning and would appear in a randomised cohort with perfect measurement; and from The Bounce, which needs selection on an extreme plus noise, whereas this is a deterministic consequence of arithmetic that reproduces exactly in data with no randomness in it. Held to its bound: it needs exposure defined by an event occurring after entry and nothing else, and the play is not offered as a claim that observational research is generally untrustworthy, that the affected findings were dishonest, or that the treatments studied do not work, the source literature presenting a correctable design fault and the play staging it as one (Suissa; Lévesque, Hanley, Kezouh and Suissa; Sylvestre, Huszti and Hanley).
