Add the misfit Good Enough to Hold: a staging of the regulatory licence view of credit ratings. A rating starts as an opinion, sold to whoever wants it, worth exactly what being right has been worth. Then a rule points at it. The capital requirement says investment grade, the fund mandate says investment grade, the eligibility schedule says investment grade, and at that moment the grade stops being a forecast anybody weighs and becomes a threshold somebody must clear. Nothing changed in the rating and nothing changed in the agency: what changed is what the grade now does. An issuer below the line cannot be held by the institutions that hold most of the money, so it must buy its way above the line, and what it is buying is legibility to the rulebook rather than an assessment of itself, so demand rises as informational content falls, because a permission does not have to be right to be necessary. The rule does a second thing that gets less attention: it decides who may issue the permission, so the designation is itself a licence, the incumbents' pricing power belongs to the regulation rather than to any record, and a more accurate newcomer cannot take business that was never about accuracy. Meanwhile the investor stops investigating, not out of laziness but out of compliance, since the mandate permits what the schedule permits and a committee that overrode a rule-recognised grade with its own analysis would be exceeding the authority it holds and would answer for the position if it went wrong, so the research quietly stops being done by the parties best placed to do it and every one of them is behaving correctly. Then a downgrade crosses the line and every holder keyed to the threshold is a forced seller in the same hour, not because anyone panicked but because the mandate says so, and a one-notch private opinion has been converted into a mechanism. Nobody is captured and nobody is corrupt: the regulator wrote the reference against the industry's preference and because it could not staff an assessment of every instrument in the market, the treasurer procuring the grade is raising the capital it was hired to raise, and the agency would rather be right and cannot be paid for it. Cut to the certificate-becomes-a-permit vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from Regulatory Capture, which requires the regulator to be serving the industry, in that here it acts in good faith and against the industry and a perfectly incorruptible regulator produces the same result; from Priced In, where the return to gathering information collapses because the price already reveals it, in that here it collapses because a rule creates demand independent of accuracy and no amount of noise restores it; from Metric Fixation, where a measure taken as a target is gamed by the measured party, in that nothing here is gamed and no behaviour changes to hit the number, the corruption being in what the number is for rather than in how it is produced; and from On Good Authority, where deference is a revisable judgement about credibility, in that here it is a legal requirement and the investor who revised it would be exceeding its mandate. Held to its bound: the trap needs a rule referencing a private grade and does not arise where the grade is advisory, and the exit, which was in fact ordered, leaves the regulator to build the assessment at public expense at a scale nobody has funded or to write the substitute standard that becomes the next licence the moment a rule points at it, since nothing in the mechanism is peculiar to credit ratings (Frank Partnoy; Lawrence J. White; the SEC's 1975 designation and the 2010 statutory removal; the Financial Stability Board's principles on mechanistic reliance).
