Add the misfit Saved for Something Else: a staging of competing risks and the censoring fault that follows from ignoring them. Censoring is one of the first things taught in survival analysis and it is taught correctly. A subject who leaves the study, or who is still alive when it closes, has simply stopped being observed, so the honest handling is to count the time they contributed and let them go, assuming they carry on being at risk in the world beyond the data. That assumption is exactly right for the loss the method was built for, which is administrative: the study ended, the calendar ran out, the family moved away, and none of those has anything to do with whether the person was going to have the event. Then the same default meets a different kind of loss. A subject who dies of something else has not stopped being observed but has stopped being at risk, permanently, for a reason that is not independent of the outcome under study, so counting them as censored counts them as though they might still have the event, and the estimated incidence rises above the truth by exactly the amount the model refused to concede. The second consequence is the one that reaches the public: everybody dies of something, so removing one cause of death raises the observed rate of every cause remaining, and the cardiac events among long-term survivors of a working cancer therapy go up, truly and measurably and in the record. Nobody in it is careless. The analyst censored the subjects it stopped watching, which is the taught handling. The oncologist reported more cardiac events in the survivor cohort, which is a true count and which it would be concealing something not to report. The regulator asked whether the therapy caused them, which is precisely the question a regulator exists to ask, and one that shrugged at a rising death rate would be failing. So a treatment that works accumulates a safety signal that is real in the numbers and false about the world, and every figure in it survives every check. The correction is real and its price is unusually exact: the cumulative incidence function and Fine and Gray's regression on the subdistribution hazard estimate the quantity that actually answers the clinical question, and they do it by keeping in the risk set subjects who have already died of the competing cause, so a correct population answer is bought by giving up any individual reading of the hazard ratio. Under that sits the deeper trouble, that cause-specific and subdistribution hazards are both correct, answer different questions, and routinely point in different directions on one dataset, with no experiment to settle which was wanted, so the analyst must choose and the choice is a question about what was asked. Cut to the cure-one-cause-and-appear-to-cause-the-rest vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from The Survivors, whose lesson is to reason about the units that never appear, in that here the absent are counted and counted as though still present, the opposite error; from Long Enough to Qualify, which also misfiles person-time, in that there a stretch of one subject's life is credited to the wrong arm with everyone correctly classified, whereas here no person-time moves at all and a subject who has permanently left the risk set is carried in it; from Better in Both, where reclassification between stages lifts both stages' averages, in that nobody here is reclassified and the movement is in which deaths the denominator will count; and from The False Trade-Off, which requires conditioning on a common effect, in that competing risks needs no collider and would appear in a perfectly randomised trial with complete follow-up. Held to its bound: it needs an event able to remove a subject from risk other than the one being counted, it needs no confounder and no missing data, and it lifts only where the question is stated precisely enough to name which hazard answers it (Jason Fine and Robert Gray; Ross Prentice, John Kalbfleisch and colleagues; Per Kragh Andersen, Ronald Geskus, Theo de Witte and Hein Putter). Borrowed Immunity's two bare Fine cells are given their author's forename in the same change, so that both halves of the shared surname resolve once the house's declared homonyms are read.
