Add the misfit Fair Warning: a staging of the green paradox, where a credibly announced future cut in demand lowers the expected future price of an exhaustible resource, so the owner brings the extraction forward and the harm arrives sooner than if nothing had been said. There are two things everyone knows about announcing a phase-out and both are true. You announce it early, because a rule that lands overnight strands the workers, the towns and the investors who built their lives around the thing being ended. And you make it credible, because a deadline nobody believes in changes nobody's plans. Neither is a compromise with the other: fairness wants the notice long and effectiveness wants it believed, and the announcement has to be both. The owner of a seam is not deciding whether to sell, since the stuff does not spoil, but when, and the only reason to leave it in the ground another year is that it will be worth more next year net of the cost of lifting it. So an announcement that demand will be cut in fifteen years is an announcement that the price in fifteen years will be lower, the return to waiting has fallen, and the field goes into production in the year the date is set. Not in defiance of the policy but in correct answer to it, by an owner doing the arithmetic any holder of a depleting asset does and citing the announcement openly in documents anyone can read. Nobody is a villain. The legislator chose fifteen years because five would be cruel and thirty would mean nothing. The campaigner pressing for a firmer date and an earlier one is pressing for exactly the right thing, since vagueness is how phase-outs die quietly. And the owner is not defying the policy, it is believing it, which is what the policy asked to be. The cruelty is unusually exact: the standard cure for a policy nobody heeds is to make it more credible, which is correct almost everywhere else and is the accelerant here, so the lever a government can actually reach for is the lever that makes it worse, and an owner who had disbelieved the government would have left the carbon in the ground longer. The corrections that reach the mechanism act on supply rather than demand, buying and retiring reserves, taxing the stock rather than the flow, or assembling a demand cartel with no unregulated buyer left, and each requires paying the owner for wealth the policy is extinguishing, a transfer to precisely the party the public believes should bear the loss. Cut to the credible-warning-brings-the-harm-forward vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from Time Inconsistency, where the announcement is not credible and agents discount it, whereas here it is fully believed and being believed is the damage, so the remedy that fixes time inconsistency is the thing that makes this worse; from Sandbag, where agents do less now because tomorrow's standard is indexed to today's output, whereas here they do more now because a future price has been indexed down, opposite in sign and different in what is indexed to what; from Seed Corn, where a manager sacrifices a future they will still be standing in, whereas here nothing is being neglected and there is no myopia to correct; and from Jevons Paradox, where demand responds to a lower effective price and the total rises, whereas here demand is being deliberately cut, the channel is supply, and the total may be unchanged while only its arrival moves. Held to its bound: it needs an exhaustible stock, a forward-looking owner able to accelerate, and a credible announcement, its magnitude is contested and turns on extraction costs and on how much of the stock is ultimately economic, and the play is not offered as an argument that climate policy is counterproductive or that phase-outs should be announced late or vaguely, the source presenting a design problem to be solved inside climate policy rather than a case against it (Sinn; Hotelling held near for the extraction rule).
