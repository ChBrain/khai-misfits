Add the misfit Exactly How Far: a staging of the alliance security dilemma. A guarantee is not a switch but a setting, and somebody has to choose where to leave it. The ally wants it tight, which is not greed but a state whose continued existence depends on whether someone else turns up, and a government that did not press for the answer would be failing at the only task that finally matters. The patron will not say, which is not cowardice but a state declining to hand the decision of when it goes to war to a smaller partner with a border dispute it did not choose, and a government that signed that away would be reckless with the lives it holds. Both are right, and there is one dial. At the tight end: to reassure at all you must be specific, because nothing vague comforts anybody, so the territory and the contingency and the forces are named, and the document that says exactly what will be defended is in the same words the document that says what will not, and it is public, and the adversary reads it. The ally's confidence has been bought by printing a map of the ground where nothing will meet them. At the loose end: stay vague and the choice comes home, and the choice that comes home is precisely the certainty the deterrent consisted of, since a deterrent is not a capability but a belief in somebody else's head. And the ally is not passive while the patron decides: a state that doubts its guarantee arms, builds its own deterrent, moves early while moving is possible, or quietly opens a line to the other side, each prudent from where it stands and each worsening the guarantor's position, with the guarantor having no standing to object since it declined to close the gap. Nor can the hedge be made privately, because a patron's conduct toward one partner is the only evidence the rest have about their own guarantee, so a doubt raised in one capital is priced in all of them and the alliance is chained to its weakest quarrel or comes apart in pieces. Nobody is faithless and nobody is grasping: the adversary reading a public treaty for its omissions is doing the work adversaries exist to do, and the partners inferring their own worth from how another was treated are reasoning correctly from the only evidence in existence. Cut to the each-horn's-cure-is-the-other-horn vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from Over a Barrel, which needs a sunk relation-specific investment and an appropriable quasi-rent and is cured by integration, in that nothing here is sunk and tighter integration makes the entrapment horn worse, the exact inverse of the hold-up remedy; from Someone Will Catch Me, whose single remedy is to precommit to withhold, in that precommitting to withhold is here not the remedy but the abandonment signal in its purest form; from The Calm You Pay For, where suppressing a disturbance in one band must raise it in another because the integral is conserved, in that nothing here is conserved and the trade exists only because three parties read one signal in three directions; and from The Double Bind, where the prohibition on naming the contradiction is what makes the bind, in that everyone here may say plainly what the dilemma is and saying it plainly is precisely what publishes the map. Held to its bound: it needs an ally whose security genuinely rests on the guarantee and an adversary reading it, the crisis reads whatever setting is standing and permits no adjustment, and entrapment is in practice far rarer than the theory predicts because great powers routinely evade what they signed, so the dilemma is real and the size of its tighter horn is disputed (Glenn Snyder; Thomas J. Christensen and Jack Snyder; Thomas Schelling; Robert Jervis and John Herz held near; Michael Beckley). The Success Trap's two bare Christensen cells are given their author's forename in the same change, so both halves of that shared surname resolve once the house's declared homonyms are read.
