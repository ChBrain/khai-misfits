Add the misfit **Once Bitten** (the hot-stove effect): an agent that learns by sampling and avoids what burned it never gathers the evidence that would correct an unduly negative impression, because a bad early draw ends the sampling, while a good early draw keeps the option in use where continued sampling corrects a lucky one. So negative errors self-seal and positive errors self-correct, beliefs from experience run systematically low, and variable options are shunned though some are better than they first seemed. After Denrell (2005; 2007) and Denrell & March (2001), with Twain's cat and the hot stove as the image and Thorndike's law of effect as the antecedent. Held to its bound: a bias conditioned on variable outcomes and the agent's own choice to sample, softening where a bad draw truly means bad, where sampling is forced, or where others' experience can be borrowed.
