Add the misfit Best Day for It: a staging of the market-value decline of variable renewables, met in the literature as cannibalisation or the falling value factor. Bright, blowing hard, and the whole fleet is running: it is the best day of the month for generating and the worst day of the month for getting paid, and those are the same fact. It starts with a developer building where the resource is best, which is not a failing but the job, and a developer that deliberately chose poorer ground to be less correlated with its rivals would be destroying its own returns to raise theirs. But every other developer of the technology is reading the same resource maps and reaching the same conclusion, so the output of the class is not merely similar but very nearly simultaneous, and nobody chose that and nobody can undo it alone. And the plant bids zero, which is also not a failing, since a plant with no fuel has no marginal cost to recover and any other number would misstate its economics. And the operator dispatches cheapest first, because that is what a merit order is for and an operator doing otherwise would be raising system costs on purpose. Put those three correct behaviours together and the market clears at the bottom of the stack in exactly the hours the fleet is producing, which are exactly the hours it has to earn in. So revenue per megawatt-hour falls as a function of how much of the technology has been built, not because it got worse but because there is more of it, and it would go on falling even if the technology got cheaper every year, because cost and value are independent quantities and only one of them is being driven by the deployment. Nor can anyone see it in the numbers that matter politically: installed capacity is up and to the right, levelised cost is down and to the right, both charts are honest, and neither can show the effect, because a levelised cost averages over output without ever asking when the output arrives and comparing that average across an intermittent and a dispatchable technology is not comparing like with like. Then the remedies, which is where it turns. Storage, transmission across weather regimes and demand shifting all break the correlation and all work, and each is paid for by the system while its benefit lands on every generator of the class alike, so no developer will fund what would raise its rivals' revenue as much as its own and a board asked to approve that would refuse. And the remedy that does pay the developer, a long fixed contract at a guaranteed price, makes the project bankable precisely by removing the price signal from the party best placed to respond to it, so the instrument that finances the build is the instrument that suppresses the reason to build the thing that would fix it. Cut to the success-destroys-its-own-revenue vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from Chasing the Price, whose whole mechanism is a lag between the price signal and the supply response, in that here there is no lag at all but simultaneity and the price is destroyed at the moment of production rather than by a decision taken a season earlier; from The Commons, in that nothing is depleted and no stock exists, what is destroyed is a price, and each generator harms itself exactly as much as it harms the others so there is no free rider; from Jevons Paradox, in that there the technology's success raises the quantity consumed while here it lowers the unit price obtained, opposite directions on different variables; and from The Driest Wells, in that there the physical asset degrades while here the best sites stay as good as they ever were and only what the output fetches has changed. Held to its bound: the trap needs units sharing both zero marginal cost and a common weather driver, the decline is a function of penetration rather than a fixed property of the technology, and it is mitigable by flexibility elsewhere in the system, so it is a reason to count value and not a reason to stop building, which is what the source literature says (Lion Hirth; Frank Sensfuss, Mario Ragwitz and Massimo Genoese; Paul Joskow; Falko Ueckerdt, Lion Hirth, Gunnar Luderer and Ottmar Edenhofer).
