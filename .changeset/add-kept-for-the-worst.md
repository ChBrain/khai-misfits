Add the misfit Kept for the Worst: a staging of the broken market for antibiotic innovation, where a last-resort drug's value to the public rises with how carefully it is withheld while its revenue rises only with how much of it is sold, so correct stewardship drives the return on the most valuable compounds toward zero. A medicine is supposed to be the easy case: it helps people, so people take it, so it earns, so the firm makes more like it and a rival tries to beat it, and usefulness and revenue point the same way. For a last-resort antibiotic that inverts exactly and by design, because the right clinical practice on a newly licensed agent against a resistant organism is to not use it, treating with the old generic first and holding the new one for the cases nothing else will touch, since every dose given is selection pressure spent and what the drug is worth is the years before resistance catches it. Meanwhile the money runs on doses, as it does for every medicine and as any payer with a budget would sensibly arrange, since paying for what is used is how a health service avoids paying for what is not. Put the two together and a compound that would save the most lives in the worst future is a compound with almost no sales, on a short acute course against a flat market, and a board setting it beside an oncology programme is not facing a hard decision. The firms leave, and they leave first from the compounds with genuinely novel mechanisms, because those are the expensive ones, so the exit is worst exactly where the science is most needed and no rival steps in, the arithmetic that drove the first firm out being the arithmetic that greets the second. Nobody is a villain: the steward is following the guidance that saves lives and a hospital using the agent freely would be doing real harm to people not yet in the room, the payer refusing to pay for what is not used is doing what a payer is for, the board is discharging a duty it would be criticised for neglecting, and nobody wants the shelf empty, the firms least of all, a company with no antibacterial franchise selling no antibacterials at all. The cost arrives years later at a bedside where the list of options for a resistant isolate has one line and it is blank, and the missing drug did not fail in trials and was not withdrawn for harm but was never made, the reason being that everybody agreed it had to be saved. Delinkage is the instrument that reaches it, a market-entry reward or a subscription paying for access whether or not a course is dispensed, and it costs: public money committed up front for a medicine nobody hopes to need, a compound valued decades before the resistance it is kept for arrives, claims to police from compounds that qualify on the letter and add no new mechanism, and a scheme that must be sustained since a reward withdrawn restores the arithmetic it escaped. Cut to the under-production vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from The Commons, which is over-consumption of something that exists and is cured by restraint, whereas here the restraint is already perfect and the thing is never made, so this is the commons read in a mirror and stewardship is the cause rather than the cure; from The Firefighter's Medal, where two activities are performed inside one organisation and credit is misallocated between them, whereas here there is no second activity and no accounting of credit can fund a product whose optimal consumption is zero; from Not Worth My While, where the benefit is thin and spread across many recipients, whereas here it is enormous and concentrated and simply cannot be billed, billing requiring use; and from Baumol's Cost Disease, where the good keeps being produced and merely becomes dearer, whereas here production stops for a reason unconnected to the cost of making it. Held to its bound: it needs a drug whose correct use is minimal and a reward tied to volume, it lifts wherever those two are separated, and the play is not offered as a claim that pharmaceutical markets generally fail, that the firms which left acted improperly, or that stewardship is a mistake (Outterson, Powers, Daniel and McClellan; Projan).
