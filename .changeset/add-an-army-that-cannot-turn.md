Add the misfit An Army That Cannot Turn: a staging of coup-proofing and its loyalty-versus-effectiveness trade-off, where a ruler who must fear its own armed forces secures itself by measures that are, exactly, the measures that destroy their ability to fight. For most states the army is an instrument and the only question is whether it is good enough, so training it harder and promoting the ablest makes it more useful without making it more dangerous. For a ruler whose gravest and nearest threat is the army itself the relation inverts, because the things that make a force able to fight are the things that make it able to act: officers the troops will follow, formations that can concentrate and move, units that talk to one another and take initiative without waiting to be told, every one of them a coup capability as much as a combat capability, since a coup is an operation. The play stages the three levers as three distinct vectors with three distinct costs rather than one lever named three times. The structural lever raises a second force that answers separately so no single command can seize the state, and the same separateness means the two cannot be concentrated by one order and compete for one supply. The personnel lever selects officers on what can be vouched for and moves commanders before a following forms, and takes with it the years in which a commander learns his ground and a unit becomes a unit. The informational lever suppresses lateral communication and curtails exercise above battalion scale, because a division moving at scale cannot be told apart from a division moving on the capital, and leaves an army that cannot combine arms and cannot manoeuvre, manoeuvre being lateral communication under fire. Nobody in it is a monster. The coup threat is frequent in the region, recent in the country and personally fatal, so a ruler who disregarded it would not be principled but dead; the guard commander is doing well the job it was given; the security man is preventing exactly what he was appointed to prevent; the officers passed over are not accused and mostly not suspected, the practice selecting against a capability that a perfectly loyal officer also has; and nobody wants the army to lose, the ruler least of all, an army that loses being a throne that falls. The remedy is known and it is the threat: promote on merit, exercise at formation scale, push initiative down, let units speak freely, which is not merely an expensive list but identically the list of capabilities required to mount a coup, so the cure cannot be separated from the danger by any amount of good faith on either side. Cut to the control-versus-capability vector no staged misfit holds, distinct from Rising to Unfit, where competence is the promotion criterion and merely fails to transfer, whereas here ability is the disqualifying trait and is selected against on purpose; from The Dilbert Principle, where the incapable are routed around, whereas here the capable are removed for being capable; from The Dead Sea Effect, where the talented leave of their own accord, whereas here nobody chooses to leave and loyalists are installed by a deliberate hand; from Pournelle's Iron Law, where the drift is unintended and the organisation still believes it pursues its purpose, whereas here the ruler is clear-eyed and the trade is made knowingly; and from The Brittle Optimum, where slack is trimmed for efficiency and the lost resilience is a by-product nobody sought, whereas here capability is removed for control by someone who can name exactly what it costs. Held to its bound: it needs a credible internal threat and it lifts where the external threat dominates, and the play is not offered as a claim that authoritarian armies are uniformly incompetent or that coup-proofing explains every defeat, the source literature presenting a trade with terms, rulers under severe external pressure taking the other side of it, fighting better for it, and becoming easier to overthrow, and one army carrying the trade struck differently in different units (Quinlivan; Talmadge; Roessler held near to mark the distinction).
