## Name

More Than It Can Back.
In 1960 Robert Triffin told the United States Congress that the arrangement holding up the postwar world of money contained a contradiction that would one day break it. The dollar was then the world's reserve currency, convertible to gold at thirty-five dollars an ounce, and every country held dollars as its reserves. That was convenient, and it was doomed. A growing world economy needs a growing stock of reserves to settle its trade and cushion its shocks, and the only way for the rest of the world to accumulate dollars is for the United States to send them out, by running persistent deficits in its balance of payments. But every dollar sent abroad is a claim on American gold, and the American gold stock was finite. So the very deficits that supplied the world its reserves were piling up foreign claims faster than the gold behind them could grow, and one day the claims would exceed the gold, and the promise to convert would become a promise no one could believe (Triffin).
And here is the bind, stated plainly. To furnish the world enough reserves, the issuer must run deficits that send out more of its currency than its backing can cover. To keep its currency credible, it must stop the deficits, and then the world is starved of the reserves it needs, and trade and growth are choked for want of them. The issuer cannot do both. Supply enough and you erode confidence; hold confidence and you supply too little. The two jobs the reserve currency is asked to do, to be furnished freely to the world and to hold its value surely, pull in opposite directions, and no amount of good management dissolves the conflict, because it is not a failure of management but a property of asking one nation's money to be everyone's reserve (Triffin).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The issuer is not a spendthrift debasing its money: it runs the deficits because the world asks for its currency, and it is repaid in a real privilege, the power to borrow from the world cheaply in money it prints itself. The world is not a mob: it holds the reserve currency because it genuinely needs reserves and has nothing better to hold. And the holder who, seeing the claims outrun the backing, moves to convert its dollars to gold before the others do is not panicking: it is reading a real deterioration correctly and protecting itself soundly. The loss is authored by no one. It is the confidence that must erode as the reserve role forces the issuer to supply past its backing, and the misfit is that the very trust that makes a currency the world's reserve is what the world's demand for it steadily undermines.
And here is what became of Triffin's warning. Through the 1960s foreign dollar holdings grew, exactly as he said, until they stood well past the American gold stock, and the promise to convert became plainly incredible. In 1971 the United States closed the gold window and refused to convert dollars to gold, and the Bretton Woods system ended. The contradiction Triffin had named eleven years earlier had run its course, not because anyone blundered at the end, but because the arrangement had the break built into it from the start.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The bind comes from lodging the world's reserve on one nation's currency, so the true remedy is to lift it off, to create a reserve asset issued by no single country, which the world can accumulate without draining anyone's backing. Keynes proposed exactly this at Bretton Woods, a supranational money he called bancor, and the world chose the dollar instead; later the International Monetary Fund created the Special Drawing Right, a reserve asset of just this kind. But such an asset asks nations to cede a piece of their monetary sovereignty and to govern a shared money together, a collective undertaking that the SDR has only marginally achieved, so the reserve role stays lodged on a national currency and the bind returns.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The dilemma is sharpest under a fixed convertibility promise, where the backing is a finite stock of gold that the claims can visibly outrun. When the gold link was cut and the world moved to fiat, floating money, the promise that could be broken on demand was gone, and the sharp convertibility crisis softened into a slower question: whether the issuer can keep supplying the world's safe assets without its external position becoming unsustainable. That milder bind is real and it is debated, and it is not a law of imminent collapse, and the issuer's exorbitant privilege is exactly why it does not wish the role away. The reserve-currency bind, the deficit-driven supply of reserves, and the erosion of convertibility are Triffin's; the supranational reserve asset is Keynes's bancor and the IMF's SDR; the modern restatement and the privilege are the work of later economists. The house stages the collision and holds the bound in view: that where one nation's money is the world's reserve, the issuer must supply more than it can back, no one at fault, and the only true exit is a money no nation issues.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a single national currency serves as the world's reserve asset, the issuer must supply ever more of it to meet a growing world's demand for reserves, and the very outflow that supplies it accumulates claims against the issuer faster than the backing behind them, so the more the asset is supplied the less credible its value becomes, and the issuer cannot both furnish the world's liquidity and preserve confidence in what it furnishes.
It runs on one nation's currency doing double duty.
A single national currency is asked to be both the issuer's money and the world's reserve, two jobs in one object (Triffin).
And the world thirsts for reserves. A growing world economy needs an ever larger stock of reserves, and only more of the reserve currency can slake it (Triffin).
And the issuer must run deficits to supply it. The only channel by which the world accumulates the reserve currency is the issuer sending it abroad, so meeting the thirst requires persistent deficits that pile up claims against the issuer (Triffin).
And the backing thins. Those claims outrun the backing behind them, gold or fiscal capacity, so the ratio that underwrites the currency's value falls the more of it is supplied (Triffin).
And confidence fails. Seeing the claims outrun the backing, a rational holder converts or diversifies before the others, and the credibility built by trust is undone by the supplying, a run with real cause and no villain (Triffin).
And it is authored by no one. The issuer supplies because the world demands and is paid a privilege, the world holds because it needs reserves, and the doubter reads a real thinning, so the bind is structural and not anyone's misdeed.
And the way out is dear. Only a reserve asset no single nation issues resolves the bind, and it asks nations to cede sovereignty and govern together, so it is proposed and barely built (Keynes; the SDR).
And the whole is held against its limit. The bind is sharpest under a fixed convertibility promise and softens under fiat, floating money into a slower question of sustainability, real and debated but not a law of imminent collapse (Farhi, Gourinchas & Rey).
