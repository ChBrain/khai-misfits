# Play: The Loud Few

## Name

The Loud Few.
A sugar tariff raises the price of sugar for everyone and hands the gain to a handful of growers. Each grower stands to make millions, so the growers organise, hire lobbyists, and press their case. Each shopper loses a few dollars a year, too little to notice and far too little to be worth a day off work to fight, so the shoppers do nothing. The tariff passes. Mancur Olson gave the general account of why. Contrary to the comfortable assumption that people who share an interest will naturally band together to advance it, Olson showed that rational, self-interested members of a large group will not act to secure their common interest, because the good they would win is a collective good: once it is provided, no member can be shut out of it, so each would rather let the others bear the cost and enjoy it for free (Olson; Samuelson).
And here is what the size of the group does. In a large group each member's share of the collective good is thin, and their own single contribution is too small to change whether the good is won at all, so the cost of acting outweighs the sliver it buys them, and each rationally sits out. The group stays latent: its shared interest is real, and it is never advanced. In a small group, by contrast, at least one member's stake in the good is large enough to be worth the whole cost of providing it alone, so the small group is privileged, and it acts (Olson).
And here is the asymmetry that opens. Where a policy concentrates its benefit on a few and spreads its cost over the many, the few each have much at stake and organise, while each of the many has so little at stake that resisting is not worth the trouble. So the organised few prevail over the unorganised many, and outcomes tilt to concentrated interests, though the diffuse majority would gain more in sum than the few they lose to (Wilson; Olson).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The few who organise are not conspirators: each simply has a stake large enough to be worth acting on, the same calculus the many run and rationally decline. The many who sit out are not lazy or ignorant: their share is genuinely too small and their single voice too slight to change the outcome, so free-riding is the sound response. And the office that grants the few their way is not bought: it acts on whoever organised to reach it, and only the few did, so it hears one voice where the many sent none. The loss is authored by no one. It is the collective good the many never provide themselves and the tilt of every outcome toward the few, and the misfit is that the very free-rider logic that is rational for each member of the large group is what leaves the group latent and the field to the few.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. A large group can be moved, but not by the collective good itself, which each would free-ride on. It can be moved by a selective incentive: a private benefit given only to those who contribute, separate from the cause, a members-only good that the free-rider does not get. Or by coercion, compulsory membership that removes the choice to free-ride. Or by an entrepreneur who bundles the cause with private inducements, or an organisation encompassing enough to internalise the whole interest. But each is costly and none is general: selective incentives are dear to supply and can capture the organisation for their own sake, so the cause becomes a vehicle for the sweetener; coercion trades the free-riding for a loss of liberty; and the encompassing organisation is exactly what the asymmetry makes hardest to build.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The failure is a tendency, not a law. It holds insofar as the group is large, the good non-excludable, and no selective incentive is at hand, and it breaks where those conditions do: large groups do mobilise, through moral entrepreneurs who supply the private reason, through mass movements, and through a fall in the cost of coordinating that lowers the price of acting for each. The theory of groups, the group-size logic, the latent and privileged groups, the selective incentive, and the tilt to concentrated interests are Olson's; the non-excludable public good and the free-rider are Samuelson's, the antecedent the theory extends; and the concentrated-benefits, diffuse-costs reading is Wilson's. The house stages the asymmetry and holds the bound in view: that where a benefit is diffuse and the cost of acting concentrated, the many stay latent and the few prevail, no one at fault, and the many move only when something gives them a private reason the shared good cannot.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a benefit is spread thin across a large group and the cost of acting falls on each who would provide it, every member of the diffuse many has too small a share to justify the cost, so each rationally free-rides and the large group stays latent, while a small group whose members each hold a concentrated stake finds it worth acting, so the organised few prevail over the unorganised many and outcomes tilt to concentrated interests.
It runs on a benefit that is a collective good.
A collective good is non-excludable: once it is provided, no member can be shut out of it, so each would rather let the others pay and enjoy it for free (Samuelson).
And the size of the group decides. In a large group each member's share of the good is thin and their single contribution imperceptible, so acting costs more than the sliver it buys, and each rationally sits out, and the group stays latent (Olson).
And the small group is privileged. At least one of its few members holds a stake large enough to bear the whole cost of acting alone, so the small group forms and acts where the large one cannot (Olson).
And the asymmetry tilts the field. Where benefit is concentrated on a few and cost diffuse over many, the few organise and the many do not, so the few prevail and outcomes bend to concentrated interests (Wilson; Olson).
And it is authored by no one. The few's organising is sound, the many's sitting out is sound, and the office hears only who shows up, so the tilt is a structure's doing and not a villain's.
And the way out is dear. A large group moves only on a selective incentive, coercion, or an entrepreneur who bundles a private reason with the cause, each costly and prone to capture, none free (Olson).
And the whole is held against its limit. The failure is a tendency conditioned on group size, non-excludability, and the absence of selective incentives, and it breaks where a movement, an entrepreneur, or cheap coordination rouses the many (Olson).
