# Play: The Spare Has to Eat

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Spare Has to Eat.
Two eggs are laid where only one bird can be raised. The second is not a mistake and not an accident of fecundity: it is insurance. First eggs in these species hatch only about half to six times in ten, so in something like four years in ten the first produces nothing and the second is the whole of that season.
Then, six times in ten, the first hatches. The insurance is now redundant, and redundant is not the same as free, because a spare chick eats. It is removed within days, by the chick that hatched first.

## Arc

The bend is this: redundancy that consumes the resource it was held against cannot be kept once it is no longer needed, so a policy that works has to be destroyed in exactly the cases where the thing it insured went well.
It runs on two facts, neither of them anybody's doing: an egg costs something to make, and the food a parent can bring back is set far from the nest and is not known when the eggs are laid.
Take the premium first. Laying the second egg is right. The comparative record says so plainly: hatching success of first eggs runs about fifty-one to sixty-one per cent in the species that do this, and at least eighty-five per cent in the species that lay a single egg and do not. The females invest about the same in each egg, so the **cost of insurance is roughly the same everywhere and the benefit is not**, and the trait sits exactly where first eggs fail often. That is not a coincidence, it is the test.
Then take the claim. In the years the first egg fails, the second is not a spare at all: it is the season. Every fledgling those years is one that would not exist without the extra egg, and the whole practice is paid for out of those years.
And then take the six years in ten when it is not needed. There is one ration, brought back from a foraging range nobody at the nest can see. It raises one. So the elder chick removes the younger, within days of hatching, and the parent does not intervene. **Nothing in that is a failure.** The elder is competing for a supply that cannot carry two. The parent laid correctly. The younger was not surplus to a plan: it was the plan, for a different year that did not happen.
Reach for a settlement and there is not one. Keep the second chick and both are underfed on a ration for one. Lay only one egg and lose four seasons in ten. Lay the second later, which is what hatching asynchrony already does, and the asynchrony is what makes the removal quick rather than slow. Know in advance which year the first egg will fail, which is the only thing that would actually solve it, and nothing at the nest has any way to know it.

## Triggers

**[Two Eggs, One Chick](plot_two_eggs_one_chick.md)**
A clutch of two in a species that fledges one. Closes when the second egg is established as insurance rather than as excess, because the parent invests about the same in each and only ever raises one.

**[Four Times in Ten](plot_four_times_in_ten.md)**
Cue: the first egg failing to hatch, which it does about four times in ten. The second is then not a spare but the entire season. Closes when the premium is shown to be paid for out of these years and only these years.

**[Within Days of Hatching](plot_within_days_of_hatching.md)**
Cue: both eggs hatching. One ration, two chicks, and an elder that hatched first and is larger. Closes when the removal is shown to be the elder competing for a supply that cannot carry two, with no failure by anybody at any point.

**[The Premium Was Paid Anyway](plot_the_premium_was_paid_anyway.md)**
Cue: the six seasons in ten when the first egg hatches and the insurance is redundant. Closes when redundancy is shown not to be free, because the spare consumes the very supply it was held against, so it cannot simply be kept.

**[The Ones That Lay Only One](plot_the_ones_that_lay_only_one.md)**
Cue: the related species whose first eggs hatch at least eighty-five per cent of the time. They lay one egg and there is no siblicide. Closes when the comparison is shown to be the test: the trait sits precisely where the failure rate makes the premium worth paying.

## Stakes

Whether a working insurance can be held once the risk it covered has passed.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether anybody at the nest miscalculates, because the parent lays the right number, the elder competes for a ration that cannot carry two, and the comparative record shows the practice sitting exactly where it pays, but whether a spare that eats can survive being unnecessary. The production cannot make it survive. It can only show that the premium is real and is paid every season, that the claim is made in the four seasons in ten when the first egg fails and every fledgling of those years depends on it, that the removal in the other six is competition rather than cruelty and takes days rather than a season, that the related species which do not need the insurance do not buy it, and that the one thing which would resolve it, knowing in advance which year the first egg will fail, is not available at the nest at the time the eggs are laid.
