# A Reason Too Many: Reference

The research warrant for **A Reason Too Many**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon the
literature calls **motivation crowding-out** (and, in the psychology, the **overjustification
effect**): Deci and Ryan's finding that an extrinsic reward can undermine intrinsic motivation,
Gneezy and Rustichini's day-care fine that a price on a duty raised the very thing it penalised, and
Frey and Jegen's crowding theory, all cited below, held with the finding that crowding-out is a
conditional effect and not a law, since incentives usually work and displace a motive only under
specific conditions.

## Line of Work

Staging motivation crowding-out as an **incentive-displaces-intrinsic-motive structure**: a behaviour
is already being carried by an internal reason, an interest taken for its own sake, a duty felt, an
act of care or altruism, and someone attaches an explicit extrinsic incentive to it, a payment for
doing it or a fine for not. The ordinary expectation is that the incentive adds to the motive and the
behaviour rises. But where the prior motive was intrinsic or moral, the incentive can displace it:
it reframes a self-chosen or obligated act as a market transaction, so a parent fined for collecting a
child late reads the fine as a price and buys the extra time with a clear conscience, lateness rising
rather than falling (Gneezy and Rustichini), and a citizen offered money to accept a civic burden
refuses what they would have shouldered for nothing (Frey and Oberholzer-Gee). The added
reason crowds out the stronger one it was meant to reinforce, and the net effect is a fall in the
behaviour the incentive was meant to raise. The governing law is not that incentives fail in general,
but that **where a behaviour is carried by an intrinsic or a moral motive, attaching an explicit
extrinsic incentive to it can crowd out that motive, reframing the act as a priced transaction, so
the incentive reduces the behaviour it was meant to increase, and the displaced motive does not
simply return when the incentive is withdrawn**. The play models the documented mechanics: the
reframing of a moral duty as a price and its persistence after the price is removed (Gneezy and
Rustichini), the penalty entering an incomplete social contract and changing what the parties take
themselves to owe each other (Gneezy and Rustichini), the crowding of prosocial motivation and the
conditions under which it happens (Titmuss; Frey and Jegen), and the civic burden refused once it is
paid for (Frey and Oberholzer-Gee), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, and not as a claim
that paying people never works.

It is a motivational interaction no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its
nearest neighbours. It is distinct from The Cobra Effect, where a reward set to reduce a problem is
farmed and breeds the thing it pays to remove: there the incentive is gamed, exploited to the letter
against its purpose, whereas here nothing is gamed, the incentive works exactly as a price and is
answered honestly, and the harm is that being a price at all is what displaces the non-priced motive
that was doing the work. It is distinct from Metric Fixation, where a number chosen to stand for a
goal is hit truthfully while the goal it stood for dies: there the engine is a proxy optimised until
it parts from its target, whereas here there is no proxy and no measure, only an incentive that
extinguishes a motive rather than a metric that drifts from a goal. It is distinct from the house's **Paid to Stop Caring**, which stages the other half of this
literature and is the reason the two must not be read as one: there a single person, seeing itself
paid to do what it once did freely, infers its own motive from the payment, so the mechanism is
self-perception and it runs entirely inside one head. Here nothing is inferred about the self. A
penalty enters what Gneezy and Rustichini call an incomplete contract, social or private, and
changes what the parties take themselves to owe one another, so a duty becomes a service that can be
bought and the buying carries no guilt: two parties, a relation between them, and a price where an
obligation stood. That is why the day-care lateness did not fall back when the fine was withdrawn,
and why the two plays share no work between them. It is distinct from Moral
Licensing, where a good deed is banked as credit that buys a later lapse: there a prior virtue funds
a later vice through a moral accounting, whereas here the incentive removes the reason to act rather
than granting a credit to be spent, so the motive is displaced at the moment of the incentive and not
drawn down afterward.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the one
  who sets the incentive as a fool or the one who responds to it as a cynic, because the source finding
  it stages is that both act reasonably: offering a reward or a fine to strengthen a behaviour is the
  ordinary, sensible move, and answering a fine as the price it now is, or losing interest in a task
  once it has become paid work, is a rational and honest response, not a betrayal (Gneezy and
  Rustichini; Deci and Ryan). The loss is authored by no one: it is the intrinsic or moral motive
  displaced by the very incentive meant to reinforce it, and the misfit is that the attempt to buy more
  of a willing act is what makes it less willing.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The remedies exist,
  withholding the incentive where an intrinsic or moral motive already carries the act, paying enough
  that the price effect outweighs the crowding rather than too little, framing a reward as recognition
  and support rather than as control, and removing an incentive before it hardens (Frey and Jegen;
  Gneezy and Rustichini), but none is free or certain: it is hard to know in advance whether a motive
  is strong enough to be worth protecting, a large enough payment is expensive and can still signal that
  the task is unpleasant, and once a motive is crowded out it does not simply return when the incentive
  is withdrawn, so the day-care lateness stayed high after the fine was dropped. The reckoning is that
  motivation is not additive, and an incentive laid on a motive it did not create can subtract more than
  it adds.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the undermining of intrinsic motivation by reward
  and the loss of self-determination are Deci and Ryan's, the overjustification effect is Lepper,
  Greene, and Nisbett's, the reframing of a fine as a price and its persistence are Gneezy and
  Rustichini's, the prosocial reading is Titmuss's and is empirically contested, and the crowding
  theory with its conditions is Frey, Jegen, and Oberholzer-Gee's. The mechanism is carried as
  carefully as the finding: that crowding-out requires a pre-existing intrinsic or moral motive and an
  incentive perceived as controlling or as pricing the act, that the harm the play centers is the
  displacement of that motive and not a mere failure of the reward to add, and above all that the effect
  is conditional and not universal, since incentives ordinarily do increase behaviour and crowd out
  motivation only under specific conditions, sometimes crowding it in instead (Frey and Jegen). The
  scope is held to incentives laid on intrinsically or morally motivated behaviour, and not stretched
  into a claim that rewards never work or that paying people is always a mistake.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Intrinsic Motive**: carries the pre-existing internal or moral reason the behaviour ran on
  (Deci and Ryan; Titmuss), an interest taken for its own sake, a duty felt, an act of care, the thing
  that was doing the work before any incentive and the thing the incentive puts at risk.
- **The Incentive**: carries the extrinsic reward or fine attached to the behaviour (Gneezy and
  Rustichini), the payment for doing it or the penalty for not, offered in the ordinary expectation
  that it will add to the motive and raise the act.
- **The Reframe**: carries the mechanism of displacement (Gneezy and Rustichini; Lepper, Greene, and
  Nisbett), the shift by which the act stops being a self-chosen or obligated one and becomes a priced
  transaction, so a fine is read as a price and a paid pastime as work, the incentive changing what the
  act means rather than only what it pays.
- **The Crowding-Out**: carries the net displacement (Deci and Ryan; Frey and Jegen), the intrinsic or
  moral motive lost by more than the extrinsic one adds, so the behaviour the incentive was meant to
  raise falls instead, the reason too many subtracting the reason that mattered.
- **The Residue**: carries the irreversibility (Gneezy and Rustichini), the motive that does not simply
  return when the incentive is withdrawn, so the behaviour is left lower than before it was ever
  incentivised, the day-care lateness that stayed high after the fine was removed.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge (Frey and Jegen; Gneezy and Rustichini), the
  fact that crowding-out is not a law but a conditional effect, holding where the prior motive is strong
  and the incentive is small or controlling, reversing to a plain price effect or even crowding motivation
  in where the incentive is large enough or framed as support, so the trap is real but bounded.
