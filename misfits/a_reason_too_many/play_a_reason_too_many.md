# Play: A Reason Too Many

## Estate

## Name

A Reason Too Many.
A day-care faced a small, familiar problem: a few parents came late to collect their children, and a teacher had to wait. So the day-care did the sensible thing and set a fine for lateness, expecting that a penalty on the behaviour would reduce it. Lateness went up. The parents, who had been coming on time out of consideration for the teacher, now read the fine as a price: a few coins buys a little more time, and cheaply, with no guilt about it, because the debt has been paid at the desk. The moral reason to be on time, do not leave a person waiting, had been replaced by a transaction, and the transaction was a bargain. And when the day-care later dropped the fine, the lateness did not go back down: the price was gone, but so was the norm it had displaced, and it did not return (Gneezy and Rustichini).
And here is the shape beneath the story. A behaviour was already being carried by a motive that needed no payment: an interest taken for its own sake, a duty felt, an act of care or of giving. Onto that motive someone lays an extrinsic incentive, a reward for doing the thing or a fine for not, in the ordinary and usually correct expectation that a price added to a motive raises the behaviour. But where the prior motive was intrinsic or moral, the incentive does not simply add. It changes what the act means. A thing done for its own sake, once paid, becomes work; a duty, once priced, becomes a service you can buy your way out of. And the new, weaker reason crowds out the old, stronger one it was piled onto, so the behaviour falls (Deci and Ryan; Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett).
And it is the same wherever a willing act meets a price. Titmuss argued that paying for blood could lower the supply and the quality a voluntary system gives, by crowding out the gift; the claim is contested and is carried here lightly, but the mechanism it named is real and shows again and again: offer people money to accept a civic burden and their willingness to accept it can fall, because a duty has been turned into a distasteful trade (Frey and Oberholzer-Gee). Pay a small amount for what people were doing for free, and they can do less of it than they did unpaid, because a little money says the task is worth little and the pleasure of it is now beside the point (Gneezy and Rustichini). The reason too many is the extrinsic one, and it does not stack on the intrinsic reason, it displaces it.
And here is the cost, and why it is quiet and hard to undo. The one who set the incentive is not a fool and the one who responds to it is not a cynic: offering a reward to get more of an act is sensible, and answering a fine as the price it now is, or losing interest in a task once it has become paid work, is honest and rational. So the loss is authored by no one. It is the intrinsic or moral motive, displaced by the very incentive meant to reinforce it, and it does not come back when the incentive is removed, because the act has been reframed and the shared understanding that carried it is gone. The behaviour is left lower than before it was ever incentivised, and the harm reads as a puzzle rather than a consequence, because the thing that caused it was a reward.
And here is what a careful hand does, and why the fix is hard to hold. The remedy is to know when not to pay: to withhold an incentive where a motive already carries the act, to pay enough that the price outweighs the crowding rather than too little, to frame a reward as recognition and support rather than as control, and to move before a motive hardens into a habit of being paid (Frey and Jegen; Gneezy and Rustichini). But none of this is free or certain. It is hard to know in advance whether a motive is strong enough to be worth protecting, a large enough payment is expensive and can still signal that the task is unpleasant, and once a motive is crowded out it does not simply return. Motivation is not additive, and an incentive laid on a motive it did not create can subtract more than it adds.
And so the misfit is not that incentives fail but that they can undo the willingness they were meant to buy. The undermining of intrinsic motivation by reward is Deci and Ryan's, the overjustification effect is Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett's, the fine that became a price and stayed one is Gneezy and Rustichini's, the gift crowded out is Titmuss's and is contested, and the crowding theory with its conditions is Frey, Jegen, and Oberholzer-Gee's. The house stages the displacement and holds the bound in view: that incentives ordinarily do raise behaviour, and crowd out a motive only under particular conditions, sometimes crowding it in, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that a reason too many can cost you the reason that mattered.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a behaviour is carried by an intrinsic or a moral motive, attaching an explicit extrinsic incentive to it can crowd out that motive, reframing the act as a priced transaction, so the incentive reduces the behaviour it was meant to increase, and the displaced motive does not simply return when the incentive is withdrawn.
It runs on a behaviour already carried by an internal reason.
An act is done for its own sake, or out of duty, or care, or altruism, a motive that needs no payment and was doing the work before any incentive (Deci and Ryan; Titmuss).
And onto that motive someone lays a price. A reward for doing the act or a fine for not, offered in the ordinary and usually correct expectation that an incentive adds to a motive and raises the behaviour (Gneezy and Rustichini).
And the price changes what the act means. Where the prior motive was intrinsic or moral, the incentive reframes the act as a transaction, a duty into a service that can be bought, a pleasure into paid work (Gneezy and Rustichini; Lepper, Greene, and Nisbett).
And the new reason displaces the old. Motivation is not additive, so the extrinsic motive does not stack on the intrinsic one but crowds it out, and the behaviour the incentive meant to raise falls instead (Deci and Ryan; Frey and Jegen).
And the loss is authored by no one. The one who set the incentive acted sensibly and the one who answered it acted honestly, so the displaced motive is a harm with no culprit, easily misread as merely paying too little.
And it does not return when the price is removed. The act has been reframed and the shared understanding that carried it is gone, so the day-care lateness stayed high after the fine was dropped, the behaviour left lower than before it was ever incentivised (Gneezy and Rustichini).
And a careful hand can bound it, at a cost. Withhold the incentive where a motive already carries the act, pay enough rather than too little, frame a reward as recognition not control, and act before the motive hardens (Frey and Jegen; Gneezy and Rustichini), each hard to judge in time.
And the whole is held against its own limit. Crowding-out is conditional and not a law: incentives ordinarily raise behaviour, and displace a motive only under particular conditions, sometimes crowding it in, so the trap is real and common and bounded (Frey and Jegen).

## Stakes

Whether a willing behaviour can be strengthened by rewarding it, when the reward can displace the very motive that was carrying it.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward more of the act, sought by a price, and away from it, because the price reframes a self-chosen or moral act as a transaction and crowds out the stronger motive it was laid upon. The production cannot make the incentive simply add to the motive, because motivation is not additive: an extrinsic reason piled on an intrinsic one changes what the act means rather than stacking with it, so the value sought and the motive lost are the one incentive seen twice. The remedies, withholding the incentive, paying enough, framing it as recognition, moving before the motive hardens, each spend real judgement and real cost and none is certain, since whether a motive is strong enough to protect is hard to know in advance and a displaced motive does not simply return. It can only show that no one schemed and no one blundered, that the payer who set the reward and the person who answered it as a price are both acting reasonably, that the fall in the behaviour is a displacement and not a failure to pay enough, and that the willingness lost leaves the act lower than before because the reason that carried it was crowded out. And it holds the bound in view, because crowding-out is conditional and not a law: incentives ordinarily raise behaviour and displace a motive only under particular conditions, sometimes crowding it in (Frey and Jegen), so the whole of the misfit is that a reason too many can cost the reason that mattered, but only where the reason that mattered was there to be lost.
