# Pitch: Once You Pay for It

## Taxonomy

[A Reason Too Many](play_a_reason_too_many.md). The default key the whole production is read in.

## Owner

- Project: khai-misfits

## Tenor

Well-meaning and sensible.
A behaviour someone would like more of, and the ordinary move of attaching a reward or a fine to get it, nothing cynical in the offer and nothing dishonest in the response.
Nothing false in it: rewarding a behaviour to strengthen it is the ordinary, usually correct move, since incentives ordinarily do raise the act.

## Undertow

Under the sensible offer, a motive that needed no payment.
The act was already carried by an intrinsic or a moral reason, and putting a price on it reframes a self-chosen or obligated act as a transaction, a duty into a service you can buy, a pleasure into paid work (Gneezy and Rustichini).
No cynicism and no blunder: the one who sets the price means to add to the motive, and the one who answers the price as the price it now is, is being honest, and that honest answer is exactly what displaces the stronger reason.

## Nerve

The plainness of paying for more of a good thing, and the catch that the payment can buy less of it:
that motivation is not additive, so an extrinsic reason piled on an intrinsic one does not stack but crowds it out, the value sought and the motive lost the one incentive seen twice,
so the reward meant to raise the act lowers it, and the motive does not return when the price is taken away, the behaviour left lower than before it was ever paid for.

## Echo

After the run: the day-care fuller of late pickups than before the fine, the willing act done less once it was bought, a motive displaced by the very reward meant to reinforce it, a loss authored by no one and misread as paying too little (Gneezy and Rustichini; Deci and Ryan).
And the remedies, each at a cost and none certain: withhold the incentive where a motive already carries the act, pay enough rather than too little, frame a reward as recognition and not control, and move before the motive hardens (Frey and Jegen).
And the bound held in view, because crowding-out is conditional and not a law: incentives ordinarily raise behaviour and displace a motive only under particular conditions, sometimes crowding it in, so the whole of it is that a reason too many can cost the reason that mattered, but only where that reason was there to be lost.
