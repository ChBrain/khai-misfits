# Who Took It Out: Reference

The research warrant for Who Took It Out.
The setting is the defined process of the software maturity frameworks; the play runs on the asymmetry between
adding a control and removing one, and on the measured difference in how acts and omissions are judged, all cited
below.

## Line of Work

Staging the procedure as **a document that can only grow, because its two directions are not the same kind of
move**: each step is added after a real incident, by a competent review acting in good faith, and genuinely
addresses what went wrong; the additions are increments that nobody weighs against the total, so the total is
never proposed and never approved; a removal cannot be an increment, being a single act with a named owner and a
date; and harm following an act is judged more harshly than the same harm following an omission, so the person
who would have to sign is reading a real asymmetry rather than being timid. The play models the documented
mechanics, the incident that warrants the step, the step that would have caught it, the line nobody weighs, the
signature a removal requires and an addition does not, the enquiry that finds a name in one direction and nothing
in the other, and the control that outlives the system it protected because compliance is cheaper than a
signature, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a bloated bureaucracy or a timid
engineer.

The governing law is that additions to a procedure are distributed and unattributed while removals are singular
and attributed, so the two directions differ in kind rather than in degree and the document has no reverse gear.

**Nobody in the sequence falls short, and that is what puts the failure outside anybody's conduct.** The incident
was real. The review was competent. The step would have caught it. The person who declines to propose a removal
has read their exposure correctly. The enquiry that asks who took it out is asking a proper question.

**And every addition is right, which the play establishes before anything else.** Something went wrong; a review
was held; it found a step that addresses what happened; the step costs an afternoon a month. **There is no line
in the document that is wrong.** Fifteen years of that is ninety pages of entirely justified procedure with no
bad decision anywhere in the sequence, which is what keeps this from resolving into a complaint about red tape.

**Then the asymmetry, which is structural before it is psychological.** An addition arrives as one more line,
small enough that nobody sets it against the total, and the total is therefore never on an agenda: it accrues
rather than being adopted, so **nobody signs for the sum because the sum was never proposed.** A removal cannot
accrue. It happens at one meeting, it has an owner, and it enters the record with a name and a date against it.
**One direction is a decision and the other is a drift.**

**Then the measured half, which is why knowing about it does not dissolve it.** People asked to judge identical
outcomes, one reached by acting and one by not acting, do not judge them alike: harm that follows doing is
condemned more than the same harm that follows not doing. So the person contemplating a removal is not being
cowardly and is not misjudging the risk. **The exposure is real and it is asymmetric**, and a bias that is true
about how one will actually be treated cannot be argued away by naming it to the person who would bear it.

**And the enquiry supplies the asymmetry without intending to.** After a harm, a proper investigation asks who
decided. Where a control was removed there is a record: a name, a date, a form, somebody to ask. Where a control
was merely never added there is nothing to find, because a non-addition leaves no record. **The same enquiry,
conducted identically and in good faith, reaches a person in one case and an absence in the other**, and that
difference is a property of what records contain rather than a flaw in how the question is put.

**Then the obsolete control, which is the case that removes every remaining excuse.** The system a step protects
was decommissioned four years ago and the step is still performed, by people who know that. There is no
uncertainty here about whether it is needed and no argument being won: nobody would defend it on the merits and
nobody is asked to. **The cheapest thing anybody can do with an obsolete control is comply with it**, since
compliance costs an afternoon a month and no signature, so the pointless work is the dominant move for every
individual who could stop it.

**And every settlement relocates the signature rather than abolishing it.** Sunset clauses work, and somebody must
decline to renew, which is a signed act in different clothes. A standing body to prune works, and its members will
be asked the same question about the same removals. Requiring each addition to name a removal genuinely works and
is the strongest of them, and it buys its effect by making addition harder **at the exact moment somebody has just
been hurt** and everybody in the room wants the control. **No move makes taking a control out cost the same as
leaving it in**, because the difference does not live in the procedure: it lives in the fact that a removal
produces somebody who can be named afterwards and a non-removal produces nobody.

It is distinct from the house's **Chesterton's Fence**, which is the nearest incumbent and stages the removal of a
safeguard whose purpose has been forgotten, where the reformer cannot know what the fence was for. Remove that
engine entirely, by stipulating perfect and complete knowledge of why every control exists, fully documented,
with the originating incident on file and understood by everybody, and this misfit is unchanged, because
**knowing exactly why a control was added does not make removing it signable.** That play turns on ignorance of
the reason; this one assumes the reason is known, and often known to be spent, and the control stays anyway.

It is distinct from the house's **The Last Layer**, which stages diminishing returns on complexity, where each
increment of elaboration buys less than the one before until the whole becomes unaffordable. Remove that engine,
by stipulating no diminishing returns at all, every practice fully earning its cost, and stipulating instead that
some controls become **obsolete** when what they protected is decommissioned: this stands entire, because the
obsolete control still cannot be removed. That play needs the aggregate to cost more than it returns; this one
needs only that a single spent control is unremovable, which is true at any level of total burden.

It is distinct from the house's **Built Before the Rule**, which stages grandfathering, where a prospective
standard binds new stock and the existing stock never has to comply. Remove that engine, by stipulating that every
rule applies to everything immediately with no exemption for what already exists, and this stands, because
**it runs the other way.** There the old stock escapes a new rule; here the old rule outlives its stock.

It is distinct from the house's **Sandbag**, which stages the ratchet effect, where good performance raises the
target so capacity is hidden. Remove that engine, by stipulating no targets anywhere and nothing measured against
anybody, and this is untouched, because **nothing here is being concealed and no performance is being rationed.**
That ratchet is on what a worker admits to being able to do; this one is on what a procedure contains, and it
turns even where every participant is entirely candid.

It runs against the house's **Nobody Sues for Taking It Down**, on this axis and at the opposite end, and the
two are the same dial read from its two harmful sides. There a host answers a takedown notice about material
it holds: leaving the material up can cost it the statutory protection its whole activity depends on, and
removing it is immunised in terms, so the removal is the free direction and everything complained of comes
down. Here the removal is the attributed direction and the non-removal leaves no record, so nobody signs for
taking a control out and the procedure accretes. **What sets the sign is where the cost of the cheap error
lands.** In this play the decider bears both errors and only one of them can be pinned on them, so the
attributable direction is the one avoided and what accumulates is their own unnecessary procedure. There the
cheap error's cost falls entirely outside the transaction, on a speaker who is neither the decider nor the
complainant, so the cheap direction is taken freely and what accumulates is other people's lawful material.
Same asymmetry, opposite occupant of the loss, opposite behaviour.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the review as
  bureaucratic, the process as bloat, or the person who declines a removal as timid, because nothing in the
  sequence has a decision to get wrong: the incident was real and costly, the review was competent and acting in
  good faith, the step it produced would genuinely have caught what happened and costs little on its own, the
  person contemplating a removal reads their exposure accurately, and the enquiry that asks who took it out is
  discharging a proper duty. The failure is authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is
  that additions are distributed and unattributed while removals are singular and attributed.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Documenting why every control
  exists changes nothing, since the difficulty is not ignorance of the reason. Auditing the procedure finds every
  line justified, because every line is. Sunset clauses require somebody to decline to renew, which is a signed
  act wearing different clothes. A pruning body's members face the same question about the same removals. Making
  each addition name a removal genuinely works, and buys its effect by adding friction at the moment somebody has
  just been hurt and everybody wants the control. And telling the person who would sign that omission bias exists
  does not change what will happen to them if they sign.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as
  the house's. The staging is the house's; the measurement of act against omission in judgement is Spranca, Minsk
  and Baron's and Ritov and Baron's, and the defined-process tradition the setting belongs to is Humphrey's and
  his successors'. The bounds are carried with them: **the omission-bias experiments are of individual judgement
  in described scenarios**, and applying them to what an organisation does with a procedure is the play's
  extension rather than the authors' finding; the structural half, that additions are distributed and removals are
  signed, is offered as a description of how such records work and not as a measured result; whether accretion is
  a problem at all in a regulated setting, where a control's existence may be a requirement independent of its
  present usefulness, is genuinely contested and the play adjudicates none of it; and the account is stated for
  procedures that grow by incident, which is not every procedure. **The play makes no claim about any
  organisation, any process or any framework, and gives no governance, quality or engineering advice of any
  kind**, only that a document which gains a line whenever something goes wrong, and loses one only when somebody
  puts a name to it, has a direction.
