# Play: The Rare Disease Test

## Estate

## Name

The Rare Disease Test.
A condition is rare, present in only a small fraction of the population, one in a thousand, one in ten thousand. A test for it is good, wrong only rarely in either direction, a low false positive rate and a low false negative rate, and by any ordinary standard a fine instrument. A person takes the test, in good faith, because it is a fine instrument and a positive result is exactly the kind of evidence a person should attend to.
The result comes back positive. And here the mind does what minds do with a specific piece of evidence: it asks how likely a result like this is if the person truly has the condition, and the test answers that clearly, very likely, so the result is read as near proof (Kahneman and Tversky). But that is the wrong question answered instead of the right one. The right question is not how likely the evidence given the hypothesis, but how likely the hypothesis given the evidence, and that depends on how rare the hypothesis was to begin with.
Because the condition is rare, even a small false positive rate applied to the vast healthy majority produces more false positives in absolute numbers than true positives drawn from the small affected minority, so among all the positive results, most are false. Physicians and students given a disease's known prevalence and a test's false positive rate mostly fail to compute the true probability of disease given a positive result, some estimating near certainty where the true probability sits under one in ten (Casscells, Schoenberger and Grayboys).
The mechanism is representativeness: people judge probability by how well a case fits a category and set aside how common the category was to begin with, so the base rate is not refused or hidden but crowded out by a more vivid and more available kind of evidence, the specific result in hand (Kahneman and Tversky; Bar-Hillel).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The test was built and is used in good faith, and it really does perform at the rate advertised. The one who reads the positive result and weighs it heavily is not careless: they are doing what representativeness makes natural, attending to the evidence most directly in front of them over an abstract number that sits further from the case at hand. No one hides the prevalence and no one refuses to use it; it is simply crowded out. The error is authored by no one: it is the ordinary weighing of representative evidence over an abstract prior, and the misfit is that a sound test read by a sound mind can still yield a confidently wrong diagnosis.
And here is the way out, and why it is not always at hand. The arithmetic that restores the prior, Bayes's rule applied to the prevalence and the false positive rate, gives the true probability and is not hard to state, but it asks the reasoner to hold two numbers in mind at once, the rarity of the condition and the accuracy of the test, and weigh them against a single vivid result that alone feels far more persuasive than either. The documented remedy, recasting the numbers as natural frequencies rather than probabilities, out of a thousand people rather than a percent, works because it changes what is vivid and not because it teaches the sum better, so the correction depends on how the problem happens to be posed (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage).
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The neglect is strongest when the base rate is presented as a pallid, non-causal statistic and weakens sharply under natural-frequency framing or a causal story for the base rate, so the house stages the neglect as a real and well-documented tendency under ordinary framing, and not a claim that people are incapable of using base rates at all. The mechanism and the name are Kahneman and Tversky's, the clinical demonstration is Casscells, Schoenberger and Grayboys's, the systematic account of when base rates are neglected is Bar-Hillel's, and the natural-frequency remedy and its bound are Gigerenzer and Hoffrage's.

## Arc

The bend is this: specific evidence about a hypothesis crowds out the prior probability of that hypothesis in ordinary probabilistic reasoning, so a positive result for a rare condition is read as far more conclusive than the arithmetic supports, a sound test and sound-seeming reasoning together yielding a confidently wrong conclusion.
It runs on a prior that is true and simply not looked at.
The population holds a rare condition at a low, real rate, one in a thousand or fewer.
And an accurate test is given, wrong only rarely, a fine instrument by any ordinary standard.
And the result comes back positive, specific evidence weighed by asking how likely it is given the disease (Kahneman and Tversky).
And the prior is crowded out. The base rate abstract and pallid next to the vivid result in hand (Bar-Hillel).
And the confidence inflates. Read as near certain when the true probability may sit under one in ten (Casscells, Schoenberger and Grayboys).
And the numbers are restored. The prior brought back by the arithmetic, easiest cast as a count of a thousand rather than a percent, and the bound held (Gigerenzer and Hoffrage).

## Stakes

Whether a sound test read by a sound mind can be trusted, when the very evidence that makes a result feel conclusive is the evidence that crowds out the rate that would have kept it in proportion.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the inflated confidence and away from the true probability, because the population holds its rare condition at a real low rate, the test performs exactly as advertised, and the positive result is weighed by the natural question of how well it fits the disease, which crowds out the prior every time it is asked instead of the other one.
