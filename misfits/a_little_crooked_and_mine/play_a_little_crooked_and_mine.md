# Play: A Little Crooked, and Mine

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

A Little Crooked, and Mine.
A person undertakes to build or assemble something themselves, a piece of furniture, a folded paper crane, a set of building blocks, and invests real effort in the task, honestly engaged and not performing the labor for show. The building itself is the mechanism: the labor generates a felt sense that the finished object is meaningfully one's own, produced by one's own hands and not merely acquired, and this felt ownership inflates the object's value in the builder's own eyes independent of the object's objective quality, so that a non-expert's imperfect, even crooked, creation is regarded by its maker as comparable in worth to an expert's polished one (Norton, Mochon and Ariely, "The IKEA Effect: When Labor Leads to Love", 2012).
The inflation is not a claim about taste or a failure of judgment. The builder is not wrong that the object means more to them; they are only mistaken in expecting that meaning to be shared by anyone who did not build it. When the object meets an outside eye, a buyer, a stranger, an appraiser with no stake in the labor that made it, the plain judgment lands far below the builder's own, and the trade both sides would gain from, the builder wanting to sell and a buyer willing to pay a fair market price, does not happen, since the builder's honestly felt price sits above what any buyer, feeling none of the labor, will pay.
The mechanism is bounded in a way that sharpens what it is. An abandoned build, begun but never finished, shows none of the inflation, so the effect is not the mere passage of time spent on a task or simple exposure to it, but requires a completed, successfully assembled object the builder can call finished and their own. And the effect is not uniform: it varies with task difficulty and is roughly proportional to the labor felt as meaningfully one's own, stronger where the building demanded more of the builder and weaker where it demanded little (Norton, Mochon and Ariely, 2012).
No one is a villain here. The builder's valuation is sincerely felt, generated by real labor and not manufactured for advantage, and the appraiser's plain judgment is not coldness but an honest read of an object it did not build. No one inflates a price to deceive and no one lowballs out of spite; the impasse is authored by no one, the predictable cost of a value that only one side's labor produced.
And the bound is held. This is a documented effect that requires completion and tracks task difficulty, and not a claim that all effort breeds attachment regardless of outcome, since the case, the mechanism, and the boundary belong to Norton, Mochon and Ariely, and the deeper theoretical grounding for why labor and not mere time carries the inflation belongs to the wider psychological-ownership and effort-justification literature that followed them.

## Arc

The bend is this: labor invested in building something generates a felt ownership that inflates the builder's own valuation of the finished result, independent of its objective quality, so a trade both sides would gain from can go unmade because only one side felt the labor that set the price.
It runs on a value only one side can feel.
A build is undertaken, and real labor is spent on it, honestly, for its own sake.
And the labor becomes ownership. The finished object felt as meaningfully one's own because of what it cost to make.
And an outside verdict is heard. The same object judged plainly, by weight of its own quality alone.
And an impasse follows. The two valuations too far apart for the trade either side would gain from.
And an unfinished build is met. Showing that the effect needs completion, not merely time spent.
And the true proportion is held. Tracking task difficulty, and not a flat, universal inflation.

## Stakes

Whether a thing made with one's own hands can ever be fairly traded to someone who did not make it, when the very labor that gives the object its value to the builder is invisible to anyone who did not spend it.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the impasse and away from the trade, because the builder's valuation is sincerely earned by real labor and the appraiser's plain read is an honest account of an object it did not build. The structure cannot both let the labor generate real, felt ownership and guarantee that ownership will be shared by a buyer, because the meaning is the mismatch: a value built from effort felt as one's own is, by the same fact, a value no outside eye spent that effort to feel. The boundary offered, that an unfinished build shows none of the extra inflation, is not a loophole a builder can use to keep their own valuation modest, since the whole point of building is to finish, and finishing is exactly what generates the felt ownership that will not meet the market. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the builder's pride is sincere and the appraiser's judgment is not coldness, and that the impasse is authored by no one, the predictable cost of a value only one side's labor produced. And it holds the bound in view, hedged, because this is a documented effect that requires completion and tracks task difficulty, and not a claim that all effort breeds attachment regardless of outcome (Norton, Mochon and Ariely, 2012; the psychological-ownership and effort-justification literature that followed), so the whole of the misfit is that a thing can be worth everything to the one who made it and still be worth, to everyone else, only what it looks like it is worth.
