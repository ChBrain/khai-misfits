# Play: It Learns When You Stop

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

It Learns When You Stop.
A rider asks a horse to slow, and closes the hand. The horse does not slow. The rider closes the hand more.
Everything about that is reasonable. It is what a person does when a thing does not happen: they ask again, and harder.
And it is the one move that guarantees the horse will never learn the answer, because what teaches the horse is not the asking. It is the stopping.

## Arc

The bend is this: the pressure motivates and the release instructs, so the harder a rider asks the more reliably they hold through the horse's attempt, and holding through the attempt punishes it.
It runs on two facts, neither of them anybody's doing: in pressure-release training the reinforcing event is the removal of the pressure and not its application, and a horse's first offer is small enough to be missed.
Take the asking first, because the play has to establish that it is **right**. The rein and the leg are signals. They tell the horse what is wanted and they supply the motivation to answer, and there is nothing wrong with either. A rider who never asks teaches nothing at all.
Then the fact that inverts it. **What the horse learns from is the release.** The pressure says ask; the removal of the pressure says yes. So the whole instructional content of the exchange lives in the moment the rider stops, and everything before that moment is only the question.
Then the hinge, which is what makes this a trap and not a lesson in timing. When the horse does not answer, the reasonable move is to ask more firmly. Asking more firmly means holding. And the horse, somewhere inside that holding, makes a small offer, a shift of weight, a fractional softening, the beginning of the thing that was asked for. If the pressure is still there when the offer comes, then what followed the offer was pressure. **The try was punished**, and the horse makes fewer of them.
And there is no line the rider can feel where the one becomes the other. The literature describes it as a continuum running from poorly timed negative reinforcement into punishment, and the far end of that continuum is a horse that has stopped offering anything at all.
Then the conclusion the rider reaches, which fits every observation and is wrong. The horse ignores the aids, resists more the firmer the ask, and does nothing when asked politely. **Stubborn** explains all of it. It is the available explanation, it is consistent with the evidence, and it is what a reasonable person concludes.
Reach for a settlement and there is one, exactly one, and it cannot be hurried. Release at the try: not at the movement, at the **try**, which is the shift before the movement. That works, and it is measured, and it requires feeling something that has not happened yet, and giving up the pressure at the moment the thing asked for is still absent. **The rider has to stop asking precisely when they most want to keep asking**, and learning to feel the moment takes years that no amount of wanting shortens.

## Stakes

Whether a person can teach an animal a thing they must stop doing in order to teach.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether the rider is cruel, because they are trying and the production refuses to cast them otherwise, and not whether the horse is difficult, because it is answering exactly what it was taught, but whether the instruction can survive the instinct that fires when the answer does not come. The production cannot make it survive. It can only show that the ask is a legitimate signal, that the release is the whole of the teaching, that firmness holds through the try and thereby punishes it, that no felt boundary separates a firm correction from a punishment, that stubborn is the explanation that fits and is not the explanation, and that the one thing which works is releasing at a shift the rider must learn for years to feel.
