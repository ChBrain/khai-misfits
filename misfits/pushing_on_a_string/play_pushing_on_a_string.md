# Play: Pushing on a String

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Pushing on a String.
An economy falls into a deep slump, and the central bank reaches for the tool it always reaches for: it cuts the interest rate and injects money, which normally lowers the cost of borrowing, draws out investment and spending, and lifts the economy back. But the rate can only fall so far. It has a floor near zero, because no one will hold a bond that pays less than the cash they could hold instead, so once the rate reaches that floor it can fall no further. And there, at the floor, something changes in the nature of money. A bond that pays nothing and cash that pays nothing are the same thing, near-perfect substitutes, so when the bank injects more money it is not passed on into loans and spending but simply held, absorbed into idle balances, and all the more so in a slump where hoarding feels safe and prices may even be falling. John Maynard Keynes named the condition: at some low rate, liquidity preference may become absolute, the demand for money infinitely elastic, so no amount of new money will push the rate down or the spending up.
And here is the cruelty of it. The lever fails exactly when it is most needed, in the deep slump that drove the rate to the floor in the first place, so the tool that can always pull an overheating economy back by raising the rate cannot push a frozen one forward by lowering it. It is pushing on a string: pull, and the economy comes; push, and the string goes slack and the economy does not move.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The bank is not timid or failing in its duty; it is wielding a tool that has no grip at the floor, and no boldness will make a rate that has reached its floor fall further. The people who hold the injected money rather than lend or spend it are not hoarders in any blameworthy sense; they are doing the sensible thing, since a bond pays no more than the cash in their hands and the cold future makes the cash worth keeping. The loss is authored by no one. It is the slump the usual tool cannot lift, worst exactly where the tool is weakest, and the misfit is that the instrument which can always cool an overheating economy cannot warm a frozen one.
And here is the way out, and why it is by another hand. The rate cut is spent, so the escape must come from elsewhere and at a real cost. The bank can try to commit, believably, to higher inflation in the future, so that expected inflation lowers the real rate even when the nominal one cannot fall, but a bank built to be credibly conservative finds it hard to promise convincingly to be loose (Krugman). Or the government can turn to fiscal policy, spending directly to fill the demand the rate cut cannot draw out, which spends real money and political capital. Or the bank can reach for unconventional tools whose power is itself debated. Each escape works against the grain of the very credibility or thrift the ordinary regime prizes, and none is the simple turn of the familiar dial.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The trap is real in the record, in Japan's long slump and the years after the great financial crisis, but it is a contested regime and not a hard wall or a permanent state. Its remedies are debated in their power, the effective floor is slightly below zero and not exactly at it, and some economists hold that a determined central bank is never truly out of ammunition, since it can create inflation by other means, so that the trap is a failure of credibility rather than an absolute limit (Bernanke). The concept and absolute liquidity preference are Keynes's, the flat-segment formalisation Hicks's, the modern revival and the expectations reframing Krugman's, and the counter that a determined bank always has tools Bernanke's and the monetarists'. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that at the floor the usual lever loses its grip, and a bank can no more push a frozen economy up than pull on a slack string.

## Arc

The bend is this: when the interest rate has fallen to its floor near zero, money and bonds become near-perfect substitutes, so the money a central bank injects to lift a slump is willingly held rather than spent or lent, and the usual lever of rate cuts loses its grip exactly when the slump most needs it, the tool that can pull an economy back unable to push it forward.
It runs on a lever that meets a floor.
An economy falls into a deep slump, and the usual cure is to cut the rate and inject money to draw out spending.
And the rate has a floor. It cannot fall much below zero, because no one holds a bond that pays less than the cash they could hold instead (Keynes; Hicks).
And at the floor money is not passed on. A bond paying nothing and cash paying nothing are the same, so the injected money is willingly held, absorbed into idle balances (Keynes).
And the lever loses its grip. The tool that can pull an overheating economy back by raising the rate cannot push a frozen one forward by lowering it, pushing on a string.
And it is authored by no one. The bank wields a tool with no purchase at the floor and the holders of cash are only sensible, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is by another hand. Credible future inflation, or fiscal action, or unconventional policy, each reaching where the rate cut cannot, and each costly and against the grain (Krugman).
And the whole is held against its limit. The trap is a contested regime, its remedies debated, and a determined bank may always have other means (Bernanke).
