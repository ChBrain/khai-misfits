# Play: Exactly How Far

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Exactly How Far.
A guarantee is not a switch. It is a setting, and somebody has to choose where to leave it.
The ally wants it tight. That is not greed. It is a state whose continued existence depends on whether someone else turns up, and a government that did not press for the answer would be failing at the only job that finally matters. So it asks the reasonable question, which is exactly how far: what territory, which contingencies, whose forces, on what timetable, and what happens on the morning the shooting starts.
And the patron will not say. That is not cowardice. It is a state declining to hand the decision of when it goes to war to a smaller partner with a border dispute it did not choose and cannot control, and a government that signed that away would be reckless with the lives it is holding.
Both of them are right, and there is one dial (Glenn Snyder).
And here is what happens at the tight end. To reassure the ally you must be specific, because a promise that does not name anything reassures nobody. So you name it. And the document that says exactly what you will defend is, in the same words, the document that says what you will not, and it is public, and the adversary reads it (Schelling). You have bought the ally's confidence by printing a map of the ground where nothing will meet them.
And here is what happens at the loose end. To keep your choice you stay vague. And the choice you keep is precisely the thing the deterrent consisted of, because a deterrent is not a capability, it is a belief in somebody else's head about what you will do. Vague, you have your freedom back, and it is worth less than what you spent on it.
And the ally is not passive while you decide. A state that doubts its guarantee does something about it: it arms, it builds its own deterrent, it moves early while moving is still possible, or it quietly opens a line to the other side. Every one of those is prudent from where it is standing, and every one of them makes your position worse.
And you cannot hedge privately, because the others are watching. A patron's conduct toward one partner is the only evidence the rest of them have about their own guarantee, so a doubt raised in one capital is priced in all of them, and the alliance is chained together or it comes apart in pieces (Christensen and Jack Snyder).
And nobody in it is doing anything wrong. Not the ally, not the patron, not the adversary reading the treaty, and not the partners drawing conclusions from the only evidence available to them.
And so the misfit is that the cure for each horn is the other horn, and that the crisis, when it comes, reads whatever setting happens to be standing that morning and does not permit an adjustment. The alliance security dilemma and the inverse relation of abandonment and entrapment are Glenn Snyder's; chain-ganging and buck-passing are Thomas J. Christensen and Jack Snyder's; the credibility mechanics are Thomas Schelling's; the parent dilemma between adversaries is Robert Jervis's and John Herz's and is cited here only to mark the line. The house stages the dial, the published map, the restored choice, the hedge, the watchers and the bound, and holds the bound in view: that the trap needs an ally whose security genuinely rests on the guarantee, and that entrapment is in practice far rarer than the theory predicts, because great powers routinely evade what they signed (Beckley), so the whole of it is that you are asked to say exactly how far you will go, and there is no answer to that question that does not cost you something you cannot get back.

## Arc

The bend is this: where one state's protection of another is set by a single dial of commitment, firmness reassures the ally and publishes to the adversary the map of the undefended ground, while vagueness restores the guarantor's choice and dissolves the certainty the deterrent consisted of, so each horn's cure is the other horn and no setting answers both.
It runs on a question that has to be asked.
The ally asks exactly how far, because its survival depends on the answer, and a government that did not ask would be failing at the only task that matters.
And a promise that reassures must be specific. Nothing vague comforts anybody, so the guarantee names the territory, the contingency and the forces (Glenn Snyder).
And the same words print the map. What is named as covered names what is not, the document is public, and the adversary reads it for the gap (Schelling).
And the other end of the dial is no better. Vagueness returns the guarantor's freedom to choose, and the freedom it returns is the certainty the deterrent was made of.
And the doubting ally acts. It arms, seeks its own deterrent, moves while it still can, or opens a line to the other side, each prudent from where it stands and each worsening the guarantor's position.
And no hedge is private. A patron's conduct toward one partner is the only evidence the others have about their own guarantee, so one capital's doubt is priced in all of them (Christensen and Jack Snyder).
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs an ally whose security truly rests on the promise, and entrapment is in practice far rarer than the theory predicts, since great powers evade what they signed (Beckley).

## Stakes

Whether a promise can be exact enough to be believed and vague enough to remain yours.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward a step that is correct on its own terms, which each is, and toward a guarantee that is worth less whichever way it is set, because one dial is being read by three audiences who need opposite things from it. The production cannot let a careful reader catch it in the usual way, since nobody is faithless and nobody is grasping: the ally that presses for specifics is doing what a state whose survival hangs on the answer must do, the patron that will not sign away its choice of war is doing what a state holding those lives must do, the adversary reading a public treaty for its omissions is doing the work adversaries exist to do, and the partners inferring their own worth from how another is treated are reasoning correctly from the only evidence they have. It can only show that the same act answers all three readers and answers them in opposite directions, that precision is reassurance and a published map in one breath, that ambiguity is freedom and the dissolution of the deterrent in one silence, and that integration, which is the standard remedy where two parties fear each other's opportunism, deepens the entrapment horn instead of curing it. And it holds the bound in view, because the trap is conditional and not universal: it needs an ally whose security genuinely rests on the guarantee and an adversary reading it, its entrapment horn is in practice far weaker than the theory predicts since great powers routinely evade what they signed, and its severity is disputed rather than settled (Glenn Snyder; Christensen and Jack Snyder; Beckley), so the whole of the misfit is that you are asked to say exactly how far you will go, and there is no answer to that question that does not cost you something you cannot get back.
