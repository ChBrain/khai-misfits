# Play: Built Before the Rule

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Built Before the Rule.
Building codes are written after things fall down. Each disaster produces a finding, the finding produces a requirement, and the requirement goes into the code for everything built from then on. That is how the codes got good, and it is the only way they could have got good, because nobody knew what to require until something demonstrated it. And then the requirement applies forward, because applying it backward would condemn the entire existing stock at once, which no city can afford and no legislature will do. So the buildings that were put up before anyone knew are left standing, lawfully, exactly as they are.

## Arc

The bend is this: the exemption is granted by age, and age is also what predicts the hazard, so the two select the same buildings.
It runs on a rule that could only ever have been written afterwards.
The Code That Came After is not slow or captured. Nobody could have required reinforcement before the earthquake that showed why, and each revision is a real improvement bought with a real disaster. Prospective application is not a concession to industry either: it is the only version that does not condemn every older structure in the jurisdiction on the day of enactment.
The Building That Predates It is therefore exempt, and lawfully so. An unreinforced masonry building, brick, typically put up before 1945, with no steel tying the walls to the floors: known since the 1930s to be among the most lethal structures in an earthquake, and legal, because it complied with everything that existed when it was built.
The One Who Owns It meets the trigger, which is the part that turns. Exemption survives only while the building is left alone. Do enough work and the whole structure must come up to current standard: under the national flood programme the line is work costing fifty per cent or more of the building's market value before the damage, and communities may set it lower, at thirty or forty.
So The Estimate decides everything, and the safest move is not to build. Repair at forty-nine per cent and the building stays exempt. Cross the line and the owner must elevate or reinforce the entire structure to the standard of a new building. The result is documented and is the opposite of the intention: owners keep the work under the threshold, and buildings decline or sit empty rather than be improved. A rule written to bring buildings up is the reason nobody touches them.
And the denominator does its own sorting. The threshold is a percentage of market value, so the cheapest buildings reach it with the least work: the same repair that is comfortably under the line on a valuable property trips it on a poor one, which means the owners with the least capacity to fund full compliance are the ones most often required to, and therefore the ones most often deterred from repairing at all.
And the corrections each charge somebody. Mandatory retrofit ordinances work, and they cost the owner a sum that often exceeds the building's value, run on compliance clocks of seven to thirteen years by vulnerability class, and displace the tenants they were written to protect. Many jurisdictions never adopt one. Lower the threshold and more buildings are caught and more repairs deterred. Raise it and more damage accumulates uncorrected. Demolish and rebuild and the standard is met by removing the cheapest housing in the city.

## Triggers

**[Written After the Fire](plot_written_after_the_fire.md)**
A disaster produces a finding, the finding produces a requirement, and the requirement enters the code for everything built from then on. Closes when the improvement is real, bought with a real catastrophe, and could not have been written any earlier by anybody.

**[It Was Legal When It Was Built](plot_it_was_legal_when_it_was_built.md)**
Cue: the new requirement applying forward, because applying it backward would condemn the whole existing stock on the day of enactment. Closes when the exemption has been granted by age, and age is also what predicts the hazard, so the two pick out the same buildings.

**[Forty-Nine Per Cent](plot_forty_nine_per_cent.md)**
Cue: a contractor's estimate, and a threshold at half the building's market value. Closes when the work has been specified to land just under the line, lawfully, on advice, with the exemption intact and the building unchanged.

**[So Nobody Touches It](plot_so_nobody_touches_it.md)**
Cue: the arithmetic understood by every owner in the district. Closes when improvement has become the thing that costs most, buildings decline or stand empty rather than be repaired, and the rule written to bring them up is the reason nobody goes near them.

**[A Percentage of What It's Worth](plot_a_percentage_of_what_its_worth.md)**
Cue: the same repair, priced against two different market values. Closes when the cheapest building has tripped the threshold on work the valuable one absorbed without notice, and the owner least able to fund compliance is the one required to fund it.

**[Seven Years to Comply](plot_seven_years_to_comply.md)**
Cue: a city that finally orders the retrofit, with clocks of seven to thirteen years by vulnerability class. Closes when the work costs more than the building is worth, the tenants it was written to protect are displaced by it, and the jurisdictions that never adopt it have paid nothing at all.

## Stakes

Whether a standard can be called protective when the rule granting relief from it and the fact predicting the danger are the same fact.
Every plot moves the same way: toward codes that improve, exemptions that are fair, thresholds applied evenly and officials with no discretion to bend them, and away from any instrument that could reach the stock that needs reaching, because reaching it means condemning what people are living in. The production cannot write a requirement before the disaster that teaches it, and it cannot ask a city to close every old building on a Tuesday. What is left to fight over is not whether codes should improve, they must, but who pays to bring a lawful building up to a standard invented after it, and whether a threshold that makes repair the expensive option was ever going to produce a repair.
