# Play: Whoever Is Not Involved

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Whoever Is Not Involved.
A board that approves changes before they are made, so that nothing goes into a live service unexamined. It meets on a schedule, it has an agenda, and it is deliberately made up of people with no stake in the work in front of it.
That last part is the point of it. A reviewer with a stake is not a reviewer.
It is also the reason nobody in the room has ever run the system being changed.

## Arc

The bend is this: the board is constituted to be independent of the work, independence is what qualifies it to judge, and independence is exactly what removes the knowledge the judgement requires.
It runs on two facts, neither of them anybody's doing: a reviewer with a stake is not a reviewer, and the risk in a change lives in the particulars of a system.
Take the intention first, because the play has to establish that it is **right**. Putting a change into a live service without any review is indefensible, and everybody knows it. So a body is created, and it is made independent, because a reviewer who benefits from approval is not performing a review. Every member is competent, every question on the agenda is reasonable, and the whole arrangement is what any responsible organisation would build.
Then the substitution, which nobody chooses and everybody performs. The board cannot assess whether this change will break this system, because that requires knowing the system, and knowing the system is what it was constituted not to do. So it assesses the things it can see: whether the form is complete, whether a rollback is described, whether the window is agreed, whether the right names are on it. **Those are real questions and they are not the question**, and asking them well is indistinguishable, from inside the room, from doing the job.
Then the measurement, which settles it. Four years of survey data across the industry find that approval by a body external to the team has **no correlation with the rate at which changes fail**. Not a weak one: none. And it correlates **negatively** with how long a change takes to reach production, with how often anything is released at all, and with **how quickly service is restored when something breaks**.
And that last one is the turn. The gate is not only failing to stop the bad change: it is standing in front of the fix. When the outage arrives, the remedy is a change, and a change goes to the board. **The control that did not prevent the failure is now delaying the recovery from it**, and the delay is longest exactly when it costs most.
Reach for a settlement and there is one, and it is the same review by different people. Have the change read by a peer who knows the system, which the same research finds does work. What that costs is the independence: the reviewer now has a stake, and an organisation that has been told for twenty years that a reviewer with a stake is not a reviewer has to give that sentence up. **The thing that has to be surrendered is not the review but the principle it was built on.**

## Stakes

Whether a body can judge a risk it is disqualified from understanding.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether review is needed, because putting an unexamined change into a live service is indefensible and the production never argues for it, and not whether the board is competent or diligent, because it is both, but whether the property that makes a reviewer trustworthy can coexist with the property that makes a reviewer able. The production cannot reconcile them. It can only show that independence is what the board is for, that it is also what removes the knowledge, that the questions left available are real and are not the question, that the measurement finds no relationship at all with the thing the board exists to prevent, that the same gate delays the recovery it failed to avert, and that the remedy is to hand the change to somebody with a stake, which works and costs the principle.
