# Play: Loath to Part

## Name

Loath to Part.
Hand a person a coffee mug, one of a stack, chosen for them at random, and ask a little later what they would take to give it back. Then take a matched person who was given no mug and ask what they would pay to buy one. The owner, on average, demands about twice what the buyer will offer, for the very same mug (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler). Nothing about the mug has changed in the minutes since it was handed over. What changed is only that it is now theirs, and that alone has lifted its worth. So when the owners and the buyers are put in a room to trade, far fewer mugs change hands than they should: many an owner who values the mug below what some buyer would pay still will not sell, because to sell now feels like giving something up, and giving something up weighs more than getting it would.
And here is the mechanism beneath the mug. Value is not measured in the abstract but from a reference point, the place you now stand, and owning the mug moves that point to include it. From there, keeping the mug is the baseline and parting with it is a loss, while for the buyer, who does not own it, acquiring it is only a gain. And a loss of a given size looms larger than a gain of the same size, this is loss aversion, the heart of prospect theory (Tversky and Kahneman; Thaler). So the owner requires more to surrender the mug than the buyer will offer to obtain it, not because their tastes differ, they are drawn from the same pool, but because one of them owns it and the other does not.
And here is what the gap does, and why it is a trap and not a haggle. Between the price the owner will accept and the price the buyer will pay lies a wedge, and when a buyer values the mug more than the owner does but by less than that wedge, the sale that should happen does not. The good stays with its owner rather than moving to the person who wants it most. And this breaks a load-bearing idea in economics: the Coase theorem says that, trade being free, the final allocation of a good does not depend on who first holds it, because whoever values it most will end up with it whichever way it started. But if ownership itself changes what the good is worth to its holder, then who is handed it first is who tends to keep it, and the theorem fails (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler). The arbitrary first assignment becomes the final one.
And here is the cost, and why it hides. Nothing is taken and no one is cheated: the owner names an honest price, the buyer names an honest price, and the prices simply do not meet. So the loss is authored by no one. It is the mutually beneficial trade left undone, the surplus that both would have shared, never realised, and it never appears anywhere because the thing that would have shown it, the sale, did not happen. Goods come to rest not where they are valued most but where they happened to land, and the misallocation is invisible because it is a gain that was never made.
And here is the bound, the one that keeps the whole thing honest. The effect is not a law of all ownership. Bring in a seasoned trader, someone who handles goods as stock and not as keepsakes, and the gap shrinks or vanishes: to a dealer a mug is for exchange, not for keeping, so parting with it is no loss and the reference point never grips (List). Market experience wears the effect away, and part of the measured gap has been argued to be an artifact of how the questions are put (Plott and Zeiler). But the seasoning that dissolves it is exactly what the ordinary owner of the things they most mean to keep, a home, an heirloom, a first car, never has, and for them the effect is real and the reference point re-forms around whatever they hold.
And so the misfit is not that people are foolish to love what they own but that the loving inflates its price beyond what anyone else will pay, so the thing does not move. The endowment effect and its grounding in loss aversion are Thaler's, the mug experiments and the failure of the Coase theorem are Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's, the reference-dependence and loss aversion beneath it are Tversky and Kahneman's, and the bound, that experience and holding-for-exchange dissolve it, is List's. The house stages the inflation and holds the bound in view: that ownership raises the price to part above the price to acquire, so trades both sides would gain from go unmade and goods stick where they first fell, real for the ordinary owner and fading for the practised hand.

## Arc

The bend is this: merely owning a good raises the price one demands to give it up above the price one would pay to acquire the same good, because giving up what one has is coded as a loss and losses loom larger than equivalent gains, so mutually beneficial trades go unmade and goods stick with their current owners rather than moving to those who would value them most.
It runs on an ordinary good, once it is owned.
A mug, a ticket, a house, a tool, unchanged in itself, but now belonging to someone, and worth more to them for that alone (Thaler).
And it turns on where value is measured from. Value is reckoned from a reference point, and ownership moves that point to include the good, so keeping it is the baseline and parting with it is a loss (Tversky and Kahneman).
And a loss outweighs an equal gain. Loss aversion means surrendering the good hurts more than acquiring it would please, so the owner demands more to part than a buyer will pay, about twice as much in the mug experiments (Thaler; Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler).
And the gap blocks the trade. Where a buyer values the good more than the owner but by less than that wedge, the sale that should happen does not, and the good stays where it sits (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler).
And the first assignment becomes the last. Because the good will not move, who is handed it first is who keeps it, so the Coase theorem's independence of initial allocation fails and the arbitrary start is the finish (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler).
And the loss is authored by no one. The owner and the buyer each name an honest price, the prices do not meet, and the foregone surplus is real and invisible, a gain that was never realised.
And the bound holds it in check, at a cost. Seasoned traders and goods held for exchange show little of the effect, so experience dissolves it (List), and part of the gap is contested (Plott and Zeiler), but the seasoning is exactly what the ordinary owner of things held to keep never has.

## Stakes

Whether a good can reach the person who values it most, when merely owning it makes its holder value it more.
Every plot but the last two moves the same way: toward a trade that would leave both sides better off, and away from making it, because ownership has lifted the price to part above the price to acquire. The production cannot make the owner's price meet the buyer's while the good is owned and held for keeping, because the value and the reluctance are the one reference point seen from two sides: the good is dearer to its holder precisely because it is theirs to lose. The forces that close the gap, market experience that seasons a trader, treating a good as stock and not a keepsake, framing that loosens the reference point, each ask exactly the detachment the ordinary owner of a cherished thing does not have, so they clear trades on a dealer's floor and leave the house and the heirloom stuck. It can only show that no one is greedy and no one is duped, that the owner who will not sell low and the buyer who will not pay high both name honest prices, that the trade left undone is a real loss no one can see, and that goods come to rest where they first fell rather than where they are wanted most. And it holds the bound in view, because the effect is not a law of all ownership but a grip that seasoning and holding-for-exchange can loosen (List), part of it contested (Plott and Zeiler), so the whole of the misfit is that what is ours is dearer to us than to anyone, and that dearness, real and quiet, is what keeps it from moving.
