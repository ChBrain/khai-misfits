# Loath to Part: Reference

The research warrant for **Loath to Part**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon the literature
calls the **endowment effect**: Thaler's finding that merely owning a thing raises what one demands
to give it up, Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's mug experiments measuring the gap between what owners
will accept and buyers will pay, and Tversky and Kahneman's loss aversion beneath it, all cited
below, held with the finding that the effect is robust for ordinary goods but attenuates with market
experience and for goods held for exchange, and that part of the measured gap is debated.

## Line of Work

Staging the endowment effect as an **ownership-inflates-value structure**: a person owns an ordinary
good, a mug, a ticket, a house, a tool, and is asked what they would take to give it up, and the sum
they demand, their willingness to accept, is reliably higher, often about twice as high, as what they
or a matched buyer would pay to acquire the same good, their willingness to pay. Nothing about the
good has changed; only that it is now theirs. The reason is loss aversion: once the good is owned it
becomes the reference point, the baseline from which the world is measured, so giving it up is coded
as a loss, and a loss looms larger than the equivalent gain of acquiring it, so the owner requires
more to surrender the good than a non-owner would offer for it (Thaler; Tversky and Kahneman). The
consequence is that mutually beneficial trades go unmade: where a buyer values the good more than the
seller once did, the sale that should happen does not, because ownership has lifted the seller's price
above the buyer's, and the good sticks where it sits rather than moving to whoever would value it most.
The Coase theorem, that the final allocation of a good is independent of who first holds it, fails,
because who holds it changes what it is worth to them (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler). The governing
law is not that anyone is greedy or irrational in any culpable way, but that **merely owning a good
raises the price one demands to give it up above the price one would pay to acquire the same good,
because giving up what one has is coded as a loss and losses loom larger than equivalent gains, so
mutually beneficial trades go unmade and goods stick with their current owners rather than moving to
those who would value them most**. The play models the documented mechanics: the endowment effect and
its grounding in loss aversion (Thaler), the willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay gap and the
undertrading it produces against the Coase prediction (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler; Knetsch), and
the reference-dependence and loss aversion that drive it (Tversky and Kahneman), as the load-bearing
vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a stubborn seller holding out for a better deal.

It is a valuation interaction no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from The Anticommons, where too many owners each hold a veto over one
resource so it sits idle: there the block is a coordination of many rights of exclusion, no single
owner able to deliver the whole, whereas here a single owner will trade and can deliver the whole, only
at a price that ownership itself has inflated, so the good does not sit wholly idle but fails to move to
a buyer who values it more. It is distinct from Over a Barrel, the hold-up over a sunk
relationship-specific investment, where a counterparty appropriates the quasi-rent on an asset worth
far more inside one relationship than out: there value comes from a specific, sunk investment and the
squeeze is another party's, whereas here nothing is specific or sunk and no counterparty squeezes, the
inflation is the bare fact of possession and it is the owner's own valuation that rises. It is distinct
from The Market for Lemons, where buyers cannot tell good from bad and so pay the average, driving the
good out: there the engine is asymmetric information about quality, whereas here quality is known and
symmetric, and the trade fails not because the buyer fears a hidden flaw but because ownership has
lifted the owner's price above what the good is worth to anyone else.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the
  owner who will not part with the good as greedy or the buyer who will not meet the price as
  miserly, because the source finding it stages is that the gap is not a bargaining posture but a
  genuine shift in felt value: once the good is owned, giving it up truly is experienced as a loss,
  and a loss weighs more than the matching gain, so the owner's higher price is what the good is now
  worth to them and not a ploy (Thaler; Tversky and Kahneman). The loss is authored by no one: it is
  the mutually beneficial trade that does not happen because ownership has lifted the seller's value
  above the buyer's, and the misfit is that goods come to rest with whoever first held them rather
  than with whoever would value them most.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The forces that
  shrink the gap exist, market experience that seasons a trader until the effect fades, treating a
  good as one held for exchange rather than for keeping, framing and practice that loosen the grip of
  the reference point (List), but none is free or general: seasoning takes real trading experience
  most owners of most goods never get, a beloved house or heirloom is precisely the good held for
  keeping and not for exchange, and the reference point re-forms around whatever one now holds. The
  reckoning is that the effect is bound but not banished, so the gap can be narrowed for practised
  traders and routine goods and remains for ordinary owners of the things they mean to keep.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here,
  never claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the endowment effect and its grounding in
  loss aversion are Thaler's, the willingness-to-accept and willingness-to-pay gap and the failure of
  the Coase theorem in the mug experiments are Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler's, and the
  reference-dependence and loss aversion beneath it are Tversky and Kahneman's. The mechanism is
  carried as carefully as the finding: that the effect requires an ordinary good held not for
  exchange and a reference point set by ownership, that the harm the play centers is the mutually
  beneficial trade left unmade and the allocation frozen to the first holder, and that the effect is
  robust for ordinary goods but attenuates with market experience and for goods held for exchange
  (List), with part of the measured gap contested as an artifact of the elicitation (Plott and
  Zeiler), so the play carries the effect and holds those bounds. The scope is held to ordinary goods
  held for keeping, and not stretched into a claim that all trade fails or that owners can never sell.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Holding**: carries the good once it is owned (Thaler), an ordinary thing, a mug, a ticket, a
  house, whose worth to its holder rises the moment it is theirs, not for anything changed in it but
  for the bare fact of possession.
- **The Reference Point**: carries the ownership baseline (Tversky and Kahneman), the status quo from
  which gains and losses are measured, so that owning the good makes keeping it the zero and losing it
  a loss rather than forgoing it a smaller gain.
- **The Loss**: carries the engine (Thaler; Tversky and Kahneman), the coding of giving up the owned
  good as a loss and the asymmetry by which losses loom larger than equal gains, the reason the price
  to surrender exceeds the price to acquire.
- **The Gap**: carries the observable (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler), the wedge between willingness
  to accept, the sum demanded to give the good up, and willingness to pay, the sum offered to get it,
  the measurable size of the endowment effect and the room in which trades fail.
- **The Trade Unmade**: carries the consequence (Kahneman, Knetsch, and Thaler), the mutually
  beneficial exchange that does not happen because ownership lifted the seller's price above the
  buyer's, the good stuck with its holder and the Coase theorem's allocation-independence broken.
- **The Exchange**: carries the boundary and the hedge (List; Plott and Zeiler), the market experience
  and the goods-held-for-trade that shrink or erase the gap, so the effect is real for ordinary owners
  and fades for seasoned traders and routine goods, bound and not universal.
