# Play: Only as Good as the Worst

## Name

Only as Good as the Worst.
On a cold morning in 1986 the space shuttle Challenger broke apart seventy-three seconds after launch, and the cause was one part: a rubber O-ring seal, stiffened by the cold, that failed to hold. Everything else on the shuttle, the millions of excellent parts and the thousands of hours of brilliant work, counted for nothing, because a single component did not do its task. Michael Kremer took this as the image for a theory of how production works and why some economies are rich and others poor. In much of production, he argued, the value of the output is not the sum of the qualities of its tasks but their product: a chain of steps each of which must be done well, so that a single one done badly cuts the value of the whole, however good the rest. The whole is only as good as its worst part (Kremer).
And here is what that multiplication does to who works with whom. If quality multiplies, then a highly skilled worker is worth far more on a chain of other highly skilled workers than on a chain with a weak link, because on the weak chain their excellence is multiplied by someone else's failure and comes to little. So a firm doing high-value work, work that needs every task done well, rationally takes only high-quality inputs and matches skill with skill, and lesser firms are left with lesser inputs. Like sorts with like, positive assortative matching, not out of snobbery but because that is how the total value is maximised when the tasks are complements (Kremer; Becker).
And here is the gap it opens. Because a small drop in any one task's quality cuts the whole product, small differences in skill produce large differences in outcome. Two workers who differ only a little in reliability end up in very different firms, on very different chains, earning very different wages and building things of very different worth, the small gap in skill multiplied and sorted into a vast gap in life. And the low-skill are not merely paid less: they are shut out of high-value production altogether, because a chain that needs every task done well cannot afford to place one in unreliable hands, so they are confined to low-value work where a weak link does less damage. It is a poverty trap, for a person and for a whole economy: high value needs long chains of complementary tasks all done well, and where the quality of even a few tasks cannot be assured, that value cannot be produced at all (Kremer).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The firm that will not hire the low-skill worker is not cruel: placing a task that could spoil the whole in unreliable hands would waste all the excellent work around it, so its refusal is sound. The skilled who keep to the skilled are not snobs: their quality is genuinely worth most among peers. And the low-skill worker is not at fault: they would gladly do the work, and are turned away only because the multiplicative structure makes their quality a risk to the whole. The loss is authored by no one. It is the high-value production the low-skill are shut out of and the human potential stranded where quality cannot be assured, and the misfit is that the very structure that makes skill so richly valuable in company with skill is what excludes those who lack it.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. A chain can bear high value only when its weakest task can, so the remedy is to raise the quality of the weakest tasks, everywhere they occur: education, training, institutions that make reliability the norm. Or to break a product into shorter chains, so fewer tasks can each ruin it, though shorter chains forgo the value only long complementary ones create. Or to add redundancy and inspection on the tasks most likely to fail, at a cost. But lifting the floor of quality across a whole chain, a whole workforce, a whole economy, is the entire difficulty of development, and cannot be done by wish, because until the weakest link can bear high value, none of the strong ones can be paid for their strength.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The whole result is as strong as production is multiplicative. Where tasks are substitutable, so a weak one can be routed around, or redundant, so a backup catches its failure, or merely additive, so a poor task subtracts only its own share, the weakest link no longer dominates and the sorting and stranding soften. The trap is real for complementary chains, and it is not a law of all production. The multiplicative production function, the weak link, the assortative matching, the amplified gaps, and the poverty trap are Kremer's; the positive assortative matching of complements is Becker's, the antecedent the theory extends. The house stages the multiplication and holds the bound in view: that where the whole is only as good as its worst part, the best gather with the best and the rest are shut out, no one at fault, and the way up is the long, costly work of lifting the worst.

## Arc

The bend is this: where production is a chain of complementary tasks whose qualities multiply, a single low-quality task sharply reduces the value of the whole, so high-quality inputs are matched with high-quality inputs and low with low, small differences in skill open large differences in reward, and low-quality inputs are stranded out of high-value production.
It runs on production that is a chain of complementary tasks.
High value is made only by many tasks each done well, the more of them the more fragile the value they combine to create (Kremer).
And the tasks multiply, they do not add. The value of the output is the product of the tasks' qualities, so a low quality anywhere cuts the whole in proportion, the whole only as good as its worst part, one weak link ruining it as one bad seal ruined the shuttle (Kremer).
And so quality sorts with quality. A skilled input is worth most beside others like it and little beside a weak link, so firms match high with high and low with low, positive assortative matching, the value-maximising response and not snobbery (Kremer; Becker).
And small gaps become large ones. Because a small drop in one task cuts the whole product, a small difference in skill is multiplied and sorted into a vast difference in wage and in the worth of what can be built (Kremer).
And the low are shut out. A chain that needs every task done well cannot place one in unreliable hands, so the low-skill are excluded from high-value production and confined to low-value work, a poverty trap for a person and an economy (Kremer).
And it is authored by no one. The firm's refusal is sound, the skilled's clustering is warranted, and the low-skill are at no fault, so the exclusion is a structure's doing and not a villain's.
And the way up is long. Lift the quality of the weakest tasks, shorten the chain, or add redundancy, each costly, the whole difficulty of development, because until the weakest link can bear high value, the strong cannot be paid for their strength.
And the whole is held against its limit. The result is as strong as production is multiplicative, and softens where tasks are substitutable, redundant, or additive, so the trap is real for complementary chains and not a law of all production (Kremer).
