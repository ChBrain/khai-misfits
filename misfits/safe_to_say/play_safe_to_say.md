# Play: Safe to Say

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Safe to Say.
A person holds a genuine view, a wish, a judgement, and in public they must decide not only what to do but what to profess. And where professing the true thing carries a social cost, a frown, a shunning, a lost job, a prison cell, they profess instead what is safe to say. The public stance is chosen not to express what they believe but to escape what honesty would cost them, and so the private truth is kept and a public lie is told. Timur Kuran called it preference falsification, and set down the plain calculus of it: a person weighs the reputational payoff of saying the accepted thing against the inner cost of betraying their own conviction, and where the pressure is strong enough the reputational payoff wins.
And here is why the concealment is rational. To say the safe thing spares you the sanction, here and now and for certain, while one dissenting voice would change nothing but its owner's fate. So the sums come out for silence, and they come out for silence again for the next person, and the next, each doing the sensible thing.
And here is the compounding harm. Each person, looking out, sees only the safe professions of everyone else, and so cannot tell how many privately agree with them. Each reads the surface of lies as the true feeling of the room, believes their own view more isolated than it is, and, believing themselves nearly alone, conceals the harder still, and may even enforce the accepted line on others to prove their own sincerity (Willer, Kuwabara and Macy). So an order that most privately reject is upheld, sincerely to all appearances, by the very people who reject it, and no one can see that the appearance is false, because the appearance is all anyone is allowed to show.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. Each concealer is only sparing themselves a real cost that honesty would carry, and no one intends to prop up the order they privately dislike. The loss is authored by no one. It is the order most reject, kept standing because each believes themselves nearly alone, and the misfit is that the very concealment each chooses to protect themselves is exactly what hides from them how many would stand with them if they only knew.
And here is the way it breaks, and why no one sees it coming. Because the true distribution of feeling stays hidden behind the wall of safe professions, the order is brittle in a way no one can measure. A small shock, a first bold voice, a crack in the pressure, can make it a little safer to speak, and that draws out the next voice and the next, each revelation lowering the cost of the one after it, until the concealment tips into revelation and the whole edifice flips at once. The revolutions of 1989 surprised a world that had read the public record and believed it, because the public record was the lie, and the truth it hid came out of never, all at once (Kuran).
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The concealment runs on the cost of honesty, so the way to let the truth surface without a rupture is to lower that cost: the secret ballot that lets a preference be cast unseen, the free-speech protection that dulls the sanction, the anonymity that lets the first truths out without their owners being marked. But it is neither cheap nor sure. Those who hold the order have every reason to raise the cost of honesty rather than lower it, protections can be revoked exactly where they are most needed, and where the pressure holds, the only other road to the truth is the cascade, which is sudden and uncontrollable and as able to install a new falsified order as to free anyone.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. Not every public conformity is a falsification: much public agreement is genuine, or genuinely persuaded, so one cannot read every conformist as a secret dissident. The cascade's timing is truly unpredictable, which is the theory's finding and not a forecast it delivers, so it explains the surprise after the fact more than it foresees it. And private preferences are, by their nature, hidden, so the account rests partly on inference. The name is Kuran's, the 1989 surprise Kuran's, the pluralistic ignorance beneath it Katz and Allport's, and the false enforcement that deepens it Willer, Kuwabara and Macy's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where the truth is unsafe to say, each says the safe thing, and a lie all share can uphold an order all reject, until, without warning, it cannot.

## Arc

The bend is this: where speaking one's true preference carries a social cost, each person publicly professes not what they believe but what is safe to say, so the public record diverges from the private truth and every concealer, seeing only the safe professions of others, believes their own view more isolated than it is and conceals the harder, so an order most privately reject is upheld by the very people who reject it, and because the true distribution stays hidden a small shock can tip concealment into revelation and the whole flip at once.
It runs on a truth kept and a lie professed.
A person holds a private preference, and finds that saying it in public would cost them (Kuran).
And so they profess the safe thing. Weighing the sanction against the inner cost of the lie, they say what is accepted and keep what they believe (Kuran).
And each reads the others' lies as truth. Seeing only safe professions, each believes their view more isolated than it is, and conceals the harder, some enforcing the line to prove their own sincerity (Katz and Allport; Willer, Kuwabara and Macy).
And the order stands on its own opponents. An order most privately reject is upheld, sincerely to all appearances, by the very people who reject it.
And it is authored by no one. Each only spares themselves a real cost, and no one means to prop up the order, so the loss is the structure's and not a fraud.
And it breaks without warning. The hidden truth means a small shock can make speaking safer, drawing out voice after voice, until concealment tips into a cascade that surprises everyone (Kuran).
And the whole is held against its limit. Not every conformity is a lie, the tipping is unpredictable, and hidden preferences are hard to test head-on.

## Stakes

Whether a people can know their own mind, when each is moved to profess the safe thing and none can see behind another's profession to the truth it hides.
Every plot but the last two moves the same way: toward the safe lie professed, and away from the private truth that stays unsaid, because honesty carries a cost each rationally declines to pay and the concealment of each hides from all how many would speak if it were safe. The structure cannot both spare each concealer the sanction and let the room see its own true mind, because the sparing IS the hiding: the safe profession and the false consensus are the one concealment seen twice, the shield each raises for itself and the blindfold it lays on everyone. The way out, to lower the cost of honesty so truth can surface unforced, spends a real cost and is resisted by those the order serves, and where the pressure holds the only other road is the cascade, sudden and uncontrollable and as able to install a new lie as to end the old one. It can only show that no one is a villain, that each concealer spares themselves a real cost and no one means to prop up the order they reject, and that the loss is the order kept by its own opponents and not anyone's fraud.
