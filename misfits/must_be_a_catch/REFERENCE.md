# Must Be a Catch: Reference

The research warrant for **Must Be a Catch**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls
**reactive devaluation** (Ross; Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross; Ross and Stillinger): that an
offer's evaluated worth falls for no reason but that an adversary proposed it, since a party assumed to
act in its own interest is assumed to have offered only what serves that interest, so the concessions
most likely to close a negotiation are the ones most heavily discounted, and agreements both sides would
accept from a neutral hand go unmade. It is held with the finding that the effect is conditional on an
adversarial relationship and a traceable source, and that it lifts where the offer is relayed blind, a
trusted mediator carries it, or the relationship itself is no longer adversarial.

## Line of Work

Staging reactive devaluation as a **source-discredits-the-offer structure**: two parties negotiate with
interests genuinely opposed in enough of the relationship to make suspicion of each other's motives, in
general, well founded, and terms exist that both would accept from a neutral hand. But an offer is not
read on its own terms; it is read backward from who made it, since a party assumed to act in its own
interest is assumed to have chosen terms that serve that interest, so before its content is weighed at
all the offer is already marked probably worth refusing (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross). The
discount lands hardest on the best concessions, since a generous offer from a rival reads as the largest
gift from the least trusted source, exactly the reasoning a wary receiver expects a catch to hide inside
(Ross and Stillinger). And the discount is not only private: a negotiator who takes a rival's offer at
face value risks looking duped to the side it answers to, so even one who privately judges the offer
sound has reason to discount it publicly, private suspicion and political cost reinforcing each other
(Ross and Stillinger). The play models the documented mechanics: the attribution from source to content,
the discount falling hardest on the strongest concessions, the political cost of appearing to trust a
rival's offer, the trap running symmetrically as both sides apply the same sound suspicion to each other
in turn, and the recovery available when a trusted third party relays the identical terms, as the
load-bearing vectors of the structure, and not as a claim that negotiators are foolish, intransigent, or
acting in bad faith. The governing finding of the source literature is the play's governing law: an
offer's evaluated worth depends on its attributed source independent of its content, so the very act of
proposing a good deal marks it as suspect, and the better the deal, the more it reads as a trap.

It is an attribution-and-negotiation structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from
its nearest neighbours. It is distinct from Over a Barrel, the hold-up problem, where a party's own sunk,
relationship-specific investment exposes a quasi-rent the counterparty can appropriate: there the leverage
comes from a cost already paid and impossible to recover, whereas here nothing is sunk at all, and the
devaluation attaches to an offer's face value purely by its source, before any investment is made or lost.
It is distinct from The Third Option, the decoy effect, where adding a dominated alternative to a choice
set shifts preference between two existing options: there the mechanism is a comparison induced by a third
item present in the set, whereas here no decoy is involved, only two parties and the offer directly between
them, discounted by attribution and not by contrast with an alternative. It is also distinct from the
house's Anyone Can See It, naive realism, where two parties read a shared situation differently and each
takes its own construal for plain fact: there the disagreement is over what the facts are, whereas here
both sides can agree completely on what is being offered and still discount it, the bias attaching to a
specific proposal's attributed motive rather than to the reading of a shared situation.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the rival as
  paranoid or the proposer as naive, because the finding it stages is that both act soundly: a party facing
  a genuine adversary is right to weigh an offer's likely motive, and a party who offers real terms in good
  faith has done nothing to deserve the discount it receives (Ross and Stillinger). The base that pressures
  against a soft read answers a real political cost, not an imagined one. The loss is authored by no one: it
  is an adversarial relationship plus an inference from source to content plus a political cost for reading
  otherwise, so a good offer is marked suspect by the same reasoning that correctly flags a bad one, and the
  trap runs symmetrically, both sides applying the identical sound suspicion to each other in turn.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. A trusted mediator relaying
  the identical terms stripped of their source restores much of the offer's evaluated worth, shown directly
  by attributing the same proposal to a neutral or trusted party instead (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and
  Ross), but a trusted third party is not summoned by wishing for one, an adversarial relationship rarely has
  one ready to hand, and where the mediator's own neutrality is itself contested, the discount reattaches to
  the channel rather than disappearing. The reckoning is that the discount cannot be argued away from inside
  the relationship that produces it, only carried around it.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The naming and the founding account of reactive devaluation are Ross's, the
  Reagan-or-Gorbachev disarmament demonstration is Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross's, and the wider
  cataloguing among the psychological barriers to a negotiated settlement is Ross and Stillinger's. The scope
  is held to offers with a traceable, adversarial source, and not stretched into a claim that all negotiated
  refusals trace to this bias, since a genuinely bad offer is refused for exactly the reasons it should be,
  and the mechanism staged here explains only the discount that falls on offers that were, on their own
  terms, sound.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Attribution**: carries the inference (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross), that a party assumed
  to act in its own interest is assumed to have offered only what serves that interest, read into an offer
  before its content is weighed.
- **The Discount**: carries the reduced evaluated worth (Ross; Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross), the
  same terms rated less favorable once attributed to a rival than to a neutral or trusted party, falling
  hardest on the most generous concessions.
- **The Mirror**: carries the symmetry (Ross and Stillinger), that the discount is not one side's error but a
  structure both sides apply to each other in turn, each reasonable about a rival whose opposed interest is,
  in general, real.
- **The Backlash**: carries the political cost (Ross and Stillinger), that a negotiator who appears to take a
  rival's offer at face value risks looking duped to its own side, reinforcing a private inference with a
  public one.
- **The Foregone Deal**: carries the phenomenon whole, the terms both sides would accept from a neutral hand
  and will not take from each other, the agreement genuinely available and genuinely unmade.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge (Ross and Stillinger; Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner
  and Ross), that the trap needs an adversarial relationship and a traceable source, and lifts where the
  offer is relayed blind, a trusted mediator carries it, or the relationship stops being adversarial.
