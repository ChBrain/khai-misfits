# Play: Must Be a Catch

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Must Be a Catch.
Two sides sit down to negotiate, each wanting a deal and each willing, in principle, to accept fair terms. If a neutral party wrote out the same proposal and set it in front of both, most would take it: it splits the difference reasonably, it costs each side something and gives each side something, it is the kind of offer negotiations are supposed to end in. And then one side actually makes that offer, and the other side does not take it. Nothing about the terms has changed. Only the name at the bottom of the page has.
And here is why the name changes everything. A rival is assumed to act in its own interest, so an offer from a rival is assumed to have been chosen because it serves that interest, and a term that serves the rival's interest is assumed, by the same reasoning, to disserve the receiver's. The offer is not read on its own terms at all; it is read backward from its source, so before its content is even weighed, it has already been marked as a proposal a self-interested party judged worth making, which is to say, in the receiver's eyes, a proposal probably worth refusing. In the most cited demonstration, Americans rating an identical nuclear arms proposal rated it as more balanced and more favorable to the United States when it was attributed to Reagan than when the very same text was attributed to Gorbachev, the content unchanged and the evaluation moving entirely with the name at the top (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross).
And the discount lands hardest on the best concessions. A small, grudging offer from a rival draws little suspicion, since it costs the rival little to make. But a generous concession, precisely the kind most likely to actually close a deal, reads as the largest gift from the least trustworthy source, and the largest gift from the least trustworthy source is exactly what a wary receiver expects a catch to be hiding in. So the very offers built to move a negotiation forward are the ones devalued most (Ross and Stillinger).
And it is not only a private judgment. A negotiator who looks to take a rival's offer seriously, who treats it as though it might really be worth what it says, risks looking naive or compromised to the constituency it answers to, since accepting help from an adversary at face value reads, to an audience braced for betrayal, as a sign of being duped or turned. So even a negotiator who privately suspects the offer is sound has reason to discount it publicly, and the political cost of looking soft reinforces a bias that started as a private inference, the two feeding each other (Ross and Stillinger).
And the trap runs both ways, with no one the villain of it. Whichever side proposes the good deal, the other discounts it for the same reason, so the discount is not something one side does to the other, it is something both sides do to each other in turn, and each is being perfectly reasonable about a rival whose interests really are opposed to its own in enough of the relationship to make the suspicion, in general, well founded. The loss is authored by no one: reading a rival's motive into a rival's offer is sound practice, and the same soundness is what makes the good offer, the one that would actually close the deal, indistinguishable from a bad one dressed to look good.
And here is what checks it, and why it costs. Strip the offer of its source, and its evaluated worth returns close to what a neutral reading would give it, an effect shown directly by attributing the identical proposal to a trusted or neutral party instead (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross). A genuine mediator, one both sides trust to relay terms without favoring either, can carry a proposal across the gap the direct offer could not cross. But a trusted mediator is not summoned by wishing for one, an adversarial relationship rarely has one ready to hand, and where the mediator's own neutrality is itself contested, the discount simply reattaches to the channel instead of the offer (Ross and Stillinger).
And so the misfit is not that negotiators are foolish or intransigent but that the source of an offer is read as evidence of its content, so the very act of proposing a good deal marks it as suspect, and the better the deal, the more it looks like a trap. The naming and the founding demonstration are Ross's, the disarmament study attributing an identical proposal to Reagan or Gorbachev is Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross's, and the wider cataloguing of reactive devaluation among the psychological barriers to a negotiated settlement is Ross and Stillinger's. The house stages the discount and holds the bound in view: that it needs an adversarial relationship and an offer with a traceable source, that discounting a rival's offer is sound practice and not paranoia, and that it lifts where the offer is relayed blind, a trusted mediator stands between the sides, or the relationship itself stops being adversarial, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that the surest way to make a good offer look bad is to be the one who makes it.

## Arc

The bend is this: where two adversarial parties would each accept a given set of terms from a neutral hand, an offer of those same terms from one party to the other is discounted purely because of who proposed it, and the discount falls hardest on the concessions most likely to close the deal, so agreements both sides would take go unmade, with the discounting sound on both sides and no one at fault.
It runs on an adversarial relationship.
Two parties negotiate with interests that are genuinely opposed in enough of the relationship to make suspicion of each other's motives, in general, well founded (Ross and Stillinger).
And an inference from source to content. A party assumed to act in its own interest is assumed to have offered only what serves that interest, so an offer is read backward from who made it before its terms are weighed on their own (Stillinger, Epelbaum, Keltner and Ross).
And a discount that lands hardest on the best offers. A generous concession reads as the largest gift from the least trusted source, so the concessions most likely to close the deal are the ones most heavily marked down.
And a political cost that reinforces it. A negotiator who takes a rival's offer at face value risks looking duped to its own side, so even one who privately judges the offer sound has reason to discount it publicly, private suspicion and public reputation feeding each other (Ross and Stillinger).
And the trap runs both ways. Whichever side proposes, the other discounts, so the bias is not one side's error but a structure both sides apply to each other in turn, each reasonable about a rival whose opposed interest is, in general, real.
And the loss is authored by no one. Reading a rival's motive into a rival's offer is sound practice, and that same soundness makes the good offer indistinguishable, on sight, from a bad one dressed to look good.
And the whole is held against its own limit. The trap needs an adversarial relationship and a traceable source, and lifts where the offer is relayed blind, a trusted mediator carries it, or the relationship stops being adversarial (Ross and Stillinger).

## Stakes

Whether a negotiation between two rivals can reach terms a neutral hand would call fair, when each side's offer is read backward from who made it before its content is weighed at all.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward terms both sides would accept, which the negotiation genuinely could produce, and toward a discount that falls hardest on exactly those terms, because a party assumed to act in its own interest is assumed to have offered only what serves it, and the better the concession, the more suspicious a gift from a rival it looks. The production cannot make the offer read for what it is, because the discounting is not a misunderstanding to be cleared up: it is sound reasoning about a rival whose opposed interest is, in general, real, so the very inference that protects a side from a bad-faith offer is the inference that marks a good-faith one as bad, the same suspicion the whole relationship runs on now the reason the relationship cannot close. The remedy, a trusted mediator relaying the offer stripped of its source, spends a real and scarce resource, since an adversarial relationship rarely has a trusted third party ready to hand, and where the mediator's own standing is contested the discount reattaches to the channel instead of vanishing. It can only show that no one is at fault, that the proposer offered in good faith, that the rival's suspicion of a rival is sound practice and not paranoia, and that the base's demand not to look duped answers a real political cost and not an imagined one, the same soundness on every side producing a deal that never closes. And it holds the bound in view, because the trap is conditional and not universal: it needs an adversarial relationship and a traceable source, and lifts where the offer is relayed blind, a trusted mediator carries it, or the relationship stops being adversarial, so the whole of the misfit is that the surest way to make a good offer look bad is to be the one who makes it.
