# Play: The Skill to See It

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Skill to See It.
A person does a thing, a piece of reasoning, a turn of grammar, a joke, a hand of cards, and then does the most natural thing in the world: they judge how well they did. That judging is the whole of the matter, and it hides a trap, because to tell a good answer from a bad one in some field draws on the very same knowledge that makes a good answer in the first place. The grammar that lets you write a correct sentence is the grammar that lets you catch a wrong one; the logic that solves the problem is the logic that spots the flaw. So the skill to do a thing and the skill to judge the doing are, at bottom, one skill. And that is the whole misfit, because a person who lacks that skill lacks both halves of it at once: they do the thing poorly, and they cannot tell that they did, since the faculty that would tell them is the very one they are short of. The deficit hides itself. Justin Kruger and David Dunning set it down: the incompetent are not only incompetent, they are unaware of it, and unaware because incompetent.
And here is the shape it makes, and why each person is only being honest. Ask people to rate themselves against others, and those who score at the very bottom place themselves near the top, holding a confidence that is highest exactly where their skill is lowest, while those who score at the top rate themselves a little too low, because the task came easily to them and they quietly assume it came as easily to everyone (Kruger and Dunning; Ehrlinger and colleagues). No one is lying. Each is doing their honest best to measure themselves with the only ruler they have, and for the unskilled that ruler is broken in the exact place they are trying to read.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The temptation is to call the confident novice a fool too proud to know it, but the finding is stranger and kinder than that: the overconfidence is blindness, not conceit. The person cannot see their gap because seeing it would take the skill they lack, so they arrive in good faith at a wrong and flattering answer, and would need to become more skilled merely to grow more doubtful. The doubting expert is no more virtuous, only mistaken in the other direction. The loss is authored by no one. It is the miscalibration that follows whenever the measure of a skill is that same skill, and the misfit is that the less you have of it, the less you can see that you lack it.
And here is the way out, and why it is so singular. There is only one road, and it is the long one: to gain the skill itself, because the same training that makes a person competent gives them, in the same motion, the metacognition to see where they had fallen short, so self-insight arrives with the skill and by no shorter path (Kruger and Dunning). You cannot simply tell someone they are unskilled, because they must judge that claim with the faculty they lack, so the news does not land and is waved away exactly as the effect predicts. There is no fixing the judgement without building the skill beneath it.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The effect is famous and it is heavily contested. Critics show that much of the classic pattern falls out of regression to the mean combined with the plain better-than-average tendency, and can be conjured even from random numbers, so that its true size, and even its standing as a distinct failure of self-insight rather than a statistical shadow, are genuinely in dispute (Krueger and Mueller; Gignac and Zajenkowski). The name is Kruger and Dunning's, the replication of the incompetent's absent self-insight Ehrlinger and colleagues', the mature account Dunning's, and the sceptical counter-reading Krueger and Mueller's and others'. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where the measure of a skill is that same skill, those who most lack it are the last to know, but how large and how real that failing is remains an open question, not a settled one.

## Arc

The bend is this: where judging how well a task is done draws on the very skill the task itself requires, those who lack the skill also lack the means to see that they lack it, so the least able judge themselves near the best and hold their overconfidence most firmly where their skill is lowest, while the most able, finding the task easy, underrate themselves, and the only remedy is to gain the skill that would also let them see its absence.
It runs on a skill that must both do and judge.
To tell a good answer from a bad one in a field draws on the same knowledge that makes a good answer, so the skill to perform and the skill to judge the performing are one skill (Kruger and Dunning).
And the one who lacks it lacks both halves. They do the thing poorly and cannot tell that they did, because the faculty that would tell them is the one they are short of, so the deficit hides itself.
And so the least skilled rate themselves high. Reading their own work with a broken ruler, they place themselves near the top, most confident exactly where their skill is least (Ehrlinger and colleagues).
And the most skilled rate themselves low. Finding the task easy, they assume by a false consensus that all find it easy, and mark themselves down (Dunning).
And it is authored by no vanity. The confident novice is blind and not conceited, judging honestly with the tools they have, so the loss is the miscalibration and not a fault of character.
And the only way out is to gain the skill. The same training that builds competence builds the self-insight to see its absence, and no telling can stand in for it (Kruger and Dunning).
And the whole is held against its limit. The effect is contested, much of the pattern reproducible by regression and the better-than-average tendency, its size and reality disputed (Krueger and Mueller; Gignac and Zajenkowski).

## Stakes

Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the self-rating that misses the mark, and away from the true measure the person cannot reach, because the ruler they read themselves with is the skill they are trying to measure, and for the unskilled it is broken exactly there.
