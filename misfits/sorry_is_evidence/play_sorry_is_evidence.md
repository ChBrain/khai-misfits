# Play: Sorry Is Evidence

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Sorry Is Evidence.
Something went wrong, and somebody is harmed. What they want, before money and often instead of it, is to be told what happened, to be told it plainly, to hear that somebody is sorry, and to know it will not happen to the next person. That is not a legal demand and it costs nothing to supply. And it is the one thing the institution cannot give them, because an explanation of what went wrong is an account of fault, and an account of fault is evidence.

## Arc

The bend is this: the cheapest thing the harmed person wants is the most expensive thing the institution can give.
It runs on a question that has an answer and no permitted route to it.
The One Who Asked What Happened is asking for the ordinary thing. When claimants are studied rather than assumed, what they say they wanted was an explanation, an assurance that others would be protected, some accountability, and compensation, in roughly that order: and the decision to sue turned not only on the original injury but on how they were handled afterwards. Of those who were given an explanation, fewer than fifteen per cent found it satisfactory.
The One Who Wants to Say It is standing right there. The clinician who was in the room usually wants to explain and to apologise, and is often the person most distressed by what happened. Nothing in their character or their profession is the obstacle here.
The One Who Guards the Claim is right to advise against it. An explanation of fault is admissible, an apology may be read as an admission, and the duty of counsel and of the insurer runs to the institution and to every future claimant's premium. This is not cowardice: it is accurate advice about what will happen to a sentence spoken in a corridor once it is written down in a witness statement.
So nobody lies, and nobody says anything. The silence is lawful, advised, and produced by people who are each doing their job properly.
And then The Only Way to Find Out opens. If the institution will not explain, the instrument that compels an explanation is a claim: disclosure, records, witness statements, an expert report. Litigation is a slow and ruinous method of answering a question, and it is the only method left, so the person who wanted a conversation issues proceedings to obtain one.
The Statute That Protects the Words was written to end exactly this. Apology laws make expressions of sympathy inadmissible so that saying sorry is safe. And the study of ninety per cent of single-specialty physicians across eight years found that on balance these laws increase liability risk rather than reduce it: no substantial effect for surgeons, and for non-surgeons a higher probability of facing a claim and a higher average payment. The mechanism is the thing the statute could not reach. An apology conveys information the patient did not have, and it is the information rather than the words that produces the claim, so the protection covers the sentence and leaves the fact it carries entirely exposed.
And the answer that works arrives with a cheque. Communication and resolution programmes pair the explanation with an early offer of compensation, and they do reduce the litigation, because they give the person all four of the things they asked for instead of one. They also require an institution to admit fault before any tribunal has found it, to decide its own liability, and to pay claims it was never compelled to pay, out of money that was not set aside for the ones that would never have been brought.

## Stakes

Whether an account of what happened can ever be given by the party best placed to give it, when giving it is the act that decides who pays.
Every plot moves the same way: toward a family asking a fair question, a clinician who wants to answer it, counsel advising accurately, and a legislature trying to help, and away from any instrument that could separate the telling from the liability, since the telling is what establishes the liability. The production cannot make an explanation inadmissible in substance, and it cannot ask an insurer to fund the consequences of candour it never priced. What is left to fight over is not whether people should be told, everybody agrees they should, but who pays for the sentence that tells them, and whether a system in which the courtroom is the only place an answer exists was ever going to produce fewer courtrooms.
