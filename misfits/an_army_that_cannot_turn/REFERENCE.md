# An Army That Cannot Turn: Reference

The research warrant for **An Army That Cannot Turn**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls
**coup-proofing** and its **loyalty-versus-effectiveness trade-off** (Quinlivan; Talmadge): that a ruler
who must fear its own armed forces secures itself by counterweight forces, by selecting officers on
loyalty rather than ability, by rotating commanders before they build followings, by curtailing
large-formation training, and by blocking lateral communication between units, and that every one of
those measures is also, exactly, a measure that destroys the ability to fight, so the state that cannot
be overthrown cannot defend itself. It is held with the finding that the trade is real, knowing, and
conditional: regimes facing severe external threat do professionalise and do fight better, and pay for
it in coup exposure, and one army may be coup-proofed in some of its units and professionalised in
others.

## Line of Work

Staging coup-proofing as a **safety-bought-with-capability structure**: the ordinary expectation is
that a state wants its army to be as good as possible, since an army exists to fight. For a ruler whose
gravest threat is the army itself, that expectation inverts, because military effectiveness is not a
neutral good but a capability that can be turned inward. Quinlivan's account names the practices and
they are specific: parallel or counterweight forces that answer separately so no single command can
seize the state; officers selected by kinship, sect, region or personal loyalty rather than by ability;
commanders rotated before they can build a following inside a unit; large-formation combined-arms
exercises curtailed, because a division moving at scale is indistinguishable from a division moving on
the capital; and horizontal communication between units suppressed, because units that cannot talk to
one another cannot conspire. Each of these is a proportionate answer to a real and well-attested
internal danger. Each of them is also the removal of a specific military capability: counterweights
fragment command and compete for resources; loyalty selection installs officers who cannot fight;
rotation prevents the cohesion and the local competence that units are made of; curtailed exercises
mean no combined arms; and suppressed lateral communication means no manoeuvre, since manoeuvre is
lateral communication under fire. The play models the three levers, structural, personnel and
informational, as three distinct vectors with three distinct costs, and not as one lever named three
times. The ordinary expectation is that a ruler wants a capable army; the finding is that where the
army is the threat, capability is the thing being defended against. The governing law is that **where a
ruler must fear its own armed forces, the measures that secure it against them are the same measures
that destroy their ability to fight, so safety from the army and the army's usefulness cannot both be
bought**.

It is a control-versus-capability structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from
its nearest neighbours. It is distinct from Rising to Unfit, the Peter Principle, where competence is
the promotion criterion and simply fails to transfer to the duties of the new post: there ability is
what is being rewarded and the trouble is that it does not carry, whereas here ability is the
**disqualifying** trait and is selected against on purpose, by someone who knows what it costs. It is
distinct from The Dilbert Principle, where the incapable are moved to where they can do least damage:
there incompetence is routed around, whereas here the **capable** are removed precisely for being
capable. It is distinct from The Dead Sea Effect, where the talented leave of their own accord and the
residue is whoever remained: there the evaporation is self-selected and nobody drives it, whereas here
nobody chooses to leave, the talent is moved or set aside by a deliberate hand, and loyalists are
installed in its place. It is distinct from Pournelle's Iron Law, where an organisation is captured by
those devoted to the organisation over its mission: there the drift is unintended and the captured
organisation still believes it is pursuing its purpose, whereas here the ruler is perfectly
goal-directed and clear-eyed, and the goal, regime survival, is simply orthogonal to the army's stated
purpose, the trade being made knowingly and in full view of its cost.
It therefore joins that play on the axis `internal-advancement`, at the same sign and by the opposite
temperament. The quantity in both is how much advancement weights devotion to the body over service to the
mission, and the difference the paragraph above draws is the **engine**: there the weighting accumulates
because nobody is watching it and the organisation still believes it is pursuing its purpose, and here it
is set deliberately, by a ruler who can price the capability being given up and pays it anyway. And it is distinct from The
Brittle Optimum, where slack is trimmed for efficiency until nothing absorbs a shock: there every trim
is a true saving and the loss of resilience is a by-product nobody sought, whereas here nothing is
removed for economy, capability is removed **for control**, and the one removing it can name exactly
what it is buying and exactly what it is giving up.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast a paranoid
  tyrant or a treacherous officer corps, because the finding it stages needs neither. The coup threat
  is not imagined: in the settings Quinlivan describes it is frequent, recent, personal and fatal, and
  a ruler who ignored it would not be principled but dead. The officers set aside are not traitors and
  are mostly not even suspected of anything, since the practice selects against a **capability** and not
  against a person. The counterweight commander is doing the job it was given and doing it well. The
  professional colonel keeping its unit ready is loyal in fact and is obstructed anyway. Nobody in the
  play wants the army to lose, and the ruler least of all, an army that loses being a throne that
  falls.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction, and here the
  correction is the threat itself. The remedy for an army that cannot fight is professionalisation:
  promote on merit, exercise at formation scale, push initiative down, and let units talk to one
  another freely. That list is not merely expensive, it is **identically the list of capabilities
  required to mount a coup**, so the ruler cannot buy competence without buying the means of its own
  removal, and the reckoning is that there is no configuration of the army that is both safe and
  useful. Talmadge supplies the no-free-hero in evidence rather than in argument: rulers under severe
  external threat do take the other side of the trade, do fight better for it, and do become easier to
  overthrow.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The account of coup-proofing as a set of named practices with a cost in
  effectiveness is Quinlivan's; the evidence that the trade is real, conditional on the external threat
  a regime faces, and variable between units of a single army is Talmadge's; and the neighbouring trade
  in which exclusion converts a coup risk into a civil-war risk is Roessler's, drawn to mark the
  distinction and not to claim his result. The scope is held to states whose rulers face a credible
  internal threat from their own armed forces, and is not stretched into a claim that authoritarian
  armies are uniformly incompetent, that coup-proofing explains every battlefield defeat, or that the
  practices are irrational, since the source literature presents the trade as a trade and the play
  stages it exactly as one.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Two Clocks**: carries the asymmetry that makes the trade rational (Talmadge), the coup being
  near, personal, certain in kind and fatal to the ruler itself, and the war being deferred,
  probabilistic and borne mostly by others, so a correct decision on the ruler's own discount rate is
  the decision that loses the war.
- **The Counterweight**: carries the structural lever (Quinlivan), the parallel force that answers
  separately so no single command can seize the state, and the same fragmentation of command that means
  no unified operation and two armies competing for one supply.
- **The Shuffle**: carries the personnel lever (Quinlivan), officers chosen by loyalty and moved before
  a following can form, and the cohesion and local competence that a unit is made of and that cannot
  form either.
- **The Silence**: carries the informational lever (Quinlivan), suppressed lateral communication and
  curtailed formation-scale exercise, since units that cannot speak to one another cannot conspire and
  cannot manoeuvre, manoeuvre being lateral communication under fire.
- **The Same List**: carries the reason there is no way out, that the measures which would restore the
  ability to fight are identically the measures which would restore the ability to depose, so the
  remedy cannot be separated from the threat and no amount of good faith on either side separates them.
- **The Bill**: carries what the war collects, an army that cannot concentrate, cannot combine and
  cannot move, defeated not by any failure of courage but by a design that was answering a different
  question.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge (Talmadge), that the trap needs a credible
  internal threat and lifts where the external threat dominates, that rulers do then professionalise and
  do fight better and become easier to overthrow, and that the trade can be struck differently in
  different units of one army, so it is a trade and not a law of nature.
