# Play: An Army That Cannot Turn

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

An Army That Cannot Turn.
For most states, the army is an instrument and the only question about it is whether it is good enough. Train it harder, equip it better, promote the ablest, let it practise at the scale it will fight at, and it becomes more useful without becoming more dangerous, because the danger it is being made ready for is outside the borders. This is the ordinary shape, and it is why most states want the best army they can afford.
And here is the case where the shape inverts. For a ruler whose gravest and nearest threat is the army itself, military capability is not a neutral good. The things that make a force able to fight are the same things that make it able to act: officers the troops will follow, formations that can concentrate and move, units that talk to one another and take initiative without waiting to be told. Every one of those is a coup capability as much as a combat capability, because a coup is an operation (Quinlivan).
And so the ruler does the sensible thing, and the sensible thing is fatal. It raises a second force that answers separately, so that no single command can take the state. It picks officers it can vouch for, by kinship or region or long service, over officers who are merely good. It moves commanders on before they can build a following inside a unit. It does not schedule the division-scale exercise, because a division moving at scale is indistinguishable from a division moving on the capital. And it keeps units from talking sideways to each other, because units that cannot talk cannot conspire.
And every one of those measures has a second face. Two forces that answer separately cannot fight as one and will compete for the same fuel. Officers picked for loyalty are, on average, worse at their trade than officers picked for competence. Commanders moved every eighteen months never learn their ground or their men, and the unit never becomes a unit. An army that has not exercised above battalion level cannot combine arms. And units that cannot speak sideways cannot manoeuvre, because manoeuvre is lateral communication under fire (Quinlivan).
And nobody in it is a monster. The coup threat is not a fantasy: in the settings this describes it is frequent, recent, and personally fatal, and a ruler who disregarded it would not be high-minded but dead within the year. The guard commander is doing well the job it was given. The security man keeping officers from settling is preventing exactly what he was appointed to prevent. The officers passed over are not traitors and are mostly not even suspected of anything, since the practice is selecting against a capability and not against a person. And nobody wants the army to lose, the ruler least of all, an army that loses being a throne that falls.
And now the cruelty. The remedy is known, and it is the threat. To make the army able to fight you promote on merit, you exercise at formation scale, you push initiative down to the men who can see the ground, and you let units talk to each other freely. That is not merely an expensive list. It is identically the list of capabilities required to mount a coup, so the ruler cannot buy competence without buying the means of its own removal, and there is no version of the reform that delivers the fighting and withholds the deposing.
And the bill is presented by someone who was not in the conversation. The war comes, or it does not. If it does, the army cannot concentrate, cannot combine and cannot move, and it is beaten by a smaller force that can do all three, and the men who die doing what they were told are not the men who made the trade.
And so the misfit is not that dictators are foolish but that safety from your own army and the usefulness of your own army are bought with the same coin, and the coin only spends once. Coup-proofing as a set of named practices with a cost in effectiveness is Quinlivan's; the evidence that the trade is real, that it turns on how badly a regime is threatened from outside, and that one army can be coup-proofed in some units and professionalised in others, is Talmadge's; and the neighbouring trade in which excluding rivals converts a coup risk into a civil war is Roessler's, held near to mark the distinction. The house stages the three levers and holds the bound in view: that the trap needs a credible internal threat, that it lifts where the external threat dominates, and that rulers who take the other side of the trade do fight better and do become easier to overthrow, so the whole of it is that an army built so it can never turn on you is an army that cannot turn at all.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a ruler must fear its own armed forces, every measure that secures it against them removes a specific capacity to fight, so the safety and the weakness are one purchase, and the state that cannot be overthrown cannot defend itself, with no party acting unreasonably and the remedy identical to the danger.
It runs on a threat that is real and near.
The coup is not imagined: frequent, recent, personal, and fatal to the ruler itself, against a war that is deferred, probabilistic, and borne mostly by other people (Talmadge).
And a structural answer that splits the sword. A counterweight force answering separately means no single command can seize the state, and means no unified operation and two armies drawing on one supply.
And a personnel answer that empties the officer corps. Selection by loyalty and rotation before a following forms mean no commander can take a unit with him, and mean no commander ever learns his ground, his men, or his trade.
And an informational answer that stops the army moving. Suppressed lateral communication and a formation exercise that is never scheduled mean units cannot conspire, and mean they cannot combine or manoeuvre either, manoeuvre being lateral communication under fire (Quinlivan).
And a remedy that is the disease. Merit promotion, formation-scale exercise, initiative pushed down, and units free to talk are the list that restores the fighting, and identically the list that restores the deposing.
And nobody unreasonable anywhere in it. The threat is genuine, the guard is competent, the security man is doing his appointed job, the officers set aside are not suspected, and no party wants the army to lose.
And a bill presented to those who did not strike the bargain. The war finds a force that cannot concentrate, combine or move, and the men who die are not the men who chose.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs a credible internal threat, it lifts where the external threat dominates, and the rulers who take the other side of it fight better and are easier to overthrow (Talmadge).
