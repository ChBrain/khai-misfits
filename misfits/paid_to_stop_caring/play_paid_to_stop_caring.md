# Play: Paid to Stop Caring

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Paid to Stop Caring.
A person does a thing for its own sake. A child draws because drawing delights it, a solver works a puzzle because the puzzle is fun, a volunteer gives blood because giving feels right. The motive is inside the act, and the act is its own reward.
And then, in good faith, someone offers a reward to encourage the valued thing: a gold star for the drawing, a payment for the puzzle, a fee for the blood. By every ordinary rule this should help, since a reward added to a thing already wanted should make it wanted more. But something turns instead. The person, seeing itself paid to do what it once did freely, reads a reason from the reward: I must be doing this for the star, the money, the fee, and not simply because I love it, an over-justification that supplies an external motive where none was needed (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett; Deci). The act is now done for the reward, and the love of it has quietly gone.
And here is where it becomes a trap, and not merely a wash. When the reward stops, or comes to feel too small, the doing does not return to where it began. It falls below the baseline it had before any reward, because the inner motive that once carried it has been displaced and does not spring back when the reward is withdrawn, so the child rewarded for drawing later draws less than the child never rewarded at all, and the thing loved for itself is done less once it has been paid for (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett; Deci, Koestner and Ryan).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The one who offers the reward means only to encourage a valued thing, and follows the sound and ordinary rule that incentives strengthen behaviour, which is true of almost everything except a thing already loved. And the person whose motive shifts is not lazy or greedy or weak: it is reading its own reasons the way anyone reads them, from what it sees itself doing and why, and being paid to do a thing is a reason to think one does it for the pay. No one's ill will kills the love of it. The loss is authored by no one: it is the intrinsic interest crowded out by the very reward that meant to feed it.
And here is the way out, and why it is narrow. There is a better way to reward, but it is easily missed: a reward that informs, that tells the person it did well, a word of praise, an unexpected acknowledgement, a recognition of real competence, can encourage without supplying a controlling reason, because it feeds the sense of doing the thing well rather than the sense of doing it for pay (Deci, Koestner and Ryan). And sometimes the truest course is to leave the freely given unpaid, to let the love of the thing be its own wage. But this asks the giver to give up the simple lever of the expected reward, to read whether a thing is already loved before pricing it, and to forgo the control that paying buys, and none of it is the plain, reliable incentive the ordinary rule promises.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The effect is real but bounded. It is strongest for an expected tangible reward given merely for doing an already-loved task and offered as control, and it is weak or absent for a dull task, for an unexpected reward, for verbal praise, and for a reward tied to a real standard of competence. Its size was once sharply contested, some reading it as small or overstated, the debate resolving toward a real but conditional effect (Cameron and Pierce; Deci, Koestner and Ryan). The first demonstration is Deci's, the over-justification naming and the child-drawing test Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's, the synthesis and Cognitive Evaluation Theory Deci, Koestner and Ryan's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that a controlling reward can crowd out a love already there, and not that rewards never work or that paying for anything kills the wanting of it.

## Arc

The bend is this: a reward offered to encourage what someone already did for its own sake reframes the act as done for the reward, so when the reward stops the inner motive that sustained it is gone, and paying for the freely given can kill the giving.
It runs on a wage that buys away the love it meant to feed.
A person does a thing for its own sake, the motive inside the act and the act its own reward.
And a reward is offered, in good faith, to encourage the valued thing, by the rule that incentives strengthen behaviour.
And the person reads a reason from it. I must do this for the reward and not for love of it, an external motive supplied where none was needed (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett).
And the inner motive is crowded out. The act now done for the reward and the love of it quietly gone (Deci).
And the reward stops, and the doing falls off. Below the baseline it had before any reward, the thing loved now done less than ever it was unpaid.
And it is authored by no one. The giver means only to encourage and the person only reads its own reasons, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is a reward that informs. Praise or the unexpected that feeds competence not control, or the freely given left unpaid, and the bound held, the effect real but bounded and once contested (Deci, Koestner and Ryan).

## Stakes

Whether a love of doing can survive being paid for, when the very reward that means to encourage it supplies the reason that crowds it out.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the act done for the reward and away from the love of it, because the person reads a reason from the reward and, being paid to do what it once did freely, comes to do it for the pay. The structure cannot both add the controlling reward and keep the love intact, because the reward supplies an external justification for a thing that needed none, and a motive read from the outside displaces the one that was inside: the same reward that should have strengthened the doing is the reward that hollows its reason, so when the reward is gone the doing falls not to its old height but below it, the love not merely unfed but spent. The way out, a reward that informs rather than controls, a word of praise or an unexpected recognition of real competence, or the leaving of the freely given unpaid, forgoes the simple lever of the expected reward and asks the giver to read whether a thing is already loved before pricing it, and it is not the plain and reliable incentive the ordinary rule promises. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the giver means only to encourage and the person only reads its own reasons as anyone would, and that the loss is the love crowded out by the reward that meant to feed it and not anyone's ill will. And it holds the bound in view, because the effect is real but bounded, strongest for an expected tangible controlling reward on an already-loved task and weak or absent otherwise, its size once sharply contested (Deci, Koestner and Ryan; Cameron and Pierce), so the whole of the misfit is that paying for the freely given can make it less freely given, and a love already there can be crowded out by the very wage that meant to reward it.
