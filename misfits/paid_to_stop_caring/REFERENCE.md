# Paid to Stop Caring: Reference

The research warrant for **Paid to Stop Caring**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon named the
**overjustification effect**, the crowding-out of intrinsic motivation by extrinsic reward: a reward
offered to encourage what someone already did for its own sake reframes the act as done for the
reward, so when the reward stops the inner motive that sustained it is gone, and paying for the
freely given can kill the giving, all cited below, held with the finding that the effect is real but
bounded, strongest for expected tangible controlling rewards on already-interesting tasks and weak
or absent otherwise, its magnitude once sharply contested.

## Line of Work

Staging the overjustification effect as a **reward-crowds-out-the-motive structure**: a person does a thing for its own
sake. A child draws because drawing delights it, a solver works a puzzle because the puzzle is fun, a reader reads
for the reading. The motive is inside the act, and the act is its own reward. And then, in good faith, someone
offers a reward to encourage the valued thing: a gold star for the drawing, a payment for the puzzle, a penny a page.
By every ordinary rule this should strengthen the behaviour, since a reward added to a thing already wanted should make it
wanted more. But something turns instead. The person, seeing itself paid to do what it once did freely, infers a reason from
the reward: I must be doing this for the star, the money, the fee, and not simply because I love it, an over-justification
that supplies an external motive and crowds the internal one out (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett; Deci). The act is now done for
the reward, and the love of it has quietly gone. And here is where it becomes a trap, and not merely a wash. When the reward
stops, or comes to feel too small, the behaviour does not return to where it began. It falls below its unrewarded baseline,
because the inner motive that once carried it has been displaced and does not spring back on the reward's withdrawal, so the
child rewarded for drawing later draws less than the child never rewarded at all, and the thing loved for itself is done
less once it has been paid for (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett; Deci, Koestner and Ryan). It is authored by no villain: the one
who offers the reward means only to encourage the valued thing and follows the sound and ordinary rule that incentives
strengthen behaviour, and the person whose motive shifts is not lazy or greedy but genuinely re-reading its own reasons from
what it sees itself paid to do. No one's ill will kills the love of it. The governing law is not that anyone is manipulative
or weak, but that **a reward offered to encourage what someone already did for its own sake reframes the act as done for the
reward, so when the reward stops the inner motive that sustained it is gone, and paying for the freely given can kill the
giving, no villain required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the love of the thing done for its own sake, the
reward offered in good faith to encourage it, the reframing that reads a new reason from the reward, the crowding-out of the
inner motive by the outer, the falling-off below the unrewarded baseline when the reward stops, and the right reward that
informs rather than controls and alone can encourage without undermining (Deci; Lepper, Greene and Nisbett; Deci, Koestner
and Ryan), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a bribe corrupting a soul.

It is a reward-crowds-out-the-motive structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from The Cobra Effect, where a reward offered for a good outcome breeds a workaround that games
it, as a bounty on dead cobras breeds the breeding of cobras to kill: there the incentive is gamed and produces more of the
very thing it meant to reduce, whereas here the incentive is not gamed at all but crowds out the inner motive, so the
behaviour does not multiply perversely but collapses when the reward is gone. It is distinct from Metric Fixation, where a
measure made into a target ceases to measure what it did, since effort bends to the number: there what is corrupted is the
measure, the proxy that stops tracking the goal, whereas here what is corrupted is the motivation, the inner reason for the
act, and no measure need be involved at all. It is distinct from the house's **A Reason Too Many**, which stages the other half of this
literature and is the reason the two must not be read as one: there a penalty enters an incomplete
social contract and changes what two parties take themselves to owe one another, so a moral
obligation becomes a service that can be bought, which is Gneezy and Rustichini's own reading of the
day-care fine and explicitly not a claim about self-perception. Here there is no second party and no
bargain to revise. One person, alone, reads its own reasons off its own behaviour and concludes it
must be drawing for the star: the mechanism is over-justification, it runs inside one head, and it
would run just the same if nobody else existed. And it is distinct from Moral Licensing, where a good deed is banked as moral
credit that a later lapse draws down: there the mechanism is a moral accounting across acts, a virtue funding a vice,
whereas here the mechanism is a single act's inner motive displaced by an outer one, no ledger of credit and no later lapse
bought, only the love of the thing quietly spent.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the reward as a bribe or the
  rewarder as a corrupter, because the source finding it stages is that the trap needs no ill will: the one who offers the
  reward means only to encourage a valued thing and follows the sound and ordinary rule that incentives strengthen
  behaviour, and the person whose motive shifts is not lazy or venal but genuinely re-reading its own reasons from what it
  sees itself paid to do. No one's manipulation kills the love of it. The loss is authored by no one: it is the intrinsic
  interest crowded out by the very reward that meant to feed it, and the misfit is that paying for the freely given can make
  it less freely given, or not given at all.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. There is a better way to reward, but it is
  narrow and easily missed: a reward that informs competence rather than controls behaviour, verbal praise, an unexpected
  acknowledgement, an autonomy-supporting recognition, can encourage without undermining, and sometimes the truest course is
  to leave the freely given unpaid (Deci, Koestner and Ryan). But this asks the rewarder to give up the simple lever of the
  expected tangible reward, to read whether a thing is already loved before pricing it, and to forgo the control that paying
  buys, and none of it is the plain and reliable incentive the ordinary rule promises. The escape works against the grain of
  the intuition that more reward is always more motivation.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the first demonstration is Deci's, the over-justification naming and the child-drawing
  test are Lepper, Greene and Nisbett's, the meta-analytic synthesis and Cognitive Evaluation Theory are Deci, Koestner and
  Ryan's, and the dissent and the field reach are Cameron and Pierce's and the motivation-crowding economists'. The
  mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding: the effect is real but bounded. It is strongest for expected tangible
  rewards given for merely doing an already-interesting task and offered as control, and weak or absent for dull tasks, for
  unexpected rewards, for verbal praise, and for rewards tied to a standard of competence; its magnitude was sharply
  contested, some meta-analyses finding it small or limited (Cameron and Pierce), and the debate resolved toward a real but
  conditional effect. The scope is held to the crowding-out of an existing intrinsic motive by a controlling extrinsic
  reward, and not stretched into a claim that rewards never work or that paying for anything destroys the wanting of it.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Love of It**: carries the setting, the intrinsic motive, the thing done for its own sake and needing no reward
  because the act is its own reward, the drawing delighted in and the puzzle enjoyed.
- **The Reward**: carries the tool, the extrinsic incentive offered in good faith to encourage the valued thing, the gold
  star or the payment or the fee, added by the sound and ordinary rule that a reward strengthens a behaviour.
- **The Reframing**: carries the mechanism (Lepper, Greene and Nisbett), the over-justification, the person reading a new
  reason from the reward, that it must be doing the thing for the reward and not for love of it, an external motive supplied
  where none was needed.
- **The Crowding-Out**: carries the substitution (Deci; Deci, Koestner and Ryan), the inner motive supplanted by the outer,
  the act now done for the reward and the love of it quietly gone, the controlling reward undermining the autonomy the
  intrinsic motive rested on.
- **The Falling-Off**: carries the harm, the behaviour falling below its unrewarded baseline once the reward stops or comes
  to feel too small, the thing loved for itself now done less than before it was ever paid for, drawn distinct from The
  Cobra Effect, Metric Fixation, and Moral Licensing.
- **The Right Reward**: carries the remedy and the bound (Deci, Koestner and Ryan; Cameron and Pierce), the reward that
  informs competence rather than controls behaviour, the praise or the unexpected acknowledgement that can encourage without
  undermining, or the leaving of the freely given unpaid, costly because it forgoes the simple lever of the tangible
  reward, and the marker of the hedge, since the effect is real but bounded and its size was fought over.
