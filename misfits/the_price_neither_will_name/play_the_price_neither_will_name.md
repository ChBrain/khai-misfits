# Play: The Price Neither Will Name

## Estate

## Name

The Price Neither Will Name.
Two people have a deal to make. A buyer would pay as much as ten for a thing; a seller would let it go for as little as six. Between six and ten sits a gain of four that a trade would split, and both of them want it made. This is the easy case, the one where a price surely exists: any number from six to ten leaves both better off than no deal at all. And yet the deal can die, and die for a reason neither did wrong.
Each knows only its own number. The buyer knows it would pay ten but not what the seller would take; the seller knows it would accept six but not what the buyer would pay. So the buyer, to keep more of the gain, offers seven and calls it its best; the seller, to keep more of the gain, asks nine and calls it its floor. Neither is lying in any way that could be caught: from where each sits, naming the true number simply hands the other the whole four, so protecting it is the sound move and not a cheat. But seven and nine do not cross, though ten and six would, and the trade sits unmade on a gain that was really there (Myerson and Satterthwaite).
And here is why no clever rule rescues it. Bring in a broker with a mechanism, any mechanism, a split-the-difference rule, a sealed-bid rule, an auction: whatever it is, the parties will play it to their advantage, so the broker may as well ask them to report their values and design the rule to make honest reporting their best reply. But honest reporting has a price. To make the buyer willing to admit a high value, the rule must let it keep a slice of the gain it could otherwise have hidden; to make the seller willing to admit a low cost, the rule must let it keep a slice too. That slice is the information rent, and it is the cost of pulling the truth out of a party that would rather hold it. The catch Myerson and Satterthwaite proved is that on the trades near the edge of the overlap, the two rents together come to more than the gain itself, so a rule that pays both rents out of the gain runs short, and a rule that refuses to run short must let those edge trades fall. No mechanism can be at once efficient, voluntary, truthful, and self-funding: one of the four has to give (Myerson and Satterthwaite).
And so the surplus dies in the gap with no culprit. The buyer offered low and the seller held high, each playing the only sensible hand; the broker ran a sound rule; and still the trade that would have left both better off did not happen, its gain going to no one's pocket. The loss reads as a puzzle, two willing parties who somehow could not agree, when it is a consequence: the rent that private information commands is larger, at the margin, than the gain it guards, so the gain cannot pay for its own revelation.
And here is the only way out, and what it costs. Efficiency is reachable: a rule can clear every trade the values warrant, if someone outside the trade covers the shortfall, paying each side its rent from a purse that is not the gain, so the Vickrey rule buys the efficient trade by running a deficit (Vickrey; Clarke; Groves). Or the parties can start not as a fixed buyer and a fixed seller but as equal co-owners of the thing, and then a balanced, voluntary, efficient rule does exist (Cramton, Gibbons, and Klemperer). But an ordinary trade has no outside purse and no chosen starting structure, so within it the choice is real and hard: subsidise the deal and break the balance of the books, or keep the books balanced and lose some of the deals the values warrant.
And so the misfit is not that markets fail but that private value has a price of its own. The impossibility is Myerson and Satterthwaite's, the revelation logic that fixes the rent is Myerson's, the efficient rule that survives only on a subsidy is Vickrey's, Clarke's, and Groves's, and the equal-ownership escape is Cramton, Gibbons, and Klemperer's. The house stages the foreclosure and holds the bound in view: that the trap needs two-sided private value over overlapping ranges, and lifts under one-sided information, a known value, guaranteed gains, or an outside purse, so the whole of it is that the price neither will name is the price that would have made the deal, and the naming is the one thing no rule can buy cheaply enough.

## Arc

The bend is this: where two parties bargain over a real gain and each privately knows only its own value across overlapping ranges, no voluntary, truthful, budget-balanced rule can realise every trade both would gain from, so some efficient trades are foreclosed by the rent private information commands, with no one lying and no rule at fault.
It runs on a real gain between two private values.
A buyer values a thing above what a seller would take for it, so a surplus sits between them that a trade would split and both would gain from, and both want the trade made (Myerson and Satterthwaite).
And each knows only its own number. The buyer knows its top price and the seller its floor, and neither can see the other's, only guess at a range, so the gain is real but its size is hidden from both.
And each shades to claim more of it. The buyer states below its value and the seller above its cost, each the best reply under any rule that asks them to report, since to name the true number is to hand the gain away, and neither is lying (Myerson).
And the shaded figures need not cross. Seven and nine do not meet though ten and six would, so the trade that the true values warrant is not struck, foreclosed by the very self-protection that is each party's sound move.
And no rule can design the gap away. To make honest reporting the best reply, a rule must concede each side an information rent, and on the marginal trade the two rents together exceed the gain, so no rule that pays itself can clear every warranted trade: efficient, voluntary, truthful, self-funding, one must give (Myerson and Satterthwaite).
And the surplus dies with no culprit. The buyer offered low and the seller held high and the broker ran a sound rule, so the lost trade is a harm with no wrongdoer, easily misread as mere stubbornness rather than the rent that private information commands.
And efficiency is reachable only at a cost. An outside party can cover the shortfall and clear every warranted trade, so the Vickrey rule buys efficiency by running a deficit (Vickrey; Clarke; Groves), and an equal ex-ante ownership can restore a balanced efficient rule (Cramton, Gibbons, and Klemperer), but an ordinary trade has neither.
And the whole is held against its own limit. The impossibility needs two-sided private value over overlapping ranges, and lifts under one-sided information, a known value, guaranteed gains over disjoint ranges, or an outside purse (Vickrey; Cramton, Gibbons, and Klemperer), so the trap is real and common and bounded.

## Stakes
