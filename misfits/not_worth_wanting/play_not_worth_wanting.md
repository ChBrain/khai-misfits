# Play: Not Worth Wanting

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Not Worth Wanting.
There is a thing a person wants, a kind of work, a freedom, a life, and it is out of reach, walled off by poverty or custom or plain circumstance. And then the mind does a quiet and merciful thing: it revises the wanting to fit the reaching, so that the good beyond the wall comes to seem not worth wanting after all. It is the oldest story: the fox that cannot reach the grapes decides the grapes are sour. Jon Elster gave the mechanism its name, adaptive preference formation, and marked the thing that makes it a trap and not a simple choice: the adjustment happens behind the person's own back. It is not a considered decision to let the thing go, weighed and made in the open; it is a causal easing of the tension between what one wants and what one can have, done unbidden, so that the wanting is gone before the person ever chose to give it up.
And here is why it is a kindness, in its own coin. To go on wanting what you can never have is to ache without end, and the mind spares you that: stop wanting the thing, and the longing that could never be met simply stops, and you rest content within your walls. Nothing about this is foolish or weak. The person who has made their peace with a smaller life is not lying when they say they are content, and they are not to be pitied as dupes. The wanting-less is real, and the relief it brings is real.
And here is the quiet harm. What changed was not the walls but the wanting, and the walls are still there. So the person now reports, in perfect honesty, that they are content, that they did not want the thing anyway, and every measure of how a life is going that trusts what people say they want, or how satisfied they say they are, reads that report at face value: the narrowed life is scored as a chosen one, the deprivation as wellbeing. Amartya Sen saw it in the hopeless beggar and the subdued wife, long habituated to want little and reporting themselves content, so that a utility measure endorses their want; Martha Nussbaum saw it in the deprived woman whose preferences have been shaped to her cage, and drew the lesson: you cannot measure a life by the satisfaction of preferences that the deprivation itself has shrunk. The constraint becomes invisible exactly to the instruments meant to find it.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. No one plants the smaller want. The mind eases its own tension, unbidden, and the person is neither cheated by another nor failing in courage. The loss is authored by no one. It is the good that goes unmissed because the wanting for it was quietly rewritten, and the misfit is that the kindest adjustment the mind can make is the very one that hides the constraint from the person inside it, and from everyone who would measure their contentment to know whether they are well.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The remedy is to stop asking what people say they want or how satisfied they are, and to ask instead what they are actually able to do and be, the capabilities open to them, since these can be seen from outside and do not shrink with the wanting (Sen; Nussbaum). But it is neither simple nor safe. To look past a person's own sincere account of their contentment is to risk a paternalism that overrides real and freely-held preferences, telling people they are worse off than they feel; and it demands the hard judgement, which no rule makes easy, of telling a deprivation-driven adaptation from an autonomous one, a masked loss from a made peace.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The adjustment is real but hard to measure and easy to misread, and not every lowered want is a hidden deprivation. Much adaptation is a healthy and autonomous making of peace with the possible, the wisdom of not breaking oneself against an immovable wall, and a good life needs it (Khader). The name is Elster's, the welfare critique and the capability turn Sen's, the capabilities account Nussbaum's, and the careful line between the deprivation-driven adaptation and the appropriate one Khader's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where a good is walled off, the wanting for it may quietly shrink to fit, so a deprivation can be felt, and reported, and counted, as contentment, though not every contentment is a deprivation and telling the two apart is the whole of the difficulty.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a good is put out of reach, a person unconsciously revises their wants to fit what they can attain, coming not to want the unattainable, so they report themselves content and are, and any measure that trusts stated preference or satisfaction reads the constrained life as a chosen and happy one, so the deprivation is certified as wellbeing and goes unseen.
It runs on a wanting that shrinks to fit the walls.
A person wants a good, a work, a freedom, a life, and finds it walled off by poverty or custom or circumstance (Elster).
And the mind eases the ache. Behind the person's own back, it revises the wanting to fit the reaching, so the unattainable good comes to seem not worth wanting, the grapes reframed as sour (Elster).
And the contentment is honest. The person now wants less, and says, and means, that they are content and did not want the thing anyway.
And the measure is fooled. Any standard that trusts what people say they want reads the narrowed life as chosen and the deprivation as wellbeing (Sen; Nussbaum).
And it is authored by no one. No one plants the smaller want, the mind eases its own tension unbidden, so the loss is the structure's and not a fraud.
And the way out looks past the wanting. Ask not what people say they want but what they are able to do and be, the capabilities that do not shrink with the want (Sen; Nussbaum).
And the whole is held against its limit. The adjustment is real but hard to read, and not every lowered want is a hidden loss, since much adaptation is a healthy making of peace (Khader).
