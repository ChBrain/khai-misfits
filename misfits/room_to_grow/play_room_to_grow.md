# Play: Room to Grow

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Room to Grow.
A population is held below what its habitat could feed, and it is held there not by any outside hand but by itself. Its own density regulates it: too many mouths at a limited food supply, so the crowd goes hungry and breeds less; too dense a press, so disease moves easily through it; too many adults, so the young are eaten by their elders. These forces bite hardest when the numbers are high, and they are the brake that keeps the population from filling every corner of its range (Ricker; Nicholson).
Then someone culls it, in good faith, to bring the number down. A manager harvests the fish, a control officer poisons the pest, a conservationist removes the invasive, and each does it by the sound and ordinary logic that to kill some is to leave fewer, a logic true of almost every population there is.
But the deaths do not only subtract. They relax the brake. With fewer mouths at the food, the survivors eat better and breed more. With fewer adults, the young those adults would have eaten now live. With the crowd thinned, the disease spreads less. So the density-dependent regulation that had been holding the population down is eased by the very mortality meant to reduce it (Abrams).
And here is where it turns. If that release outweighs the removal, the survivors overcompensate: they produce more than the cull took, and the population rebounds not to where it was but past it, more numerous after the cull than before. It is not a fable but a field result: efforts to control nuisance and invasive species have raised their abundance, the removal falling on a crowded, self-limiting stage and the cull making room the survivors filled and more (Zipkin, Kraft, Cooch and Sullivan).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The one who culls acts in good faith to reduce the population and follows the plain logic that killing some leaves fewer, a logic that holds for nearly every population that is not regulating itself so tightly. And the survivors that eat and breed and live are doing nothing but what any organism does when the pressure eases: they are not scheming and not multiplying on purpose, only responding to a food supply and a safety that the thinning restored. No one intends the rebound, and no single removal is placed to see it, since from where the culler stands each death is simply one fewer. The loss is authored by no one: it is the population's own density-dependence, relaxed by the very mortality meant to reduce it, and the misfit is that a cull can raise the number it was meant to lower.
And here is the way out, and why it is not easily taken. One can beat the backfire by culling hard enough to overwhelm the compensation, or by aiming the mortality away from the self-limiting stage, or by removing the density-dependence itself, the food limit or the crowding, rather than the animals. But each asks a knowledge of how the population regulates itself that is often absent before the fact, and a cull calibrated wrongly raises the very number it meant to cut. The escape works against the grain of the intuition that killing some always leaves fewer.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The hydra effect requires overcompensating density-dependence and mortality that falls on the crowded, self-limiting stage, so it appears under specific conditions and is a documented mechanism there, not a general law that culling raises abundance, since most culls reduce the population as they intend. The synthesis and the name are Abrams's, the foundation of overcompensating density-dependence is Ricker's and Nicholson's, and the field case of control backfiring is Zipkin, Kraft, Cooch and Sullivan's. The house stages the structure and holds the scope in view: that where a population's own regulation is strong enough to overcompensate, a cull can raise it, and not that killing a population always makes more of it.

## Arc

The bend is this: a population held down by its own density-dependence can be raised, not lowered, by killing a fraction of it, since the deaths relax the competition or cannibalism that held the survivors back and they overcompensate, so a cull can backfire through the population's own regulation with no villain and no error.
It runs on a population that holds itself down.
The numbers are held below the ceiling by the population's own density, the competition and the crowding and the cannibal adults (Ricker; Nicholson).
And a cull is made, in good faith, to bring the number down, by the plain logic that to kill some is to leave fewer.
And the deaths make room. The brake relaxed, fewer mouths at the food and fewer adults to eat the young (Abrams).
And the survivors overcompensate. Eating better and breeding more, producing more than the cull removed.
And the population rebounds. Risen past where it began, more numerous for the culling (Zipkin and colleagues).
And the bound is held. The effect needing overcompensating density-dependence and mortality on the crowded stage, not a law that culling always backfires (Abrams).

## Stakes

Whether a population can be reduced by killing a part of it, when the population is held down not by any outside hand but by its own density, which the killing relaxes.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the rebound and away from the reduction, because the population regulates itself, and the cull that removes the animals also removes the crowding that held them back. The structure cannot both cull the population and be sure of lowering it, because the culling is the relaxing: the same deaths that subtract from the number ease the competition and the cannibalism and the crowd-borne disease that had kept the number down, so the harder the population was holding itself back, the more the cull frees it to grow. The way out, culling hard enough to overwhelm the compensation, or aiming the mortality off the self-limiting stage, or removing the density-dependence rather than the animals, asks a knowledge of the population's own regulation that is often absent before the fact, and a cull calibrated wrongly raises the very number it meant to cut. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the culler follows a logic true of almost everything and the survivors do only what any creature does when the pressure eases, and that the rise is the population's own regulation relaxed, authored by no one. And it holds the bound in view, hedged, because the effect requires overcompensating density-dependence and mortality on the crowded stage and is not a general law (Abrams), so the whole of the misfit is that where a population holds itself down hard enough, a cull can give it room to grow.
