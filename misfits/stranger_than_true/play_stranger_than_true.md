# Play: Stranger Than True

## Name

Stranger Than True.
There is an old saying, that a lie can travel halfway around the world before the truth has got its boots on. Soroush Vosoughi, Deb Roy, and Sinan Aral put it to the largest test of its kind, following some one hundred and twenty-six thousand news stories as they spread across a social network over eleven years, each story rated true or false by independent fact-checkers. The saying held. Falsehood diffused significantly farther, faster, deeper, and more broadly than the truth, in every category and most sharply in political news, a false story routinely reaching many times the audience a true one did. And the cause was not what most would guess. It was not bots: when the researchers removed the automated accounts, the gap barely moved, because bots spread true and false alike. It was ordinary people, and the reason was novelty (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
And here is the mechanism, and why it needs no villain. People share what is novel and surprising. To pass on a striking piece of news is a rational and social thing to do: it marks you as informed, someone in the know with something to tell, and it gives the people you tell something new (Berger and Milkman). So the sharing network is a filter, and what it selects for is surprise. Now, a true story is bound by what actually happened, and can be no more surprising than the truth allows. A false story is bound by nothing, and can be made, or happen to be, as surprising as anyone likes. So on average the false is more novel than the true, and novelty is exactly what wins the share. The false out-competes the true at the one contest the network runs, and no one has to intend any of it (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
And here is what that does to the shared picture of the world. Because the false is shared more, it travels farther and reaches its audience sooner, while the true story is still lacing its boots. And the correction, when it comes, comes late, because refuting a surprising falsehood costs far more effort than telling it did, so the truth reaches the people the lie reached long after the lie has done its work (Brandolini). The information a network carries is thus tilted toward the false, not by a liar and not by a machine, but by the sum of ordinary people passing on the surprising.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The sharer is not a liar: they pass on what struck them, as anyone does, and often believe it. The crowd is not gullible: the false story wins because it is more surprising, not because people cannot tell truth from falsehood and not because they prefer to be lied to. And the false story itself need not be a schemer's weapon: an honest rumour, an innocent error, a story too good to be true, is novel too, and travels for the same reason. The loss is authored by no one. It is the false reaching farther than the true because it is stranger, and the misfit is that the very appetite for the novel that makes sharing worthwhile and social is what carries the false ahead of the true.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The tilt comes from the filter selecting for surprise, so the remedies all try to slow the share: a prompt to read the thing or check it before passing it on, fact-checking and prebunking, teaching people to pause on what most delights them to repeat. But none is free or fast. The correction always lags, because the bullshit asymmetry means refuting costs far more than asserting. And friction on sharing fights the grain of the human love of the new, and slows the true along with the false, and costs the reach and speed that a sharing platform lives on. Each brake is real and each is dear, and none catches the lie that is already round the world.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The finding is a tendency, not a law: the false is more likely to spread, not certain to, and much truth spreads too. It is strongest in political news and weaker elsewhere. And novelty is the researchers' best-supported explanation of a robustly measured differential, well evidenced by the novelty and emotion analysis but an inference, not a settled certainty. People are shown to prefer the novel, not the false. The differential spread, the novelty measure, the emotion profile, and the bot control are Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral's; the account of why the surprising is shared is Berger and Milkman's; and the lie-outruns-truth image and the bullshit asymmetry are the old saying's and Brandolini's. The house stages the filter and holds the bound in view: that where people share the news they find surprising, the false that is stranger than the true travels ahead of it, no one at fault, and the only brakes on the lie also slow the truth.

## Arc

The bend is this: where people share the news they find novel and surprising, because novelty carries social and informational value, and false stories, unconstrained by what is true, are on average more novel than true ones, each rational share of the surprising amplifies falsehood over truth, so the public information environment systematically favours the false, with no one intending to deceive and no agent required.
It runs on a network that shares.
People pass on the news they find striking, and the sharing network is a filter that selects for surprise (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
And sharing the novel is rational. To pass on the surprising marks the sharer as informed and gives the receiver something new, so it is sound social behaviour and not idle (Berger and Milkman).
And the false has the edge. Truth is bound by what happened, a falsehood by nothing, so the false can be more surprising than the true and wins the one contest the filter runs (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
And so the false outruns the true. Shared more for being more novel, it reaches farther, faster, and deeper, while the true story is still lacing its boots (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
And it is authored by no one. The sharer is no liar, the crowd no fool, and bots spread both alike, so the tilt is the filter's doing and not a villain's.
And the correction comes late. Refuting the surprising costs far more than telling it, so the truth reaches the lie's audience long after the lie has (Brandolini).
And the whole is held against its limit. The tilt is a tendency, strongest in political news and driven by novelty as the best-supported explanation, the false more likely to spread and not all of it spreading (Vosoughi, Roy, and Aral).
