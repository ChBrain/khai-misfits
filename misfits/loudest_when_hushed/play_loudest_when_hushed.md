# Play: Loudest When Hushed

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Loudest When Hushed.
There is a photograph of your house seen by a handful of people, or a document in a corner of the web, or a post that names you, and you want it gone. So you do the sensible thing: you move to take it down, by a letter, a lawsuit, an injunction, a ban. Removing a thing should leave fewer eyes on it, and that is the whole of your intent. But the removal is not silent. A lawsuit is a matter of record, a takedown leaves a hole where the thing was, a ban is announced, and each of these says plainly to anyone who notices: here is something someone did not want seen. And that is the surest sign a thing is worth seeing. The name is Mike Masnick's, coined in 2005 for what happened when Barbra Streisand sued in 2003 to remove an aerial photograph of her clifftop home from an environmental survey of the California coast. Before the suit the image had been downloaded a handful of times, twice of them by her own lawyers. The suit made it news, and it was seen hundreds of thousands of times. The attempt to hide the photograph is exactly what made it famous.
And here is why the hiding advertises. The move to suppress is itself an act, and a visible one, so it carries a signal: that here is a thing worth the trouble of hiding, which is to say a thing worth seeing (Jansen and Martin). Those who are told they may not have a thing want it the more for being told, the forbidden made desirable, the freedom reasserted by the very act of looking (Brehm). And on a medium where copying costs nothing, the wanting turns at once into mirrors and reposts and screenshots, so that the thing passes beyond any recall the moment the eye is drawn. The suppression does not move the thing elsewhere and it does not breed more of the thing: it amplifies the one thing, in the very place it already was, far past what silence would have done.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The one who suppresses is usually protecting something real and sympathetic, a privacy, a reputation, a secret, and does the locally sensible thing in trying to remove what harms them, as any such interest would. The crowd that spreads the thing is not a mob and not conspiring: it is people reacting to what looks like overreach, or simply drawn to what has suddenly become news. The loss is authored by no one. It is the attention the information draws because it was suppressed, the obscure thing made famous by the attempt to bury it, and the misfit is that the very act meant to hide a thing is the surest sign that it is worth seeing (Brian Martin).
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The backfire runs on the visibility of the suppression, so the way to escape it is to suppress nothing: to say nothing, let the thing lie unremarked, and deny the fight the oxygen that would spread it. But the shrug is not free. It means tolerating the very exposure you wanted gone, leaving the photograph up and the post live, and it works only before the takedown is filed, since once the suit is a matter of record the signal is already sent and cannot be recalled. And where a genuine and urgent harm demands removal, the strategic silence is no option at all.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The backfire is real and documented, in the Streisand case and in Jansen and Martin's study of censorship that rebounds, but it is not automatic. It needs the suppression to be visible, to seem heavy-handed or unjust, and to meet a networked audience able and willing to copy and resist. Quiet, lawful, unremarked, or widely-accepted removal can and often does succeed without any backfire, and much material is taken down every day and stays down. The name is Masnick's, the censorship-backfire analysis Jansen and Martin's, the general backfire model Brian Martin's, and the reactance beneath the defiance Brehm's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where hiding a thing is itself an act that can be seen, the hiding can advertise, and the thing is loudest when hushed.

## Arc

The bend is this: where an actor moves to suppress a piece of information before an audience that can see the move and copy the thing, the attempt is itself visible and signals that the information matters, provokes reactance in those told they cannot have it, and invites cheap copying that puts it beyond recall, so the suppression draws more attention to the information than leaving it alone would, and each rational move to hide a thing spreads it wider, the opposite of the intent.
It runs on a move to hide that cannot be made unseen.
Someone with a real interest to protect moves to remove a thing, by takedown or lawsuit or ban, since removing it should leave fewer to see it (the coinage).
And the removal is itself visible. A suit is on the record, a takedown leaves a hole, a ban is announced, and each says here is a thing someone did not want seen (Jansen and Martin).
And so the hiding signals. That a thing was worth the trouble of suppressing is the surest sign it is worth seeing, and the eye turns toward exactly what the move meant to turn it from.
And those forbidden it want it. Told they may not have a thing, people want it the more and look to reassert the freedom, so the ban becomes a draw (Brehm).
And the copies put it past recall. On a medium where copying is free, the wanting spawns mirrors and reposts and screenshots, and the thing is beyond any taking-back.
And it is authored by no one. The suppressor protects something real, the crowd reacts to overreach or fresh news, and the amplified reach is the structure's doing and not a fraud (Brian Martin).
And the whole is held against its limit. The backfire is not automatic: it needs visible suppression, seen as heavy-handed, before an audience able to copy and resist, so quiet removal need not backfire at all (Jansen and Martin).

## Stakes

Whether you can take a thing down without the taking-down being the very thing that spreads it, when the move to hide is itself an act that everyone can see.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the thing seen more, and away from the quiet the suppressor wanted, because the move to hide is visible and says the thing is worth seeing, those forbidden it want it, and the copies put it beyond recall. The structure cannot both remove the thing and keep the removal unseen, because the removal is itself the signal: the suppression and the amplification are the one visible act read two ways, the attempt to bury and the thing made famous. The way out, to say nothing and let the thing lie unremarked, spends a real cost, since it means tolerating the exposure the suppressor wanted gone, and it is no way out at all once the takedown is filed and the signal already sent, or where a genuine and urgent harm demands removal. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the suppressor protects a real interest and does the sensible thing, that the crowd reacts to overreach or fresh news and does not conspire, and that the amplified reach is the visibility of suppression's doing and not anyone's fraud.
