# Sold to Find the Price: Reference

The research warrant for Sold to Find the Price.
The term is the house's; the play runs on the information account of new issue underpricing, anchored on the
bookbuilding literature and the empirical work on offer prices and allocations, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging new issue underpricing as **information bought with the asset it is meant to value**: a company with
no traded shares has no price, and the price is not concealed anywhere, it is distributed across the
institutions that will be buying, each holding a fragment and none able to see the whole. The only way to
assemble it is to ask them, and asking creates the reason to shade, because the same act that requests a
valuation submits a bid. The play models the documented mechanics, the number that exists in nobody's
possession, the indication that is free to give and costly to give truthfully, the discount that functions as
consideration rather than as loss, the discretionary allocation that carries the consideration across issues,
the uninformed side that rationing sorts into the wrong half of the market, and the first day that prints one
figure for three different things, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of banks
cheating founders.

The governing law is that the currency and the commodity are the same asset. There is no cash with which to
buy the information, no way to value the information except by the price it produces, and no way to make a
payment mean anything inside a single issue, so it is settled in shares and carried forward by the power to
allocate. That is why the cost never appears in any statement of costs: it is not charged, it is deducted from
the price, and it falls on the shares the seller retained.

What removes any possibility of blame from the buy side is that the tax on candour is arithmetic. An investor
who names a higher limit has, in the act of naming it, raised the price they will pay, so the truthful answer
is the expensive one and the shaded answer is free, and this holds for a scrupulous holder exactly as it holds
for a cynical one. No wording of the question separates the valuer from the bidder, because they are the same
person.

What removes it from the seller is that the alternative is not a lower cost but an unknown error. A firm's
estimate of its own worth is the one number in the room with no market behind it, and setting the offer at the
level the book implies simply removes the reason anybody answered the book. The choice is not between paying
and not paying: it is between a known deduction and a guess.

And the uninformed close the argument, because even with no shading at all the discount cannot go to zero.
Rationing bites hardest where demand was strongest, so an investor without a view holds least of what turned
out well and all of what turned out badly, and their average is worse than the average issue through the
allocation rather than through any error of their own. An offering that stops compensating them stops being a
public offering.

It is distinct from the house's **The Price Neither Will Name**, where a buyer and a seller both stand to gain
but each conceals their price and the deal dies in the gap: there private information defeats the trade, and
the outcome is that nothing happens. Here the trade happens, every time, and the private information is not
defeated but purchased, out of the asset being traded, so the cost shows up as a completed sale at the wrong
number rather than as a sale that never took place.

It is distinct from the house's **The Winning Bid**, where the bidder who most overestimates a common value
wins and has thereby overpaid: there the curse falls on the winner through their own optimism, and the seller
is the beneficiary. Here the curse falls on the party who did not bid aggressively at all, delivered by
rationing rather than by enthusiasm, and it is anticipated and paid for by the seller in advance, which is the
opposite direction of both the injury and the money.

It is distinct from the house's **Ask the One Who Sells**, where the expert who tells you what you need is
paid to supply it, so you are sold more than an informed buyer would take: there the conflict is the
mechanism, and an honest adviser dissolves it. Here a perfectly disinterested bank still has to discount,
because the discount is the price of the information rather than a margin on a service: the conflict exists,
it is documented, and it rides on top of a cost that would be there without it.

It is distinct from the house's **Too Dear to Fake**, where a hidden worth can be proved only by a signal
expensive enough that a pretender would not send it, so each holder burns real resource to be believed: there
the party who knows pays, and the payment goes to nobody. Here the payment runs the other way entirely, from
the party who does not know to the party who does, as the consideration for disclosing, and it is received
rather than burned.

It is distinct from the house's **Nobody Reads It**, where the terms are available to anybody and reading them
costs more than it saves, so too few read them to police them: there the information exists, in a document, and
the failure is one of attention. Here there is nothing to read at any price, because the number is not in the
prospectus, not in the accounts and not in the seller's possession: it does not exist in written form anywhere
until the day after it was needed.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the banks as
  thieves or the investors as liars, because nobody in the sequence has a decision to get right: the seller
  cannot price its own shares from the inside and cannot set the offer at the book's own level without
  emptying the book; the informed cannot answer truthfully for free, since naming a higher limit raises what
  they pay, and an indication is non-binding, so shading is not a lie; the uninformed are sorted into the
  wrong half of the market by a rationing they cannot see and cannot correct without the view they lack; and
  the underwriter cannot compensate anybody without discretion, which is the same discretion that makes
  favour payable. The cost is authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that the
  only route to the price runs through the people who will pay it, and they will only walk it for a fee drawn
  on the thing being priced.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. An auction removes the
  underwriter's discretion and removes with it the instrument that pays for information, which is why
  bookbuilding displaced auctions in most markets rather than the other way round. Publishing the allocations
  makes the rent visible and makes the payment visible too, since the ledger cannot distinguish them.
  Regulating the discount downward reaches the consideration and the capture in one stroke, because they are
  the same number on the same screen. Instructing investors to bid honestly reaches nothing, since the tax on
  honesty is arithmetic rather than a habit.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the models, the samples, the estimates and the
  disputes are theirs. The bounds are carried with them: underpricing has several live and competing
  explanations, including signalling, litigation risk, information cascades, prospect theory and the
  dispersion of ownership, and the information account staged here is one of them rather than the settled
  answer; the magnitude has moved far more than a cost-of-information story requires, from about 7 per cent
  in the 1980s to 65 per cent in 1999 to 2000, which is itself evidence that something outside the mechanism
  is also at work; the allocation and commission findings are agency behaviour the information account does
  not need and cannot excuse, and they are reported as correlations in samples rather than as findings
  against any named firm; bookbuilding is a widespread practice and not the only one, since auctions and
  fixed-price offerings exist and are used, so the misfit is a property of how new issues are commonly sold
  rather than of selling shares as such; and the models cited are stylised, with results that follow from
  their assumptions. **The play makes no claim that any particular issue was priced correctly or
  incorrectly**, or that the discount observed in any case was the amount the mechanism required, only that
  a price which can be found solely by asking the buyers cannot be found for nothing.
