# Play: Over a Barrel

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Over a Barrel.
The most valuable deals are often the most particular. A supplier builds a plant right beside one buyer's factory, so the parts travel a hundred yards instead of a thousand miles. A maker tools a line to one customer's exact specification. A worker learns a craft useful only inside one firm. Each of these is worth far more in this one relationship than in any other, and that is precisely why they create so much value: the fit is tight, the waste is gone. Before the investment is made, everything is fair. There are other buyers the supplier could serve, other suppliers the buyer could use, and that competition holds both sides honest: the terms are set by what the alternatives would offer, and neither party can ask for more than the market allows.
And here is what the investment quietly does. The moment the plant is built beside that one factory, or the line tooled to that one spec, its value everywhere else collapses. The plant a hundred yards from the buyer is worth a fortune to that buyer and almost nothing to anyone else; sold or repurposed, it would fetch only its scrap. So the supplier now holds an asset whose worth is almost entirely inside this one relationship, and the difference between that inside worth and the little it would fetch outside is a surplus sitting on the table, a quasi-rent, that the buyer can now reach for. Because the supplier has nowhere else nearly as good to go, the buyer can reopen the terms, threaten to walk unless the price is cut, and the supplier, facing the loss of nearly everything if the deal collapses, must accept almost any split rather than lose it all.
And here is the turn that makes it a trap and not just a hard bargain. None of this is a breach and none of it is hidden. No contract could have named every future contingency, so reopening the terms when circumstances shift is within the rules, not a broken promise, and the buyer pressing the advantage the sunk asset created is taking a gain the situation offers, not committing a fraud. The competition that would have disciplined the price is simply gone, converted by the act of investing from a market of many into a standoff of two, and the party who created the most value by fitting itself so tightly to the relationship is exactly the party the fit has left exposed.
And here is what a party that can foresee all this does, and why the cost is so quiet. It does not build the plant beside the factory. It builds a general plant it could sell to anyone, worth less in the relationship but safe from the squeeze, or it invests less, or it does not invest at all. The most valuable version of the deal, the one whose specificity would have created the most, is never made, not because anyone acted in bad faith but because everyone acted in good sense: the buyer would squeeze, so the supplier does not sink, so the surplus that the specific investment would have created is foregone before it can ever be fought over. The loss is authored by no one, and it never appears, because the thing that would have caused it was never built.
And the fixes cost what the trap costs. Bring both parties under one owner, and there is no separate party left to hold up, but the sharp incentives of the market are replaced by the dulled ones of the firm and its bureaucracy (Williamson). Write a contract long enough to foreclose the squeeze, and it is dear to draft and still cannot foresee everything (Grossman and Hart). Post a hostage, build a reputation, and each raises the cost of the squeeze without abolishing the exposure, which is inherent in specificity itself. The mechanism, the appropriable quasi-rent on a relationship-specific asset, is Klein, Crawford, and Alchian's; the fundamental transformation from competition to bilateral monopoly is Williamson's; and the underinvestment it forces is Grossman and Hart's. The vivid old case, Fisher Body and General Motors, is contested in the literature, so this play carries the mechanism and holds that one example lightly.

## Arc

The bend is this: once a relationship-specific investment is sunk, its value inside the relationship far exceeds its value outside, and that difference, the quasi-rent, can be appropriated by the counterparty through ex-post renegotiation, so each party, foreseeing the hold-up, rationally invests less in the specific asset that would have created the most value, and the largest gains go unmade with no contract breached and no one deceived.
It runs on an investment that fits one relationship.
The most valuable deals need an asset worth far more inside one relationship than anywhere else, a plant beside one factory, a tool cut to one spec, a skill useful to one firm, and that tight fit is exactly what creates the value (Klein, Crawford, and Alchian).
And it turns on what the fit costs. The moment the specific asset is sunk, its worth everywhere else collapses to its salvage value, so the difference, the quasi-rent, sits exposed, and the party that made the investment has no comparable outside option left (Klein, Crawford, and Alchian).
And the competition that held terms honest is gone. The sinking converts a market of many possible partners into a standoff of two, the fundamental transformation from ex-ante competition to ex-post bilateral monopoly, so the counterparty can reopen the terms and threaten to walk (Williamson).
And the squeeze is within the rules. No contract could name every contingency, so reopening the terms is not a breach and pressing the advantage the sunk asset created is not a fraud, the surplus taken openly and the investor accepting almost any split rather than lose it all (Klein, Crawford, and Alchian).
And so the specific investment is never made. Foreseeing the hold-up, the investor builds general rather than specific, or builds less, or not at all, so the most valuable version of the deal is foregone before it can be fought over, the largest gains unmade (Grossman and Hart).
And the whole is authored by no one. The buyer that would squeeze breaks no promise, the supplier that will not sink shows no failure of nerve, and the loss never appears because the thing that would have caused it was never built.
Bring both under one owner, or bind them, and the hold-up goes at a price. Vertical integration removes the party who could hold up but replaces the market's sharp incentives with the firm's dulled ones and its bureaucracy, a long contract is dear and still cannot foresee everything, and ownership protects one party's incentive to invest only by weakening the other's (Williamson; Grossman and Hart): so the exposure is inherent in specificity, and every guard against it trades one cost for another, which is why the most valuable deal is so often the one not done.

## Stakes

Whether the most valuable version of a deal can be done at all, when the specificity that creates its value is the same specificity that exposes whoever invests in it.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the tight fit that creates the most value, and away from making it, because the tighter the fit the deeper the exposure. The production cannot make the specific investment safe while keeping it specific, because being worth far more inside the relationship than out is exactly what strips the investor's outside option and hands the counterparty the quasi-rent: the value and the vulnerability are the same fit measured two ways. The one solvent is to remove the separate party who could hold up, by ownership, or to bind them by contract or hostage, and each trades the sharp incentives of the market for the dulled ones of the firm, or buys one party's security with the other's. It can only show that no one is a crook and no one is a fool, that the buyer who would squeeze breaks no promise and the supplier who will not sink shows no failure of nerve, and that the deal which would have created the most value, the plant beside the factory, the tool cut to spec, is precisely the deal the exposure discourages, so the largest gains go unmade before anyone could fight over them, and the loss never appears because the thing that would have caused it was never built.
