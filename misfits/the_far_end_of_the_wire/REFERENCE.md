# The Far End of the Wire: Reference

The research warrant for The Far End of the Wire.
The term is the house's; the play runs on voltage rise from reverse power flow in low-voltage
distribution networks, the hosting-capacity limit set out in the power-systems literature, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging voltage rise under reverse flow as **a relationship that inverts its sign while everything holding
it stays correct**: a distribution feeder is engineered for power travelling one way, from the substation
outward, and the voltage drop along its length is compensated by setting the supply end deliberately high
so the far end still arrives inside limits. When enough customers export at once the current reverses, the
same relationship that lowered voltage toward the far end now raises it, and the compensation that was
protecting the far end becomes the pressure that pushes it over the ceiling. The play models the documented
mechanics, the feeder built correctly for the flow that existed, the installations that are each legal and
correctly sized, the reversal that requires nothing but simultaneity, the setting that turns from
protection into pressure without being touched, the protective trip that curtails the output it was fitted
to safeguard, and the corrections that each charge somebody, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure,
not as a tale of an obsolete network or a greedy exporter.

The governing law is that voltage along a line is a function of current and the line's own impedance, and
that function does not know which way the current is going. Reverse the flow and the sign reverses with it.
Nothing has to break, degrade or be neglected: the same arithmetic that made the network work in one
direction produces the fault in the other, so there is no component anywhere that could be inspected and
found wanting.

What makes it structural rather than an oversight is where the cost falls. Voltage rise is greatest at the
electrical end of the feeder, so the household that curtails most is the one furthest from the substation,
and its position on the wire is not a choice it made, a rule it broke or a thing it can change. Inverters
first reduce output as voltage climbs and disconnect if it stays above the permissible limit, which is the
protection working exactly as specified. The generation lost is legal generation, from correctly sized
equipment, shut off by its own safety device.

The threshold is measured rather than argued. Effects begin to appear on ordinary feeders at low penetration
and become significant power-quality problems above roughly a third of customers exporting, which is the
hosting-capacity limit: a number that belongs to the wire, that no participant can see, and that nobody is
told when they apply.

It is distinct from the house's **Right for the Last World**, where a form fitted to its environment is
rewarded, hardens into how things are done, and executes on after the environment has turned: there the
structure is obsolete and its continued correct execution is the harm. Here nothing has hardened and
nothing is obsolete. The feeder is not doing the wrong thing, it is doing exactly what a conductor does,
and the physics of the correct operation is what produces the overvoltage.

It is distinct from the house's **The Commons**, where a shared stock is drawn down because the cost of one
more taking is split among all: here nothing is consumed and no stock exists. The limit is a fixed
electrical ceiling rather than a depleting resource, and the cost does not fall on everyone in shares, it
falls on whoever sits at the end of the wire.

It is distinct from the house's **Onto the Survivors**, where a failed member's load falls on the rest until
the sharing ruptures: there something fails and its burden is redistributed. Here nothing fails, every unit
goes on working correctly, and what accumulates is a voltage rather than a load handed over.

It is distinct from the house's **Best Day for It**, where a correlated fleet suppresses the price in the
very hours it earns: there the loss is revenue, set in a market, and it falls on the whole fleet alike.
Here the loss is energy, set by physics on one particular wire, and it falls on one household by position.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the network
  engineer as short-sighted or the exporting household as a free rider, because nobody in the sequence has a
  choice to get wrong: the feeder was correctly engineered for the only flow that existed when it was built,
  the deliberately high supply-end setting is the standard and correct way to keep the far end inside limits
  under load, each installation is legal, correctly sized and separately harmless, and the inverter that
  shuts down is a safety device performing exactly to specification. The failure is authored by nobody and
  emerges from the structure, a relationship that does not know which way the current is going, so the
  misfit is that the compensation protecting the far end is the same quantity that later pushes it over.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Reconductoring the feeder
  or fitting new regulators works and is paid by every customer on the tariff, including those with no
  panels and no export. Volt-var and droop-based curtailment work and are paid in output by the owners
  themselves, and sharing the burden across the feeder raises the total energy curtailed rather than
  lowering it, so the fairer distribution is the more wasteful one. Capping export on a saturated feeder
  works and is paid entirely by whoever applies last, refusing a lawful upgrade to a household whose only
  fault is its position in a queue nobody published.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the electrical relationship, the hosting-capacity
  studies, the measured thresholds and the curtailment schemes are theirs. The bounds are carried with
  them: hosting capacity varies enormously with conductor size, feeder length, transformer rating, load
  profile and local standards, so the thresholds quoted are typical findings rather than constants, and
  modern smart-inverter standards materially change the picture on networks that have adopted them. **The
  play makes no claim that rooftop generation should not be connected**, only that a wire built for one
  direction carries a limit that belongs to nobody on it.
