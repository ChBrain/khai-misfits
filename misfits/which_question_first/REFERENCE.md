# Which Question First: Reference

The research warrant for Which Question First.
The term is the house's; the play runs on judgment aggregation, and on what happens when a majority is decisive on
every question separately and the answers it produces do not hang together, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging the collegial panel as **a group of consistent people who cannot be consistent together**: three judges, two
questions, and a rule joining them that nobody disputes; each judge answers coherently and derives the verdict their
answers require; and the case is then decided by whether the panel counts the questions or counts the verdicts. The
play models the documented mechanics, the agreed connective that is never examined because it is agreed, the two
genuine majorities pointing opposite ways, the route chosen by practice rather than by argument, the individual
coherence that makes the contradiction inescapable rather than correctable, and the published judgment that records
the outcome and not the route, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a muddled judge or a
badly run court.

The governing law is that majorities taken proposition by proposition need not produce a consistent set of
propositions, so where the propositions are logically connected the verdict is settled by the order they are put
rather than by anything anybody voted for.

**Nobody in the sequence falls short, and that is what puts the failure outside anybody's conduct.** Each judge
answers on the evidence and derives the verdict correctly. The rule is agreed. Both counts are arithmetically right.
The chair orders the questions properly. The judgment is honestly reasoned.

**And the arithmetic can be checked rather than believed, which the play does in the open.** The first judge finds a
contract and no breach. The second finds a breach and no contract. The third finds both. On the contract question the
majority is two to one **yes**; on the breach question, two to one **yes**; so the agreed rule gives **liable**. Ask
the same three for verdicts and it is not liable, not liable, liable: the defendant wins **two to one**. **Both are
genuine majorities and neither is a trick**, from the same three coherent judges, the same agreed law and the same
evidence.

**Then the agreement is the cause, which is the hardest thing in the play.** Without a logical link between the
questions, majorities on each compose into nothing and there is no difficulty. It is precisely because all three
accept that liability requires a contract **and** a breach that a set of majority-backed answers can be inconsistent.
**The one thing nobody disputes is what makes agreement impossible**, and being uncontested is exactly why nobody
examines it when the contradiction appears.

**And there is no error to find.** Every individual position is consistent, the rule is correctly applied in both
directions, and both counts are right. So the contradiction sits in the aggregation rather than in any participant,
and no improvement in anybody's reasoning, preparation or care touches it. That is what makes this an impossibility
result rather than an account of a bad panel, and the general form has been proved: no aggregation rule can satisfy a
short list of reasonable conditions and always return a consistent set of judgments.

**Then the decision happens where it does not look like one.** Which count governs is a matter of practice, of who
presides and of how the conference is run, and it is not usually put as a question at all. It looks like scheduling.
It is discharged properly by somebody not aware of deciding anything substantive. And **it is invisible in exactly
the cases where it is decisive**, being indistinguishable from the many occasions on which the two counts agree and
it decided nothing.

**And the record does not carry it.** The published judgment gives an outcome and reasons, which is what a judgment
is for, and nothing about whether the panel tallied issues or verdicts, because the aggregation rule a panel used to
combine itself is not conventionally part of one. Nothing is concealed on purpose. But review addresses reasoning and
outcome, and a panel's internal arithmetic is neither, so **the cause of the loss is not merely unrecorded but
unreviewable**, and the losing party reads an unimpeachable document and cannot name what beat it.

It is distinct from the house's **Whoever Asks Last**, which is the nearest incumbent and stages the Condorcet
paradox, where majority preferences over three or more options cycle and whoever controls the agenda controls the
result. Remove that engine entirely, by stipulating that every individual has transitive preferences **and** that the
majority relation is transitive too, with no cycle available anywhere, and this misfit stands whole: **there is no
cycle here and there are only two options.** The panel is deciding one binary question, the majority is decisive on
every proposition, and what fails is not transitivity but consistency: the set of propositions a majority supports
cannot all be true together. The two are cousins in one literature and they break different things, so the agenda
control that exploits a cycle is not what is happening when a chair orders two questions neither of which cycles.

It is distinct from the house's **Too Sure to Be True**, which stages the Condorcet jury theorem, where a majority of
independent voters each slightly better than chance becomes almost certainly right as the group grows. Remove that
engine, by stipulating nothing about anybody's accuracy at all, that no judge is more or less likely to be correct
and that there is no fact of the matter to be right about, and this stands entire, because **it is a question of
consistency and not of accuracy.** That play asks whether the majority gets the answer right; this one asks whether
the majority's answers can be held together, and the contradiction here would arise among three infallible judges
disagreeing only because they saw different evidence.

It is distinct from the house's **The Bigger Picture**, which stages Simpson's paradox, where an association within
every subgroup reverses once the subgroups are combined. Remove that engine, by stipulating no subgroups, no
confounding variable and no rates to pool, and this stands, because **nothing here is being pooled or averaged.**
There the aggregation misleads about a quantity; here it produces a set of answers that cannot all be true at once,
and the objects being combined are judgments on logically connected propositions rather than measurements on
partitioned data.

It is distinct from the house's **The Crowded Middle**, which stages Hotelling's law, where competitors converge on
the position that cannot be beaten and variety is lost. Remove that engine, by stipulating no competitors, no
positions to take and nothing to capture, and this stands, since **nobody here is positioning against anybody.** The
three judges are not choosing where to stand relative to each other, and the outcome is not a convergence but a
contradiction that neither route can avoid.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast any judge as muddled or
  the chair as manipulative, because nobody in the sequence has a decision to get wrong: each judge answers on the
  evidence, applies a rule nobody disputes to their own findings, and reaches the verdict those findings require, with
  no gap anywhere and no inconsistency to correct; the rule is settled and correctly applied in both directions; both
  counts are arithmetically right and neither conceals anything; the chair orders the questions, which is what
  presiding consists of, and does so without deciding anything they would recognise as substantive, since the two
  counts agree in almost every case; and the judgment is honestly reasoned and complete by every existing convention.
  The failure is authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that a set of propositions each
  carried by a majority need not hang together.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Counting issues lets a panel hand
  down a verdict a majority of its own members would each have voted against. Counting verdicts lets a panel announce
  a result contradicting the reasons a majority of it holds, leaving the next case a result and no usable rule.
  Requiring unanimity leaves most panels deciding nothing. Fixing one route by rule does not dissolve the choice but
  makes it once for every future case of the kind before any of them is heard, still without anybody voting on it.
  And recording the route in the judgment would make it visible without making it wrong, since neither count is an
  error.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the doctrinal paradox is Kornhauser and Sager's, the discursive dilemma and the
  impossibility result are List and Pettit's, and the insistence that the two are distinct problems is Mongin's. The
  bounds are carried with them: **whether courts should decide issue by issue or case by case is genuinely
  contested**, and the play carries that argument rather than settling it; the worked example is the canonical
  three-judge, two-premise case and is offered as the shape of the difficulty rather than as a report of any
  proceeding; the general impossibility holds under stated conditions and the play does not claim the theorem for the
  court case, which is why the distinction is cited; the account treats a legal test as a clean conjunction where real
  doctrine is messier, and treats a panel as three where panels vary, which are its own simplifications; and the
  mechanism is stated for propositions that are logically connected, which is not every set of questions a group
  decides. **The play makes no claim about any court, any panel, any judge or any case, is not a description of any
  real proceeding, and gives no legal advice of any kind**, only that majorities taken one question at a time need not
  produce answers that hang together, and that where they do not, the order of the questions is the judgment.
