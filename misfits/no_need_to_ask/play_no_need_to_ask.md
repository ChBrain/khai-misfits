# Play: No Need to Ask

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

No Need to Ask.
A country strikes it rich. Under the ground is oil, or gas, or a great seam of ore, and the state sells it abroad, and a river of money runs into the treasury. This looks like nothing but good fortune, and often it means well with the money, spending it on the people, on subsidy and public jobs and roads and schools. But there is a strange fact under the good fortune, and it is the whole of the matter: the money reached the treasury without passing through the citizens' pockets. The ordinary state has no such river. It must tax to live, and to tax it must bargain with those it taxes, tell them what it takes and why, and submit to being checked by them, so that all through history the power to tax and the power to hold power to account grew up in the same struggle, no taxation without representation. The rentier state is loosed from that struggle. It need not ask its citizens for money, and a state that need not ask need not answer. Hossein Mahdavy named it the rentier state, the state that lives on the rent it receives rather than the revenue it extracts.
And here is how it plays out, and why each step is reasonable. The state, with the rents in hand, does the sensible thing: it distributes rather than extracts, spending on the handout that buys quiet, the subsidy, the public post, the patronage, rather than on the slow and thankless institutions, the capable bureaucracy, the honest court, the legislature that holds the purse, that a taxing state is forced to build because it must answer to the taxed (Karl). And the citizen, untaxed and given the handout, does the sensible thing in turn: they press no hard claim to hold the rulers to account, because the lever ordinary publics hold, the withholding of the revenue the state depends on, is not theirs to pull, the state depending on the ground and not on them (Beblawi and Luciani). Each move is rational on its own, and the sum is a state grown unanswerable, its checks withered for want of the pressure that builds them, its repression affordable because it need not tax (Ross).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The temptation is to read it as the tale of a greedy despot, but the misfit is deeper and worse than that, because the mechanism bites a government of ordinary good will as surely as a rapacious one: it is the funding that severs the link and not the character of the one it funds, so even a ruler who means only well finds patronage cheaper than institutions and finds no public pressing them to build the checks. And the citizen who takes the subsidy and asks nothing is no fool, but is answering the incentives before them, trading a voice they cannot easily raise for a benefit in hand. The loss is authored by no one. It is the accountability that never grew, because the pressure that grows it, the state's need of its citizens' money, was never there, and the misfit is that the very wealth that should have freed a people leaves the power over them less answerable than poverty would have.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The link is severed by the funding, so the way to hold it open is to refuse the ease the rents offer: to build or keep the accountable institutions before the windfall lands, to tax the citizens even when the rents make taxing needless, to bind the rents in a transparent fund and publish the accounts, so that the state answers to its people by design where it need not by necessity. But this asks a state to lay on itself the very discipline the rents were meant to spare it, it leans on a prior strength most petro-states did not have, and the pull to spend the rents on quiet rather than on checks returns with every boom.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The resource curse is real in the record but it is contested and conditional, not a law. It turns on the institutions the windfall meets: producer-friendly institutions make the resource a blessing and grabber-friendly ones make it a curse, so a state that entered the boom already accountable has escaped it, Norway and Botswana the standing counterexamples (Mehlum, Moene and Torvik). And the very link between resource wealth and unaccountable rule has been challenged by work on long runs of within-country data that finds little support for it (Haber and Menaldo). The name is Mahdavy's, the rentier-state theory Beblawi and Luciani's, the fiscal sociology of the petro-state Karl's, and the evidence and its channels Ross's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where a state can live on what it sells rather than on what it taxes, it has no need to ask its people, and so no need to answer them.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a state is funded by resource rents rather than by taxing its citizens, it need not seek their consent to raise revenue, so the fiscal bargain that ties taxation to representation and forces accountable institutions is severed, and each party acts rationally, the ruler spending rents on patronage and subsidy rather than on the institutions that would constrain him and the citizen taking the subsidy and pressing no claim to account, so accountability and the institutions that check power atrophy and the windfall leaves governance weaker than no windfall would have.
It runs on a treasury filled from the ground and not from the people.
A great flow of resource money reaches the state without passing through its citizens' pockets, revenue it need ask no one for (Mahdavy).
And a state that need not ask need not answer. The bargain that ties taxation to accountability is cut, because the lever of the taxed, the granting or withholding of revenue, is not in play (Beblawi and Luciani).
And so the state distributes rather than builds. It spends the rents on the handout that buys quiet, subsidy and patronage, rather than on the slow institutions a taxing state must build (Karl).
And the citizen, untaxed, presses no claim. Given the subsidy and holding no lever of revenue to withhold, they ask nothing of the power over them, and rationally so.
And the checks wither. The institutions that hold power to account atrophy for want of the pressure that builds them, and repression stays affordable to a state that need not tax (Ross).
And it is authored by no one. The mechanism bites a government of good will, since the funding and not the character severs the link, so the loss is the structure's and not a fraud.
And the whole is held against its limit. The curse is contested and conditional on prior institutions, so a state already accountable escapes it (Mehlum, Moene and Torvik; Haber and Menaldo).

## Stakes

Whether a people can hold power to account when the power is not paid for by them, given that the money that funds the state comes from the ground and not from their pockets.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the state loosed from its citizens, and away from the accountability that only their withheld revenue could compel, because the rents fill the treasury without a tax and a state that need not ask need not answer. The structure cannot both free the state with the windfall and keep it answerable to its people, because the freeing IS the loosening: the wealth that lifts the state above the need to tax is the same stroke that lifts it above the need to account, the good fortune and the unaccountability the one severed link seen twice. The way out, to tax the people anyway and build the checks before the windfall lands, spends a real cost, since it asks the state to lay on itself the discipline the rents were meant to spare it, and it leans on a prior strength most did not have.
