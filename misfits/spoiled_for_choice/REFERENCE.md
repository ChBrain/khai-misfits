# Spoiled for Choice: Reference

The research warrant for **Spoiled for Choice**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon named the
**paradox of choice**, or choice overload: past a point more options do not free the chooser but
overwhelm, so a larger assortment lowers the odds of choosing at all and the satisfaction of the
choice made, each option added in good faith to serve a varied taste summing to paralysis and
regret, all cited below, held with the strong finding that the effect is sharply contested, a large
meta-analysis finding no reliable average and later work locating it only under specific conditions.

## Line of Work

Staging the paradox of choice as a **too-many-options-paralyse structure**: a chooser stands before a set of options, and
each option is there for a reason. Tastes vary, and to serve a varied taste one offers more kinds, more flavours, more
funds, more plans, since a larger range means a better chance that somewhere in it lies each person's perfect fit. By the
ordinary logic of freedom, more choice can only help, because a chooser who did not want the extra options could simply
ignore them. But past a point the abundance turns on the one it was meant to serve. The burden of comparing rises with the
number of options, each to be weighed against all the others, and the range raises the expectation that a perfect fit must
be in there to be found, so the chooser, unable to compare them all and unwilling to settle while a better one might yet be
unseen, defers or declines to choose at all: in Iyengar and Lepper's study a display of twenty-four jams drew more lookers
but far fewer buyers than a display of six, the larger choice demotivating rather than freeing (Iyengar and Lepper). And
for the chooser who does choose, the abundance shadows the choice made: with so many forgone alternatives, each a road not
taken, the chosen option is measured against all it might have been, expectations raised too high to meet, and any
shortfall read as the chooser's own fault, since with so much on offer an unhappy outcome must be a failure to have chosen
well, so satisfaction falls and regret and self-blame rise, the more offered the less enjoyed (Schwartz). It is authored by
no villain: each option is added in good faith to fit some real taste, the one who offers the larger range means only to
serve more people better, and the chooser who freezes or regrets is not weak or ungrateful but meeting a real cognitive
burden that grows with the count of options. No one intends the paralysis. The governing law is not that anyone offers too
much out of malice, but that **past a point more options do not free the chooser but overwhelm, so a larger assortment
lowers the odds of choosing at all and the satisfaction of the choice made, each option added in good faith to serve a
varied taste summing to paralysis and regret, no villain required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the varied
taste that makes the abundance reasonable, the large assortment offered to serve it, the rising burden of comparison, the
freeze of the chooser who defers or declines, the regret that shadows the choice made, and the curated few that ease the
burden (Iyengar and Lepper; Schwartz), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a chooser too feeble
to want freedom.

It is a too-many-options-paralyse structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from The Anticommons, where too many owners each holding a veto leave a resource unused, the
tragedy of fragmented control: there the many are owners who each can block, and the harm is a resource gridlocked by
scattered rights, whereas here the many are options before a single chooser, and the harm is the chooser's own paralysis
and regret, no resource blocked and no veto held. It is distinct from Not Worth Wanting, the adaptive preference in which
the wanting itself is quietly revised to fit what is available, sour grapes: there the desire is what changes, bent to the
constraint, whereas here the desire is intact and it is the choosing that is overwhelmed, the person still wanting the good
thing but unable to pick it from the crowd of options. And it is distinct from The Tyranny of Small Decisions, where many
small choices each sound in itself sum over time to an aggregate outcome no one would have chosen whole: there the harm is
the summed consequence of many separate decisions across time, whereas here the harm is the overwhelm of a single decision
faced among too many options at once, a paralysis in the moment of choosing and not a drift across a sequence of choices.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the abundance as a trick or
  the one who offers it as a manipulator, because the source finding it stages is that the trap needs no ill intent: each
  option is added in good faith to fit some real taste, the one who offers the larger range means only to serve more people
  better, following the sound logic that a wider range gives each person a better chance of a perfect fit, and the chooser
  who freezes or regrets is not weak or ungrateful but meeting a real burden of comparison that grows with the count of
  options. No one intends the paralysis. The loss is authored by no one: it is the chooser overwhelmed by an abundance meant
  to serve it, and the misfit is that more options, each a courtesy, can sum to a chooser who chooses less and enjoys it
  less.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. There is a way to ease the burden, but it
  spends the very freedom the abundance offered: to curate, to cut the assortment to a few well-chosen options, to set a
  sensible default, or for the chooser to satisfice, to take the first option that is good enough rather than search for the
  best (Schwartz). But curation takes the choice of what to omit out of the chooser's hands and risks omitting the one they
  would have wanted, a default steers them, and satisficing asks them to give up the search for the perfect fit that the
  abundance promised. The escape works against the grain of the intuition that more freedom is always better, and none of it
  restores both the full range and the ease of choosing.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the demonstration is Iyengar and Lepper's, the popular synthesis and the
  maximizer-satisficer distinction Schwartz's, and the sharp dissent and the conditional reconciliation the meta-analysts'.
  The mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding, and this warrant hedges hard, because the effect is genuinely
  contested: a large meta-analysis found no reliable average effect of choice overload across some fifty experiments
  (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd), and a later synthesis found it real but only under specific conditions of choice-set
  complexity, task difficulty, preference uncertainty, and decision goal (Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman), so the strong
  more-is-less claim is overstated and the effect is conditional rather than general. The scope is held to choice overload
  under those conditions where it appears, the paralysis and lowered satisfaction of a chooser facing too many options at
  once, and not stretched into a claim that choice is generally bad or that less is always more.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Varied Taste**: carries the setting, the real diversity of preferences that makes offering many options reasonable,
  the good reason a wider range is added, since somewhere in it may lie each person's perfect fit.
- **The Abundance**: carries the phenomenon, the large assortment, each option put there in good faith to serve some taste,
  the wide range that by the ordinary logic of freedom can only help.
- **The Weighing**: carries the mechanism, the rising burden of comparing many options each against all the others and the
  expectation the range raises that a perfect fit must be found, the cognitive cost that grows with the count.
- **The Freeze**: carries the paralysis (Iyengar and Lepper), the chooser who defers or declines to choose at all as the
  options mount, the larger display drawing more lookers but fewer who buy, choice turned demotivating.
- **The Regret**: carries the harm, the chosen option shadowed by all the forgone alternatives, expectations raised too high
  to meet and any shortfall read as the chooser's own fault, so satisfaction falls and self-blame rises, the more offered
  the less enjoyed, drawn distinct from The Anticommons, Not Worth Wanting, and The Tyranny of Small Decisions.
- **The Curated Few**: carries the remedy and the bound (Schwartz; the meta-analysts), the curation or default or satisficing
  that eases the burden by cutting the assortment or accepting the good enough, costly because it spends the freedom the
  abundance offered, and the marker of the deep hedge, since the effect is contested and conditional, absent on average and
  real only under specific conditions.
