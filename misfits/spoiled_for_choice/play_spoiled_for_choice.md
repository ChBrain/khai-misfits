# Play: Spoiled for Choice

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Spoiled for Choice.
A chooser stands before a set of options, and each option is there for a reason. Tastes vary, and to serve a varied taste one offers more kinds, more flavours, more funds, more plans, since a larger range means a better chance that somewhere in it lies each person's perfect fit. By the ordinary logic of freedom, more choice can only help, because a chooser who did not want the extra options could simply ignore them.
But past a point the abundance turns on the one it was meant to serve. The burden of comparing rises with the number of options, each to be weighed against all the others, and the range raises the expectation that a perfect fit must be in there to be found. So the chooser, unable to compare them all and unwilling to settle while a better one might yet be unseen, defers or declines to choose at all: in Iyengar and Lepper's study a display of twenty-four jams drew more lookers but far fewer buyers than a display of six, the larger choice demotivating rather than freeing (Iyengar and Lepper).
And for the chooser who does choose, the abundance shadows the choice made. With so many forgone alternatives, each a road not taken, the chosen option is measured against all it might have been, expectations raised too high to meet, and any shortfall read as the chooser's own fault, since with so much on offer an unhappy outcome must be a failure to have chosen well, so satisfaction falls and regret and self-blame rise, the more offered the less enjoyed (Schwartz).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. Each option is added in good faith to fit some real taste, and the one who offers the larger range means only to serve more people better, following the sound logic that a wider range gives each person a better chance of a perfect fit. And the chooser who freezes before the shelves or regrets the thing it bought is not weak or ungrateful: it meets a real cognitive burden that grows with the count of options, and a real ache of the forgone that grows with the range. No one intends the paralysis. The loss is authored by no one: it is the chooser overwhelmed by an abundance meant to serve it, and the misfit is that more options, each a courtesy, can sum to a chooser who chooses less and enjoys it less.
And here is the way out, and why it spends what the abundance offered. One can ease the burden by curating, by cutting the assortment to a few well-chosen options, by setting a sensible default, or the chooser can satisfice, take the first option that is good enough rather than search for the best (Schwartz). But curation takes the choice of what to omit out of the chooser's hands and risks omitting the very one they would have wanted, a default steers them, and satisficing asks them to give up the search for the perfect fit the abundance promised. The escape works against the grain of the intuition that more freedom is always better, and none of it restores both the full range and the ease of choosing.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest, and this misfit is hedged hard. The effect is genuinely contested. A large meta-analysis of some fifty experiments found no reliable average effect of choice overload (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd), and a later synthesis found it real but only under specific conditions, when the choice set is complex, the task hard, preferences uncertain, or the decision goal ill-defined (Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman), so the strong more-is-less claim is overstated and the effect is conditional rather than general. The demonstration is Iyengar and Lepper's, the popular synthesis and the maximizer-satisficer distinction Schwartz's, and the dissent and the reconciliation the meta-analysts'. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that under those conditions where it appears, an abundance of options can paralyse the chooser and sour the choice, and not that choice is generally bad or that less is always more.

## Arc

The bend is this: past a point more options do not free the chooser but overwhelm, so a larger assortment lowers the odds of choosing at all and the satisfaction of the choice made, each option added in good faith to serve a varied taste summing to paralysis and regret.
It runs on an abundance that turns on the one it serves.
Tastes vary, so more kinds are offered to fit more people, since a wider range gives each a better chance at a perfect fit.
And the abundance is stocked, in good faith, by the plain logic that more choice can only help.
And the burden of comparing rises. Each option to be weighed against all the others, and the range lifting the hope of a perfect fit (Iyengar and Lepper).
And the chooser freezes. Unable to compare them all and unwilling to settle, it defers or declines, the larger display drawing more lookers but fewer buyers.
And the choice made is shadowed. Measured against all the forgone, expectations too high and any shortfall its own fault, the more offered the less enjoyed (Schwartz).
And it is authored by no one. Each option a courtesy and the chooser meeting a real burden, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is a curated few. Fewer options, a default, or satisficing, each easing the burden by spending the freedom the abundance offered, and the bound held, the effect contested and conditional (Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman).

## Stakes

Whether a chooser can be well served by abundance, when the very range meant to fit every taste is the range that overwhelms the one who must choose from it.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the chooser overwhelmed and away from the easy choosing the abundance promised, because to serve a varied taste more options are offered, and the burden of comparing rises with the count, and the range lifts the hope of a perfect fit too high to meet. The structure cannot both offer the full range and keep the choosing easy, because the ease of choosing falls as the options rise: the same abundance that gives each taste its better chance of a perfect fit is the abundance that makes the choosing harder and the chosen thing more shadowed, so the wider the range the more it paralyses the chooser and the more it sours the choice. The way out, a curated few, a sensible default, or the satisficing that takes the good enough, spends the very freedom the abundance offered, taking the omitted options out of the chooser's hands or asking it to give up the search for the best, and it is not the full range with the ease of choosing both. It can only show that no one is a villain, that each option is a courtesy added in good faith and the chooser meets a real burden and a real ache of the forgone, and that the loss is the chooser overwhelmed by an abundance meant to serve it and not anyone's fault. And it holds the bound in view, hedged hard, because the effect is genuinely contested, absent on average across some fifty experiments and real only under specific conditions of complexity and difficulty and uncertain preference (Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd; Chernev, Bockenholt and Goodman), so the whole of the misfit is that under those conditions more options, each a courtesy, can leave the chooser choosing less and enjoying it less, spoiled for choice.
