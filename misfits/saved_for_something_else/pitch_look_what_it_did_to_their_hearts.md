# Pitch: Look What It Did to Their Hearts

## Taxonomy

[Saved for Something Else](play_saved_for_something_else.md). The default key the whole production is read in.

## Owner

- Project: khai-misfits

## Tenor

Concerned and evidence-led, the tone of people who noticed something and said so.
Cardiac events among long-term survivors of the therapy are up, the count is exact, the cohort is real, and the question of whether the treatment caused them is being asked by the people whose job it is to ask.
Nothing false in it: an oncologist who suppressed a rise in deaths would be concealing something, and a regulator who shrugged at one would be failing.

## Undertow

Under the count, a risk set holding people who are not in it.
A patient who died of something else has not stopped being observed but has stopped being at risk, and censoring them writes down that they might still have the event; and since everybody dies of something, removing one cause raises the observed rate of all the rest (Prentice, Kalbfleisch and colleagues).
No careless statistician and no buried signal: censoring the unobserved is the taught handling, correct for the administrative loss it was built for.

## Nerve

The propriety of the alarm, and the catch that the alarm is arithmetic:
that a therapy which works produces a genuine rise in every cause it does not prevent, so success and the appearance of harm are the same fact counted twice,
and look what it did to their hearts is the sentence in which a cure is charged with what it did not do.

## Echo

After the run: a safety signal real in the numbers and false about the world, with every figure surviving every check anyone knows how to run.
And the remedy, real and priced: the cumulative incidence function and regression on the subdistribution hazard answer the clinical question, and do it by keeping in the risk set patients already dead of the competing cause, so the hazard ratio cannot be read for any individual at all (Jason Fine and Robert Gray).
And the bound held in view: cause-specific and subdistribution hazards are both correct, answer different questions, and disagree here, so the analyst must choose and the data will not choose for them (Andersen, Geskus, de Witte and Putter).
