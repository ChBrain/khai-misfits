# Play: Better in Both

## Name

Better in Both.
Will Rogers is supposed to have said that when the Okies left Oklahoma and moved to California, they raised the average intelligence of both states. It is a joke about arithmetic: take the lowest members out of one group and put them at the top of another, and both averages rise, though not one person has changed in the slightest. Feinstein, Sosin, and Wells found the same arithmetic running quietly through cancer statistics, and gave it his name (Feinstein, Sosin and Wells).
And here is the ground it runs on. Cancer is sorted into stages by how far it has spread, and survival is reported stage by stage, because that is the only way to compare like with like: a stage I patient and a stage IV patient are not the same case, and a single overall figure would tell you more about the mix of stages than about the medicine. So the stages are the yardstick, and everyone uses them, and they are the right thing to use.
And then the scanner gets better. A new imaging technology sees smaller deposits than the old one could, so metastases that were always there but invisible are now found. A patient who last decade would have been staged I, because nothing showed, is this decade staged II or III, correctly, because something shows. The radiologist has not erred; it has reported what is genuinely present. The registrar has not erred; it has applied the current staging rule to the evidence in front of it. Nobody has been misclassified. If anything, the classification is more truthful than it used to be (Feinstein, Sosin and Wells).
And look at who moved. The patients who migrate upward are not a random sample. They are, by construction, the ones with hidden disease, which makes them the sickest members of the stage they are leaving: the ones who would have died soonest of that group, dragging its average down. And they are, equally by construction, the ones whose spread is so slight it took a new machine to see it, which makes them the healthiest members of the stage they are joining: better off than the patients whose metastases were large enough to find with the old equipment. So they leave the bottom of one distribution and arrive at the top of another.
And so both averages rise. Stage I loses its worst cases, and its survival figure improves. Stage II gains cases better than its existing members, and its survival figure improves too. Every stage in the ladder can be improved at once by this single movement, and the improvement is real in the table and absent from the world: not one patient lived a day longer, not one treatment worked better, and the same people are dying at the same times of the same disease. What changed is which column they are counted in (Feinstein, Sosin and Wells).
And the comparison that reveals progress is the one that manufactures it. An oncologist setting this decade's stage-specific survival beside last decade's sees improvement across the board and reads it as medicine advancing, which is exactly what stage-specific survival is supposed to show and exactly what it has always been used to show. But the two tables were built by different instruments, so the stage labels do not mean the same thing in both, and comparing them compares a measurement to itself under a changed rule. The stronger the new scanner, the larger the apparent gain.
And the loss is authored by no one, and it is not merely academic. A treatment protocol can be credited with an improvement it did not produce, and adopted on that strength. A new scanner can be justified by survival gains that are its own artefact. Historical comparison becomes untrustworthy in the one direction everyone wants it to point. And a patient staged III today, under a rule that would have called them stage I a decade ago, may be given the more aggressive treatment that stage III warrants, though their disease is the disease that used to sit two rungs down. Nobody committed a fraud; a fixed set of categories was read across two eras of measurement.
And here is what corrects it, and why it costs. Count the whole cohort instead of counting stage by stage: total mortality across everyone diagnosed, which no reclassification can touch, since a patient moved between stages is still in the total (Welch, Schwartz and Woloshin). Or restage the old cohort under the new rule, so both tables are built by one instrument, which means retrieving decades-old imaging that was never made to answer this question, and often cannot. Both are real work, both are slower and less flattering than the figure they replace, and neither is what a stage-specific table is set up to produce, so the honest comparison is the one nobody's reporting is built to make.
And so the misfit is not that cancer statistics lie but that a category boundary read across two instruments will move people without moving anyone. The phenomenon and its naming after Rogers's line are Feinstein, Sosin, and Wells's, and the wider caution that survival figures are the wrong endpoint where detection has changed is Welch, Schwartz, and Woloshin's. The house stages the migration and holds the bound in view: that it needs stage-specific comparison across a change in detection, that every actor in it is behaving correctly, and that it lifts where whole-cohort mortality is counted or the old cohort is restaged, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that you can improve every group at once by moving people between them and improve nothing at all.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a fixed set of categories is used to report outcomes, and the instrument that assigns cases to those categories improves, the cases that migrate are the worst of the group they leave and the best of the group they join, so every group's average improves at once though no individual's outcome has changed, and a comparison across the two eras reads the arithmetic as progress.
It runs on a fixed ladder of categories.
Disease is sorted into stages by extent of spread, and outcomes are reported stage by stage, because comparing across stages would compare unlike cases and a single pooled figure would track the mix rather than the medicine (Feinstein, Sosin and Wells).
And an instrument that gets better. New imaging finds deposits the old imaging could not, so disease that was always present but invisible is now detected, and the staging built on it is more truthful than before, not less.
And a migration that is not random. The patients who move up are those with hidden spread: the sickest of the stage they leave, and, their spread being slight enough to have needed a new machine, the healthiest of the stage they join.
And both averages rise together. The lower stage loses its worst cases and improves; the upper stage gains cases better than its own and improves; every rung can lift at once from the one movement (Feinstein, Sosin and Wells).
And nobody lived longer. No treatment worked better and no patient's course changed, so the improvement is entirely in which column each case is counted in, real in the table and absent from the world.
And the comparison manufactures the progress it reports. Two tables built by different instruments carry stage labels that do not mean the same thing, so setting them side by side compares a measurement to itself under a changed rule, and the better the new scanner the larger the apparent gain.
And the loss is authored by no one. The radiologist reported real disease, the registrar applied the current rule, the oncologist used the standard yardstick, and a protocol or a machine may be credited with a gain it did not produce.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs stage-specific comparison across a change in detection, and lifts where whole-cohort mortality is counted or the old cohort is restaged under the new rule, neither of them free (Welch, Schwartz and Woloshin).
