# The Wasted Vote: Reference

The research warrant for **The Wasted Vote** (play and company not yet staged; this warrant is drafted first, to prove the
science and the distinction before authoring). The title is provisional, a reading of the phenomenon named **Duverger's
Law**: under a plurality, winner-take-all electoral rule a vote for a third party is likely wasted, so each voter
strategically deserts it for one of the two viable contenders, and this individually rational desertion sums to a
self-reinforcing two-party lock that narrows the choices everyone then faces, all cited below, held with the finding that
the law is a tendency and not a determinism, strong under single-member plurality and softened by regionally concentrated
third parties and by the social cleavages that share the cause.

## Line of Work

Staging Duverger's Law as a **wasted-vote-locks-the-field structure**: a plurality, winner-take-all rule awards the single
seat to whoever finishes first, and nothing to anyone else, so a vote cast for a candidate who cannot finish first wins
nothing at all. This is the mechanical effect, the rule under-rewarding a third party that draws real support but not the
most (Duverger). And a voter who sees this, and who prefers a third candidate but judges that candidate cannot win, faces a
choice: cast a sincere vote that will likely be wasted, or desert the true preference for whichever of the two front
runners is the lesser evil, so as to have a say in the outcome that will actually be decided. Choosing to be heard, the
voter deserts. This is the psychological effect, the anticipation of the wasted vote driving the sincere preference out
(Duverger; Cox). And here is the turn that makes it a trap: the third candidate is deserted because it is judged unable to
win, and being deserted is exactly what makes it unable to win, so the judgement fulfils itself, and the desertion of each
voter who does not want to waste a vote sums to a self-reinforcing equilibrium in which only two candidates are ever viable
and the field is locked to two. It is authored by no villain: each voter deserting the third party is doing the rational
thing, since one sincere vote for a sure loser changes nothing but its own casting and forfeits a say in the real contest;
the two front runners consolidating the deserters are only competing to win; and no one intends to narrow the field or to
silence the third view. The governing law is not that any voter is foolish or any party is scheming, but that **under a
plurality, winner-take-all rule a vote for a third party is likely wasted, so each voter strategically deserts it for one of
the two viable contenders, and this individually rational desertion sums to a self-reinforcing two-party lock that narrows
the choices everyone then faces, no villain required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the third candidate with
real but insufficient support, the counting rule that gives the seat to the first alone, the belief that a vote for the sure
loser is thrown away, the strategic desertion of the true preference for the viable lesser evil, the self-reinforcing lock
of the field to two, and the other ballot of proportional or ranked and runoff rules that alone lets a third vote count
(Duverger; Riker; Cox), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of timid voters who simply lacked the
courage of their convictions.

It is a wasted-vote-locks-the-field structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from The Crowded Middle, where two rivals each edge to the centre to win the median voter, so
both cluster alike and the ends go unserved: there the engine is the two candidates' spatial convergence in what they
offer, whereas here the engine is the voters' strategic desertion of a third option under a counting rule, and the field is
reduced in number to two rather than converged in position, so the two can stand together, a two-party field whose pair
also cluster centrist, but the misfit here is the locking to two and not the clustering alike. It is distinct from The
Beauty Contest, where players paid to guess what the room will guess price opinion about opinion and float free of worth:
there the coordination is on others' valuations in a market with no counting rule, whereas here the coordination is on which
candidates are viable under a specific electoral rule, and the vote is not a price chasing a resale. And it is distinct from
Information Cascade, where each rationally copies the visible choices of those ahead and discards a private signal, so a
herd forms on little information: there the private signal is thrown away as the belief is updated from others' actions,
whereas here the voter keeps its private preference intact, still preferring the third candidate, and abandons only its
expression, best-responding to a counting rule rather than inferring a truth from the crowd, so the wasted-vote desertion is
strategic voting and not belief-updating herd behaviour.

## Origin

The phenomenon in practice, and the research spine beneath it.

| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Duverger**          | _Political Parties: Their Organization and Activity in the Modern State_ (1951; English 1954)          | **The law**: the simple-majority single-ballot system favours the two-party system, through two effects, the mechanical (the rule under-rewards third parties in seats) and the psychological (voters anticipate the waste and desert them). | **Riker**             | "The Two-Party System and Duverger's Law: An Essay on the History of Political Science" (_APSR_, 1982) | **The canonical refinement**: the law holds near-deterministically at the level of the single district, but nationally a regionally concentrated third party can persist under plurality (Canada, India), so the law is distinguished from the looser hypothesis and its scope is fixed to the district contest. | **Cox**               | _Making Votes Count: Strategic Coordination in the World's Electoral Systems_ (1997)                   | **The strategic-coordination generalisation**: the wasted-vote logic as strategic voting, the M+1 rule bounding the viable candidates in a district to the seats plus one, and its condition, that voters need shared information about who is viable, without which the coordination on two can fail. The Other Ballot, the bound.                                                    |

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the voters as timid or the
  parties as scheming, because the source finding it stages is that the two-party lock is the sum of individually rational
  acts: a voter who prefers a third candidate but judges it cannot win forfeits a say in the real contest by voting sincerely
  for a sure loser, so deserting to the viable lesser evil is the rational way to be heard, and the two front runners who
  gather the deserters are only competing to win. No one's error makes the field close. The loss is authored by no one: it is
  the choices narrowed to two, the third view left unrepresented and the voter voting against rather than for, and the misfit
  is that the individually rational desertion of the wasted vote sums to a lock no voter chose and no party imposed.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The way out exists, but by another ballot
  than the plurality one and at a real cost: proportional representation, or ranked and runoff rules, under which a vote for
  a third candidate is no longer simply wasted, so the psychological pressure to desert is relieved and more than two parties
  can be viable (Duverger's corollary; Cox). But changing the rule is itself a political act, resisted by the two parties the
  current rule favours and the ones who must legislate the change; proportional and multi-party systems carry their own costs
  of fragmented coalitions and slower, less accountable government; and no ballot removes the need for voters to coordinate.
  The escape works against the grain of the very incumbents the rule entrenches, and none of it is the simple sincere vote
  under the rule as it stands.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the law and its mechanical and psychological effects and the corollary are Duverger's,
  the district-versus-national refinement and the regional-third-party exceptions are Riker's, the strategic-coordination
  generalisation and the M+1 rule and its informational condition are Cox's, and the cleavage critique that the rule shares
  the cause with social structure is Lipset and Rokkan's. The mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding: the law is a
  tendency and not a determinism. It holds most strongly at the level of the single-member district and can be overridden
  nationally by a regionally concentrated third party (Canada, India); its psychological effect needs voters to share
  information about who is viable, and can fail without it (Cox); and the number of parties is shaped by social cleavages as
  well as by the rule, so the rule channels rather than dictates it (Lipset and Rokkan). The scope is held to the
  wasted-vote dynamic under plurality rules, and not stretched into a claim that every two-party system is caused by the
  ballot alone or that proportional rules are without cost.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element. The play and its company are not yet authored; these are the proposed load-bearing
pieces the warrant commits the staging to carry, one per vector, no filler.

- **The Third**: carries the setting, the third candidate with real but insufficient support, genuinely preferred by some
  and unable to finish first, the option the rule will strand.
- **The Count**: carries the mechanical effect (Duverger), the plurality winner-take-all rule that awards the single seat to
  whoever finishes first and nothing to anyone else, so a vote for a candidate who cannot come first wins nothing.
- **The Wasted Vote**: carries the psychological effect (Duverger; Cox), the belief that follows from the count, that a
  sincere vote for a sure loser is thrown away, forfeiting a say in the contest that will actually be decided.
- **The Desertion**: carries the act, the strategic switch by which the voter abandons its true preference for whichever of
  the two front runners is the lesser evil, so as to be heard, its preference kept but its expression changed.
- **The Lock**: carries the harm, the self-reinforcing equilibrium in which the third is deserted because judged unable to
  win and cannot win because deserted, so the field settles to two and the choices narrow, the third view unrepresented and
  the voter voting against rather than for, drawn distinct from The Crowded Middle, The Beauty Contest, and Information
  Cascade.
- **The Other Ballot**: carries the remedy and the bound (Duverger's corollary; Cox; Lipset and Rokkan), the proportional or
  ranked and runoff rule under which a third vote is no longer wasted and more than two parties can be viable, costly and
  resisted by the incumbents the current rule favours, and the marker of the hedge, since the law is a tendency, overridden
  by regionally concentrated third parties and shaped by social cleavages as much as by the rule.
