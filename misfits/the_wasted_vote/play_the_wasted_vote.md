# Play: The Wasted Vote

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Wasted Vote.
An election runs under a plurality, winner-take-all rule: the single seat goes to whoever finishes first, and nothing to anyone else. A third candidate stands, drawing real support, genuinely preferred by some, but not the most. And a voter who prefers that third candidate faces a choice. To vote sincerely for it is, under this rule, likely to throw the vote away, since a candidate who cannot finish first wins nothing at all, and one sincere vote for a sure loser changes nothing but its own casting. Or to desert the true preference for whichever of the two front runners is the lesser evil, and so have a say in the contest that will actually be decided. This is the shape of it: the mechanical effect, the rule that under-rewards a third party in seats, and the psychological effect, the anticipation of the wasted vote that drives the sincere preference out (Duverger; Cox).
And here is the turn that makes it a trap. The third candidate is deserted because it is judged unable to win. And being deserted is exactly what makes it unable to win. So the judgement fulfils itself: each voter, not wanting to waste a vote, deserts to one of the two, and the very desertion confirms that only two are viable, so the next voter deserts too, and the field locks to two. It is a self-reinforcing equilibrium, and the choices everyone then faces are narrowed to a pair, the third view unrepresented, the voter voting against rather than for.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The voter who deserts the third party is doing the rational thing, since a sincere vote for a candidate who cannot win forfeits any say in the real contest, and to be heard is to choose among those who can win. The two front runners who gather the deserters are not scheming to close the field; they are only competing to win, as candidates do. No one casts a vote meaning to narrow the nation's choices or to silence the third view. The loss is authored by no one. It is the field locked to two by the sum of sensible votes, and the misfit is that each voter, choosing rationally not to waste a vote, helps build the lock that wastes the third choice for all.
And here is the way out, and why it is by another ballot. The desertion is forced by the rule that a vote for any but the first is thrown away, so the escape is to change the rule: proportional representation, or ranked and runoff ballots, under which a vote for a third candidate is no longer simply wasted, the pressure to desert relieved and more than two parties able to be viable (Duverger's corollary; Cox). But this is not free. Changing the rule is itself a political act, resisted by the two parties the current rule favours and who must legislate any change; proportional and multi-party systems carry their own costs of fragmented coalitions and slower, less accountable government; and no ballot removes the need for voters to coordinate. The escape works against the grain of the very incumbents the rule entrenches.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The law is a tendency and not a determinism. It holds most strongly at the level of the single-member district, and nationally a regionally concentrated third party can persist under plurality, as in Canada and India; its psychological effect needs voters to share information about who is viable, and can fail without it; and the number of parties is shaped by enduring social cleavages as well as by the rule, so the rule channels rather than dictates it (Riker; Cox; Lipset and Rokkan). The law and its two effects and the corollary are Duverger's, the district-versus-national refinement Riker's, the strategic-coordination generalisation Cox's, and the cleavage critique Lipset and Rokkan's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that under a winner-take-all rule the fear of the wasted vote deserts the third and locks the field to two, and not that the ballot alone dictates every party system.

## Arc

The bend is this: under a plurality, winner-take-all rule a vote for a third party is likely wasted, so each voter strategically deserts it for one of the two viable contenders, and this individually rational desertion sums to a self-reinforcing two-party lock that narrows the choices everyone then faces.
It runs on a rule that rewards only the first.
A third candidate stands, genuinely preferred by some but not the most.
And the rule gives the seat to the first alone. A vote for a candidate who cannot finish first wins nothing at all (Duverger).
And the voter reads the waste. Seeing the third cannot win, a sincere vote for it forfeits a say in the contest that will be decided (Duverger; Cox).
And the voter deserts. It abandons its true preference for the lesser of the two who can win, so as to be heard.
And the field locks to two. The third is deserted because judged unable to win, and deserted it cannot win, so the judgement fulfils itself and only two stay viable.
And it is authored by no one. Each voter deserts to be heard and the two only compete to win, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is by another ballot. Proportional or ranked and runoff rules under which a third vote is not wasted, costly and resisted by the incumbents, and the bound held, the law a tendency softened by regional third parties and social cleavages (Riker; Lipset and Rokkan).

## Stakes

Whether a people can keep more than two real choices on the ballot, when the rule that counts the votes makes a vote for any but the top two a vote thrown away.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the field locked to two and away from the third choice, because each voter, rather than waste a vote on a candidate who cannot win, deserts to the lesser of the two who can, and the desertions sum. The structure cannot both keep the winner-take-all rule and keep the third choice alive, because under that rule the third is deserted for being judged unable to win and cannot win once deserted, so the judgement fulfils itself: the same rational care not to waste a vote is the care that wastes the third choice for everyone. The way out, to count it by another ballot, proportional or ranked or runoff, spends a real cost, the incumbents the rule favours resisting the change and the multi-party systems it opens carrying their own costs of coalition and delay, and it is not the sincere vote under the rule as it stands. It can only show that no one is a villain, that each voter deserts to be heard and the two front runners only compete to win, and that the loss is the field narrowed to two by the sum of sensible votes and not anyone's failure of nerve. And it holds the bound in view, because the law is a tendency and not a determinism, holding at the single district but overridden nationally by a regionally concentrated third party, needing shared information to bite, and shaped by social cleavages as much as by the rule (Riker; Cox; Lipset and Rokkan), so the whole of the misfit is that the fear of the wasted vote deserts the third and locks the field to two, and each vote cast not to be wasted helps build the lock.
