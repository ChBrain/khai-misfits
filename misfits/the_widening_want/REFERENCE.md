# The Widening Want: Reference

The research warrant for **The Widening Want** (play and company not yet staged; this warrant is drafted first, to prove
the science and the distinction before authoring). The title is provisional, a reading of the phenomenon named the
**Tocqueville paradox**, or the revolution of rising expectations: as a society's conditions improve, expectations and
aspirations rise faster than the conditions themselves, so the gap between what people have and what they now feel
entitled to widens and discontent grows even as things get better, reform and progress breeding the very unrest they
might be thought to quiet, all cited below, held with the finding that the felt gap is a real and documented pressure but
not a deterministic trigger, since mobilization and opportunity mediate whether discontent becomes revolt.

## Line of Work

Staging the Tocqueville paradox as an **improvement-raises-expectations-faster structure**: a society's conditions
improve. A reform relieves an oppression, a right is extended, a standard of living rises, and the improvement is real
and offered in good faith. But the improvement does not only add to what people have; it raises what they expect, because
a gain once shown is a gain now expected, and a burden once seen to be liftable is a burden no longer borne as
inevitable. So expectations rise with the conditions, and past a point rise faster, and the gap between what people hold
and what they now feel entitled to widens rather than closes (Tocqueville). Tocqueville read it in the French Revolution,
which broke out not where oppression was heaviest but where it had most been eased, and observed that an evil patiently
endured while it seemed inescapable becomes intolerable the moment escape is conceived, so the parts of the old regime
that reform had improved were the parts whose remaining faults were felt as most unbearable (Tocqueville). And Davies
gave the shape a curve: a prolonged rise in expectations and their gratification, followed by a short sharp reversal in
what can be gotten, opens an intolerable gap, because expectation keeps climbing on the memory of the long improvement
while gratification drops, and it is the gap between the expected and the actual, not the absolute level of either, that
turns to unrest (Davies). Beneath both lies relative deprivation: discontent is not measured against zero but against a
standard, and when the standard rises faster than attainment, the felt shortfall grows even as the attainment grows, so
people are angriest not when they have least but when they have gained much and expected more (Stouffer; Runciman; Gurr).
It is authored by no villain: the reformer improves conditions for their own sake and means only to relieve, and the
people whose expectations rise are not ingrates but reading their prospects rightly, since an improvement demonstrated is
an improvement reasonably expected to continue and extend. No one raises the expectations on purpose or against the
conditions; the gap is the arithmetic of a standard that rises with the gain and then outruns it. The governing law is
not that anyone is greedy or ungrateful, but that **an improvement raises the expectations against which the remaining
shortfall is judged, so as conditions get better the gap between what is had and what is expected can widen and
discontent grow, and progress can breed the unrest it might be thought to quiet, no villain required**. The play models
the documented mechanics: the real improvement, the expectation that rises with it, the gap that widens as expectation
outruns condition, the discontent the gap produces, the sharp reversal that opens it intolerably, and the unrest that can
follow, held to its bound (Tocqueville; Davies; Gurr), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a
mob that bites the hand that fed it.

It is an improvement-raises-expectations-faster structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its
nearest neighbours. It is distinct from More to Stand Still, where the felt pleasure of a good fades as one adapts to it
and the level must be raised merely to feel what one first felt: there the movement is a backward-looking, individual
reset of perception toward a neutral hedonic baseline, the satisfaction dimming as the good persists, whereas here the
movement is a forward-looking rise of social expectation and aspiration, and the harm is not a dimmed pleasure returning
to neutral but a widening gap between rising expectation and slower condition, felt as grievance and turning to unrest.
And it is distinct from The Arms Race, where each party spends more to get ahead of the others and the prize is rank, so
the spending cancels and all stand higher at no one's relative gain: there the comparison is to rivals and the waste is
in the mutual outspending for position, whereas here the comparison is to one's own rising expectations over time, no
rival and no rank, and the gap is between what one has and what one has come to expect, not between oneself and a
neighbour.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the reformer as a fool who
  loosed the mob or the discontented as ingrates who bit the hand that fed them, because the source finding it stages is
  that the trap needs no ill will: the reformer improves conditions for their own sake and means only to relieve, and the
  people whose expectations rise are reading their prospects rightly, since an improvement shown is an improvement
  reasonably expected to continue. No one raises the expectations on purpose or against the conditions. The discontent is
  authored by no one: it is a standard that rose with the gain and then outran it, and the misfit is that progress can
  widen the gap it was meant to close and breed the unrest it might be thought to quiet.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. One might improve conditions faster than
  expectations rise, so the gap closes rather than widens, but expectations feed on the very improvement and are hard to
  outrun, and a reform that raced ahead of every rising hope would have to accelerate without pause. And to withhold
  improvement, to keep expectations low by keeping conditions low, is to choose the stagnation and the oppression the
  reform was meant to end, a cure worse than the trap. The escape works against the grain of the fact that a gain, once
  shown, becomes the floor from which the next is expected.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the observation that revolution follows improvement and that reform inflames is
  Tocqueville's, the J-curve of rising expectations and sharp reversal is Davies's, relative deprivation as the felt gap
  against a rising standard is Stouffer's, Runciman's, and Gurr's, and the mediating role of mobilization and opportunity
  is the resource-mobilization literature's. The mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding: the felt gap between
  rising expectations and slower or reversing conditions is a real and documented pressure toward discontent, but it is
  not a deterministic trigger of revolt, since grievance is widespread and revolution rare, and organization, resources,
  and political opportunity mediate whether a felt gap becomes collective action. So the house stages the structure, that
  improvement raises the expectations against which the shortfall is judged and the gap can widen and breed discontent,
  and holds that rising expectations are a pressure and not a guarantee of unrest.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element. The play and its company are not yet authored; these are the proposed load-bearing
pieces the warrant commits the staging to carry, one per vector, no filler.

- **The Improvement**: carries the setting (Tocqueville), the real gain offered in good faith, the reform or the right
  extended or the standard of living raised, that relieves an oppression once borne as inevitable.
- **The Rising Expectation**: carries the turn (Tocqueville), the aspiration that rises with the gain, since an
  improvement shown is an improvement now expected and a burden seen to be liftable is a burden no longer borne.
- **The Widening Gap**: carries the mechanism proper (Tocqueville; Davies), the space between what is had and what is now
  expected, widening rather than closing as expectation rises faster than condition.
- **The Discontent**: carries the felt harm (Stouffer; Runciman; Gurr), the grievance the gap produces, relative
  deprivation measured against a rising standard, sharpest not where least is had but where much was gained and more
  expected.
- **The Reversal**: carries the trigger (Davies), the short sharp check after prolonged improvement, expectation still
  climbing while gratification drops, opening the gap intolerably, the J-curve's downturn.
- **The Unrest**: carries the outcome and the bound (Davies; Gurr; the mobilization critique), the discontent that can
  turn to collective action, drawn distinct from More to Stand Still and The Arms Race, and the marker of the hedge, since
  the gap is a real pressure but mobilization and opportunity mediate whether it becomes revolt, grievance widespread and
  revolution rare.
