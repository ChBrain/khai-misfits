# Play: The Widening Want

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Widening Want.
A society's conditions improve. A reform relieves an oppression long borne, a right is extended, a standard of living rises. The improvement is real and offered in good faith, and it is meant to close a gap between how people live and how they might.
But the improvement does not only add to what people have. It raises what they expect, because a gain once shown is a gain now expected, and a burden once seen to be liftable is a burden no longer borne as inevitable. So expectations rise with the conditions, and past a point they rise faster, and the gap between what people hold and what they now feel entitled to widens rather than closes (Tocqueville).
Tocqueville read it in the French Revolution, which broke out not where oppression was heaviest but where it had most been eased. An evil patiently endured while it seemed inescapable becomes intolerable the moment escape is conceived, so the very parts of the old regime that reform had improved were the parts whose remaining faults were felt as least bearable, and the reforms that relieved the people were the reforms that stirred them (Tocqueville).
And Davies gave the shape a curve. A prolonged rise in expectations and in their gratification, followed by a short sharp reversal in what can be gotten, opens an intolerable gap: expectation keeps climbing on the memory of the long improvement while gratification drops, and it is the distance between the expected and the actual, not the absolute level of either, that turns to unrest (Davies). Beneath both lies relative deprivation: discontent is measured not against zero but against a standard, so when the standard rises faster than attainment the felt shortfall grows even as attainment grows, and people are angriest not when they have least but when they have gained much and looked for more (Stouffer; Runciman; Gurr).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The reformer improves conditions for their own sake and means only to relieve, and follows the plain and good rule that to lift a burden is to do good. And the people whose expectations rise are not ingrates but reading their prospects rightly, since an improvement demonstrated is an improvement reasonably expected to continue and to extend. No one raises the expectations on purpose, and no one sets them against the conditions; the gap is the arithmetic of a standard that climbs with the gain and then outruns it. The loss is authored by no one: it is progress that widens the gap it was meant to close, and the misfit is that to make things better is to raise the bar by which they are judged, and so to breed the discontent that better was meant to quiet.
And here is the way out, and why it cannot simply be taken. One might improve conditions faster than expectations rise, so the gap closes rather than widens, but expectations feed on the very improvement and are hard to outrun, and a reform that raced ahead of every rising hope would have to accelerate without pause. And to hold expectations down by holding conditions down, to keep the people content by keeping them low, is to choose the stagnation and the oppression the reform was meant to end, a cure worse than the trap. The escape works against the grain of the fact that a gain, once shown, becomes the floor from which the next is expected.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The felt gap between rising expectations and slower or reversing conditions is a real and documented pressure toward discontent, but it is not a deterministic trigger of revolt, since grievance is widespread and revolution rare, and organization, resources, and political opportunity mediate whether a felt gap becomes collective action. The observation that revolution follows improvement is Tocqueville's, the J-curve of rising expectations and sharp reversal is Davies's, relative deprivation as the felt gap is Stouffer's and Runciman's and Gurr's, and the mediating role of mobilization is the resource-mobilization literature's. The house stages the structure, that improvement raises the expectations against which the shortfall is judged so the gap can widen and breed discontent, and holds that rising expectations are a pressure and not a guarantee of unrest.

## Arc

The bend is this: an improvement raises the expectations against which the remaining shortfall is judged, so as conditions get better the gap between what is had and what is expected can widen and discontent grow, and progress can breed the unrest it might be thought to quiet.
It runs on a standard that climbs with the gain and then outruns it.
The conditions improve, a real gain offered in good faith to close a gap.
And the expectation rises with it. A gain once shown expected to continue, a burden seen liftable no longer borne (Tocqueville).
And the gap widens. Expectation outrunning condition, the space between the had and the hoped for growing as things get better.
And the discontent grows. The shortfall felt against a risen standard, sharpest where much was gained and more expected (Gurr).
And a sharp reversal opens it. Expectation still climbing while gratification drops, the gap turned intolerable (Davies).
And the unrest can follow. The discontent turning to collective action, held to its bound, a pressure and not a guarantee (the mobilization critique).

## Stakes

Whether a society can be made better without breeding the discontent that better was meant to quiet, when the very gain that relieves is the gain that raises the bar by which the remainder is judged.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the widening gap and away from the closing one, because the improvement is real and the expectation it raises is reasonable, and the standard climbs with the gain and then outruns it. The structure cannot both improve the conditions and hold the expectations still, because the improving is the raising: the same gain that relieves a burden is the gain that shows the burden was liftable and so raises the hope of lifting the rest, and the more is given the more is expected, and the faster conditions rise the faster the standard against which they are judged rises with them. The way out, improving faster than expectations rise, or holding conditions low to hold expectations low, either outruns what cannot easily be outrun or chooses the stagnation the reform was meant to end. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the reformer relieves in good faith and the people read their prospects rightly, and that the discontent is a standard that rose with the gain and outran it, authored by no one. And it holds the bound in view, hedged, because the felt gap is a real pressure toward discontent and not a deterministic trigger of revolt, grievance widespread and revolution rare, mobilization and opportunity mediating the turn (Davies; Gurr; the mobilization critique), so the whole of the misfit is that to make things better is to raise the bar by which they are judged, and progress can widen the want it was meant to fill.
