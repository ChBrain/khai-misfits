# Play: Clear from Here

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Clear from Here.
Fischhoff put the question plainly: does knowing how something turned out change how predictable it seems? He gave people accounts of historical episodes and asked how likely each possible ending had been. Told which ending had actually occurred, they rated it as having been far more probable than people judging the same account without that knowledge, and they did not notice they were doing it. He called the effect creeping determinism: the past quietly rearranging itself into a line that could only have gone one way (Fischhoff).
And the sharper demonstration came the same year. Fischhoff and Beyth had people forecast the outcomes of Nixon's trips to China and the Soviet Union, then asked them afterward to recall their own predictions. People misremembered their forecasts in the direction of what had happened, reporting that they had expected the actual outcome more strongly than their own written estimates showed. They were not lying. They could not get back to what they had thought before they knew (Fischhoff and Beyth).
And this is the thing that makes it a trap rather than a mistake. It is not that reviewers are careless, and it is not cured by care. Told about the bias in advance and instructed to disregard the outcome, people still show it, because the outcome has already reorganised what the record looks like: the three warning signs that were three signals among two hundred are now the three that were pointing at it all along, and the reviewer is not manufacturing that pattern, it is seeing it, because it is genuinely there once you know where to look.
And so the review goes wrong in a particular direction. A decision made under real uncertainty, where several endings were live and the chosen action was sound against all of them, is read after the fact as a warning ignored, since the ending that came is now the obvious one and the signals for it are now the salient ones. The decider is judged not on the fog it stood in but on a map drawn afterward, and that map has one road on it.
And it runs the other way too, which is the half people forget. A reckless call that happened to come off is read as foresight, because the same straightening that makes a bad outcome look predictable makes a good one look intended, so the gambler who got lucky is promoted for judgement it never exercised and the careful decider who got unlucky is disciplined for negligence it never committed (Baron and Hershey).
And nobody in this is behaving badly. The harmed are entitled to an account, and a system that met a serious failure with no inquiry at all would be the worse system by a wide margin, so the demand that produces the review is legitimate and must be met. The reviewer is reading the record honestly and reaching the conclusion the record now supports. The decider is not being framed. Every part of the process is working, and the process arrives at a wrong answer about a right decision.
And the real cost is not only the unfairness. It is that the review was supposed to teach the organisation something, and hindsight replaces the lesson with a culprit. If the finding is that someone ignored an obvious sign, the action item is to tell people to notice obvious signs, which is no lesson at all, since the sign was not obvious at the time and will not be obvious next time either. The genuine lesson, that this was a real gamble taken under real fog and here is how such gambles should be weighed, is exactly the lesson creeping determinism makes invisible, so the inquiry consumes the failure and produces nothing that would help.
And here is what checks it, and why it costs. Write it down before you know: a decision log recording what was expected, with what confidence, and which alternatives were live, made at the time and sealed, so the review can be handed the fog instead of reconstructing it. This works, and Fischhoff and Beyth is precisely the demonstration of why it must be written rather than recalled. And it is expensive to maintain across every decision that might one day be reviewed, most of which never will be; it is only as good as the honesty of the entry, which is written by someone who knows it may be read at an inquiry; and it can be curated afterward, since a log that can be added to is a log that can be furnished. The record narrows the gap and does not close it.
And so the misfit is not that reviewers are unfair but that the vantage point does the work, and nobody can stand anywhere else. The finding and the naming of creeping determinism are Fischhoff's, the demonstration that people cannot recover their own prior forecasts is Fischhoff and Beyth's, and the companion effect by which decision quality is judged from outcomes is Baron and Hershey's. The house stages the distortion and holds the bound in view: that it needs a genuinely uncertain decision judged after its result is known, that the reviewer is not pretending, and that it lifts under records made before the outcome and never entirely, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that the road only has one lane when you are looking back down it.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a decision made under genuine uncertainty is evaluated after its outcome is known, the knowledge of the outcome reorganises how foreseeable it appears, so a sound decision with a bad result is judged as negligence and a reckless one with a good result as foresight, and the review produces a culprit instead of the lesson it was convened to find, with no evaluator pretending and none able to stop.
It runs on a decision taken in fog.
Several endings are live, the evidence points in more than one direction, and the action chosen is sound against the spread of what might happen rather than against any one of them (Fischhoff).
And an outcome that arrives. One branch becomes actual, and from that moment it is not one possibility among several but the thing that happened.
And a past that straightens behind it. Outcome knowledge reorganises the record: the signals that pointed the way it went become salient and the ones that did not recede, so the episode reads as a line that could only have run one way, and this is seen rather than manufactured (Fischhoff).
And a memory that will not go back. People asked to recall their own prior forecasts misremember them toward what happened, so even the decider's own account of its reasoning drifts, and the fog cannot be recovered by trying (Fischhoff and Beyth).
And a verdict that follows the straightening. The decision is judged against a map drawn afterward with one road on it, so a sound call reads as a warning ignored, and a reckless call that came off reads as judgement (Baron and Hershey).
And a demand that is legitimate. The harmed are entitled to an account and a failure met with no inquiry would be worse, so the review must happen and cannot simply be waved off.
And the lesson is consumed. If the finding is that someone missed an obvious sign, the remedy is to tell people to notice obvious signs, which teaches nothing, since the sign was not obvious then and will not be next time.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs a genuinely uncertain decision judged after its result is known, and lifts under records written before the outcome, which cost upkeep, can be curated afterward, and never close the gap.
