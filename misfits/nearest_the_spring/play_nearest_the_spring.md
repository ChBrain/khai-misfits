# Play: Nearest the Spring

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Nearest the Spring.
New money must enter the economy somewhere, and it cannot enter everywhere at once. It comes in through a particular hand: the miner who brings gold out of the ground, the borrower who takes the new loan, the seller of bonds to the central bank, the government that spends before it taxes. Wherever it enters, it enters at a point, and from that point it must spread.
The hand nearest the source spends it first, and spends it for ordinary reasons, and spends it at the prices that prevail now, before the new money has circulated and bid those prices up. So it takes real goods at the old prices, and is better off not merely in money but in what the money buys, richer by the difference between the prices it paid and the prices to come (Cantillon).
Then the money moves, hand to hand, and as it spreads it raises the prices it touches, not all at once but along the path it travels, activity and prices lifting in the interval as the new money works its way through the economy (Hume). What was one hand's cheap purchase becomes the next hand's higher price, and the wave of rising prices rolls outward from the spring.
By the time it reaches the last hands, the wage earner whose pay was set for the year, the pensioner on a fixed income, the saver who held cash, the prices those hands must pay have already risen, and the money that finally reaches them buys less than it would have before it moved. So they meet the higher prices with money that has not grown, and lose real purchasing power, poorer by the same difference the first hand gained (Mises).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The one who injects the money does so for ordinary and often needful reasons: a war to fund, a road to build, a faltering economy to steady, credit to extend, ore to bring up. And each hand that receives the money and spends it does exactly what anyone would, at the prices it finds, in a fair exchange at the going rate. No one moves purchasing power from the late to the early on purpose, and no single spender is placed to see the tilt, since from inside the flow every transaction is honest and fairly priced. The loss is authored by no one: it is a redistribution by the order in which the money arrives, and the misfit is that to be nearest the spring is an advantage no one granted, and to be last is a loss no one levied.
And here is the way out, and why it spends what it would save. One might enter the money more evenly, as a broad and equal transfer rather than through a single market or a single hand, so that fewer sit ahead of the price rise and the tilt is flattened. But money must still enter somewhere and reach the rest over time, and to spread it perfectly and instantly is to abolish the very transmission by which monetary policy does its work, the interval in which the new money lifts activity before it lifts all prices. And to withhold the injection entirely, to spare the last hands the tilt, is to forgo the war funded, the economy steadied, the credit extended. No injection reaches everyone at once, and the escape trades the distributional cost for the loss of the thing the money was created to do.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. That money is non-neutral along the path of its injection, and that relative prices and the distribution shift in the transition, is standard across the classical, Austrian, and modern accounts. But the magnitude of the redistribution, and even the net winners in any given episode, are genuinely contested, since the same expansion that reaches asset holders first may also lower unemployment and lift the wages of the last hands. The mechanism and the name are Cantillon's, the non-neutral interval Hume's, the step-by-step diffusion Mises's, and the modern distributional argument the monetary-policy literature's. The house stages the transmission and its direction, that the order of the money's arrival redistributes from the late to the early, and holds the size and the incidence as disputed, not that monetary expansion always enriches the first and impoverishes the last by a settled amount.

## Arc

The bend is this: new money enters through particular hands and spreads over time, so those nearest the injection spend at the old prices and gain while those it reaches last meet the new prices and lose, a redistribution by the order of the money's arrival that no one intends and no single hand can see.
It runs on a flow that must enter at a point and spread from it.
The money is injected, at a point, for an ordinary and needful reason, since it cannot enter everywhere at once.
And the first hand spends it cheap. At the prices that still hold, taking real goods before the money has bid them up (Cantillon).
And the money spreads. Hand to hand, lifting the prices it touches along the path it travels, the wave rolling out from the spring (Hume).
And the last hand pays dear. Meeting the risen prices with money that has not grown, on a wage or a pension set before the rise (Mises).
And the order is the whole of it. The early gain and the late loss the plain consequence of who received the money first, a redistribution no transfer intends.
And the tilt is seen only from above. The net running from the late to the early, and the bound held, the direction standard and the size contested (the distributional-policy debate).
