# Worth More Nearly Gone: Reference

The research warrant for **Worth More Nearly Gone**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls the
**anthropogenic Allee effect** (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues): that where demand values a
species for its rarity as such, the price rises as the population falls, so exploitation becomes more
rewarding exactly as the population becomes least able to bear it, and the feedback runs toward
extinction through a market rather than through any biological failure, with no party intending it and
the protective listing itself capable of raising the premium. It is held with the finding that the effect
is conditional on demand keyed to rarity as such and a supply still reachable, and that it lifts where
the premium is broken or the demand dissolved, neither reliable and both able to feed what they meant to
end.

## Line of Work

Staging the anthropogenic Allee effect as a **scarcity-prices-its-own-extinction structure**: for most
exploited populations, scarcity protects. A thinned stock is expensive to work, effort moves elsewhere,
and the species is shielded by its own unprofitability, which is why most exploited populations decline
without vanishing. For a class of goods the relation inverts, because rarity is not an obstacle to the
value but its source: the specimen wanted because few exist, the trophy because it is nearly the last,
the caged bird because no one else has one, the delicacy because it is unobtainable. For these,
willingness to pay climbs as the population falls (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues). The
ordinary brake becomes an accelerator: each animal taken raises the value of those remaining, which makes
the next expedition worth mounting though there is less to find, so effort rises as the stock declines
and the economics calls the hunters in hardest where the population can least bear it. The loop contains
no biology at all, which is what separates it from the classical Allee effect: no mate-finding fails and
no cooperative defence collapses, the positive density dependence being supplied entirely by a price
responding to a headcount. The play models the documented mechanics: the premium that scarcity itself
creates, the exploitation that follows the price, the spiral in which each loss funds the next, the
protective listing that publishes the rarity to the buyers whose willingness to pay is the danger, and
the market-flooding remedy that can collapse the premium and can also launder illegal stock and
normalise an appetite that grows faster than it is fed, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, and
not as a tale of a monstrous poacher or a greedy collector. The ordinary expectation is that scarcity
raises cost and so relieves pressure; the finding is that where scarcity raises value instead, the
relief runs backwards. The governing law is that **where the value of a species derives from its rarity,
each reduction in the population raises the reward for further exploitation, so the decline finances its
own continuation and can proceed to extinction with every participant behaving ordinarily**.

It is a market-and-extinction structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its
nearest neighbours. It is distinct from Safety in Numbers, which stages the **classical** Allee effect,
where per-capita fitness falls with density through mate-finding, cooperative defence, and cooperative
foraging, and a population thinned past a critical threshold spirals down by its own demography: there
the positive density dependence is **intrinsic and biological**, arising inside the population whether or
not any human is present, whereas here no biological mechanism is involved at all and the positive
feedback is supplied wholly from outside, by a price that rises as the headcount falls, so the two
misfits share a spiral shape and share nothing of its engine. It is distinct from The Commons, where a
shared stock is drawn down because access is unpriced and each user's rational share sums past the
regeneration rate: there the trouble is that the resource is **too cheap to whoever takes it** and the
remedy is to price or enclose it, whereas here the resource is extremely expensive and getting dearer,
and its price is the very thing killing it, so pricing is the mechanism rather than the cure. And it is
distinct from Chasing the Price, the cobweb cycle, where lagged supply responses to price produce
oscillation: there the price and quantity **cycle** around an equilibrium through a production lag,
whereas here there is no lag and no oscillation but a monotonic spiral, each loss raising the price
permanently and irreversibly, since the stock does not grow back into the next round.

It runs against the house's **Room to Grow**, and the pair is worth reading together: there thinning
a population relieves its own crowding and the survivors overcompensate, so the number can rise,
whereas here thinning it raises the price it commands and the exploitation intensifies, so the
decline accelerates. Same intervention, opposite sign, and the difference is the channel: the
response there is biological and self-limiting, running through competition at the bottleneck, and
here it is economic and self-reinforcing, running through a market that pays more for what is nearly
gone. A cull that a fishery survives can finish a rarity, and nothing about the population tells you
which.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast a monstrous
  poacher or a grotesque collector, because the finding it stages does not require either: valuing a
  thing because it is rare is close to the definition of collecting and is not a perversion; supplying a
  high-priced good is what a supplier does, often from a place with few other ways to earn; and listing
  an endangered species is exactly what a regulator exists to do. There is no conspiracy and no party
  that wants the species gone, a species gone being a market ended. The loss is authored by no one: it is
  a demand keyed to rarity plus a supply that can still reach the animal, so ordinary wanting and
  ordinary supplying sum to a spiral, and the loss falls on a thing that cannot bid for itself.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction, and here both remedies
  turn in the hand. Listing the species is right, necessary, and simultaneously an announcement to every
  buyer that it is now officially scarce, so the premium can rise on the day the protection lands and the
  document meant to save it can raise the price on its head (Hall, Milner-Gulland and Courchamp). And
  flooding the market with bred or farmed stock, which is the only intervention that attacks the premium
  directly, can collapse the price and can also give illegal stock a channel to be laundered through and
  normalise a larger market that recruits demand faster than it satisfies it. The reckoning is that the
  two available corrections are each real, each unreliable, and each able to feed the thing they were
  meant to end.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The anthropogenic Allee effect, the rarity-value account of extinction, is
  Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan, and colleagues'; the work on perceived rarity driving exploitation and the
  hazard that publicising endangered status raises value is Hall, Milner-Gulland, and Courchamp's; and
  the classical Allee effect the phenomenon is named against is Allee's, drawn to mark the distinction
  and not to claim the modern result. The scope is held to species whose demand prizes rarity as such,
  and is not stretched into a claim that conservation listing is generally counterproductive, that
  protection should be withheld, or that captive breeding is generally the wrong policy, since the
  source literature raises these as hazards to be managed within conservation practice and not as
  arguments against it, and the play stages the difficulty exactly as a difficulty rather than as a
  recommendation.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Premium**: carries the inversion (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues), rarity as the
  source of value rather than an obstacle to it, so willingness to pay is keyed to the headcount and
  rises as the headcount falls, the one fact the ordinary brake depends on being false.
- **The Dwindling**: carries the falling population, the stock reduced by taking that is answering a
  price, each reduction being also an increase in the value of what remains.
- **The Chase**: carries the exploitation response, effort rising with the price so the next expedition
  is worth mounting though there is less to find, the economics calling hardest where the population can
  least bear it.
- **The Spiral**: carries the feedback whole (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues), each loss
  raising the value of the remainder and financing the next loss, a positive feedback containing no
  biology at all, which is what separates it from the classical effect.
- **The Notice**: carries the remedy that turns (Hall, Milner-Gulland and Courchamp), the protective
  listing that is both the right act and a published announcement of scarcity to the buyers whose
  willingness to pay is the danger.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge, that the trap needs demand keyed to rarity as
  such and a reachable supply, and lifts where the premium is broken by flooding the market or the demand
  is dissolved, neither reliable and the first able to launder illegal stock and enlarge the appetite it
  was meant to satisfy.
