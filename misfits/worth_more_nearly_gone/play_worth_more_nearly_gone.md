# Play: Worth More Nearly Gone

## Name

Worth More Nearly Gone.
For most things in the world, scarcity and value move together in a way that eventually saves the thing. A fishery thinned out becomes expensive to work, the boats go elsewhere, and the stock recovers in the gap. Cost rises with rarity, effort falls off, and the species is protected by its own unprofitability. This is the ordinary shape, and it is why most exploited populations do not simply vanish.
And here is the case where the shape inverts. For some things, rarity is not an obstacle to the value: rarity is the value. A collector wants the specimen precisely because few exist. A trophy is worth having because it is the last of its kind, a caged bird because no one else has one, a medicine or a delicacy because it is unobtainable. For these, the willingness to pay does not fall as the population falls. It climbs (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues).
And that inversion turns the ordinary brake into an accelerator. Every animal taken makes the remainder rarer, and rarer means dearer, and dearer means the next expedition is worth mounting even though there is less to find, and the one after that is worth more still. The economics that would ordinarily call the hunters off is calling them in, harder, exactly as the population approaches the point where it cannot bear the loss. This is a positive feedback with no biological component at all: no mate-finding is failing, no cooperative defence is collapsing, the loop runs entirely through a price (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues).
And nobody in it is a monster. The collector is not destroying anything; it is buying a legal or grey-market item it genuinely values, and valuing rarity is not a perversion, it is close to the definition of collecting. The supplier is responding to a price, which is what suppliers do, often from a place with few other ways to earn. There is no conspiracy and no one who wants the species gone, since a species gone is a market ended, and yet the sum of ordinary wanting and ordinary supplying runs the population down.
And now the cruelty. The obvious remedy is to protect the species: name it, list it, publish its status, make its plight known. And the listing is a public announcement, to everyone including the buyers, that this thing is now officially scarce. The very document that mobilises protection advertises the rarity that is the source of the premium, so a species can become more valuable on the day it is declared endangered, and the notice meant to save it can raise the price on its head (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues; Hall, Milner-Gulland and Courchamp). Nobody drafting the listing intended that, and the listing is still the right thing to do, and it still has that effect.
And the second remedy carries its own reversal. Break the premium by supplying the market: breed the thing in captivity, farm it, flood the trade with legal stock until scarcity stops meaning anything and the price collapses. It can work. It can also fail in two documented ways: the legal supply gives illegal stock a channel to be laundered through, and a bigger, more visible, more normalised market can recruit demand faster than it satisfies it, so the intervention meant to end the premium ends up enlarging the appetite that pays it.
And the loss is authored by no one and falls on a thing that cannot bid. The collector paid a fair price for something it loved. The supplier answered a signal any supplier would answer. The warden listed a species that plainly needed listing. The breeder tried the only intervention that reaches the premium at all. And the population goes down a spiral driven not by anyone's malice but by the plain fact that what people were willing to pay rose as fast as the numbers fell.
And so the misfit is not that people are greedy but that value keyed to rarity turns every loss into a reason for the next one. The anthropogenic Allee effect, the rarity-value account of extinction, is Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan, and colleagues'; the experimental work on how perceived rarity drives exploitation is Hall, Milner-Gulland, and Courchamp's; and the classical Allee effect it is named against, the intrinsic kind, is Allee's. The house stages the price-driven spiral and holds the bound in view: that it needs demand which prizes rarity as such and a supply still reachable, that every actor is behaving ordinarily, and that it lifts where the premium is broken or the demand dissolved, neither reliable and both able to feed what they meant to end, so the trap is real and bounded, and the whole of it is that a thing worth more the less of it there is has been priced into its own disappearance.

## Arc

The bend is this: where demand values a species for its rarity as such, the price rises as the population falls, so exploitation becomes more rewarding exactly as the population becomes less able to bear it, and the feedback runs to extinction through a market rather than through any biological failure, with no one intending it and the protective listing itself capable of raising the price.
It runs on a demand that prizes rarity.
For some goods, scarcity is not a cost but the source of the value: the specimen, the trophy, the caged bird, the delicacy, wanted precisely because few remain (Courchamp, Angulo, Rivalan and colleagues).
And an inversion of the ordinary brake. Normally a thinned stock becomes unprofitable to work and the effort moves on, which is what saves most exploited populations, and here the opposite happens because willingness to pay climbs as numbers fall.
And exploitation that follows the price. Each animal taken raises the value of those remaining, so the next expedition is worth mounting though there is less to find, and effort rises as the stock declines.
And a spiral with no biology in it. No mate-finding fails and no cooperative defence collapses; the loop is a price responding to a headcount, and the headcount responding to the price.
And the protection that publishes the scarcity. Listing a species announces to every buyer that it is officially rare, so the document meant to save it can lift the premium on it, and it is still the right document to issue (Hall, Milner-Gulland and Courchamp).
And the flooding that can enlarge the appetite. Breeding or farming to collapse the premium can work, and can also launder illegal stock and normalise a market that recruits demand faster than it is satisfied.
And the loss is authored by no one. The collector paid for what it loved, the supplier answered a signal, the warden listed what needed listing, and no party wanted the species gone, a species gone being a market ended.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs demand keyed to rarity as such and a supply still reachable, and lifts where the premium is broken or the demand dissolved, neither reliable and both able to feed the trap.
