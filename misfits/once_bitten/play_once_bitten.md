# Play: Once Bitten

## Name

Once Bitten.
Mark Twain put it in a cat. A cat that sits once on a hot stove-lid, he wrote, will never sit on a hot stove-lid again, and that is well, but it will never sit on a cold one either. The cat has learned too much from one bad experience: it now avoids all stove-lids, and because it avoids them, it never discovers that a cold one would have been fine. Jerker Denrell turned that cat into a theory of how anyone who learns from experience comes to misjudge the world. We form our impressions by sampling: we try an option, a restaurant, a colleague, a strategy, and we go on trying the ones that went well and stop trying the ones that went badly. That is a sound rule, and it has one built-in flaw. When a first try goes well, we keep sampling, so if the good result was only luck, continued experience corrects it: the option regresses to its true, lower worth and we learn it. But when a first try goes badly, we stop, and because we stop we never gather the experience that would tell us the bad result was only luck. The good option that happened to have a bad day is written off, and never asked back (Denrell).
And here is the asymmetry that opens. Errors on the high side are self-correcting, because we keep sampling what looks good and the truth catches up with it. Errors on the low side are self-sealing, because we stop sampling what looks bad and the truth never gets the chance. So the record we learn from is truncated on the low side: it holds many looks at the options that pleased us and a single bad look at the ones that did not, and the honest average of that record is biased downward. We end up thinking worse of the world than it is, not through any gloom, but through the plain arithmetic of what an adaptive sampler goes on to see (Denrell & March).
And here is what it does to risk. The options most likely to hand you a bad first draw are the variable ones, the ones whose outcomes swing high and low, because a wide spread is what produces an unlucky low. So the variable options are the ones most often written off after one bad draw, and an agent with no fear of risk at all comes to shun variability, having been burned by it and never having gone back to see it pay. Risk aversion here is not a taste brought to the world; it is manufactured by the sampling, a preference for the safe learned from a record that never recorded the variable option's good days (Denrell).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The learner is not prejudiced or lazy: avoiding what burned you is the rational thing to do, and forming your view from your own experience is exactly what good judgement is supposed to mean. The option written off is at no fault: it may be genuinely good and merely have drawn badly once. There is no bad judge here and no unfair hearing, only a fair rule applied to a sample the rule itself truncates. The loss is authored by no one. It is the good option never given a second try and the person met once at their worst and disapproved of ever after, and the misfit is that the very prudence that keeps us from repeating a painful experience is what keeps us from ever discovering the experience was chance.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The only cure is to go back and sample what burned you, deliberately, against the pull of avoidance: to give second chances by policy, to randomise a few trials, to re-explore the options dropped after a poor start. But this is dear, because most of what burned you really was bad, so to rescue the few good options written off by luck you must pay to retouch many stoves that really are hot, and you cannot tell in advance which is which. Or you can borrow others' experience of the option, learning from a sample you did not truncate yourself, where that is available and honest.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The bias is as strong as outcomes are variable and sampling is the agent's own choice. Where outcomes are near-deterministic, so a bad draw truly means the option is bad, there is nothing to correct and no error to seal. Where sampling is forced, or an outside record keeps testing what you avoid, the low side gets observed after all and the asymmetry softens. And most options avoided after a bad draw really are bad, so the effect misjudges only the variable and unluckily-sampled, not everything shunned. The hot-stove effect, the sampling asymmetry, the impression bias, and the manufactured risk aversion are Denrell's, with Denrell and March for the information-restriction form; the cat and the stove are Twain's image, and the law of effect is Thorndike's, the reinforcement antecedent the effect turns into a bias. The house stages the asymmetry and holds the bound in view: that where we learn by sampling and avoid what burns us, the bad first draw is never corrected and the good option is never asked back, no one at fault, and the only remedy is the costly one of trying again anyway.

## Arc

The bend is this: where an agent learns from experience and chooses whether to sample an option by its past outcomes, a bad early outcome leads it to avoid the option and so never gather the evidence that would correct an unduly negative impression, while a good early outcome keeps the option in use where continued sampling corrects an unduly positive one, so negative errors are self-sealing and positive errors self-correcting, and beliefs formed from experience carry a systematic downward bias.
It runs on an agent that learns by sampling.
We form impressions by trying options and keeping the ones that go well, a sound rule for learning what is good (Denrell).
And a good first draw is corrected. Keep sampling what looked good, and if the good result was luck, continued experience regresses it to the truth, so the high-side error washes out (Denrell).
And a bad first draw is not. Stop sampling what looked bad, and the experience that would show the bad result was luck is never gathered, so the low-side error seals itself (Denrell & March).
And the record tilts low. Held with many looks at the kept options and one bad look at the dropped ones, the sample is truncated on the low side, and its honest average is biased downward (Denrell & March).
And risk is shunned for free. The variable options draw badly most often, so they are written off most often, and an agent with no fear of risk comes to avoid variance, the aversion manufactured by the sampling (Denrell).
And it is authored by no one. The learner's avoidance is rational and the option is at no fault, so the bias is a property of adaptive sampling and not a prejudice.
And the way out is dear. Re-sample what burned you, by policy or randomised trial or borrowed experience, and pay to retouch many hot stoves to find the one that was only unlucky (Denrell).
And the whole is held against its limit. The bias is as strong as outcomes are variable and sampling is chosen, and softens where a bad draw truly means bad, where sampling is forced, or where others' experience can be borrowed (Denrell).

## Stakes
