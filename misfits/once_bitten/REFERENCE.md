# Once Bitten: Reference

The research warrant for **Once Bitten**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon the literature
calls **the hot-stove effect**: Denrell's account of why an agent that learns from experience and
chooses whether to sample an option by its past outcomes forms a systematically negative picture of
the world, because a bad early outcome ends the sampling that would have corrected it, while a good
early outcome keeps the option in use where continued sampling corrects it, all cited below, held
with the finding that the bias bites where outcomes are variable and sampling is the agent's own
choice, and softens where outcomes are reliable, sampling is forced, or others' experience can be
observed.

## Line of Work

Staging the hot-stove effect as an **asymmetric-sampling-seals-the-error structure**: an agent that learns which
options are good by trying them, and rationally avoids what has burned it, will misjudge the world in one
direction only. Mark Twain drew the image: a cat that sits on a hot stove-lid will never sit on a stove-lid again,
hot or cold, and so never learns that a cold one is safe. When an option gives a bad early outcome, the agent
avoids it, and because it is avoided it is never sampled again, so an impression that was merely unlucky is never
corrected: the negative error seals itself. When an option gives a good early outcome, the agent keeps using it,
and continued use exposes a lucky first draw to correction, so the positive error washes out. The two errors are
not treated alike: the agent goes on testing what looked good and stops testing what looked bad, so the record it
learns from is truncated on the low side, and beliefs formed from experience carry a systematic downward bias.
Variable options suffer most, because variability is what produces the early bad draw that ends sampling, so an
agent with no intrinsic fear of risk comes to shun risk all the same, and a person met once at their worst is
disapproved of ever after because the meeting is never repeated. The governing law is not that anyone judges
carelessly, but that **where an agent learns from experience and chooses whether to sample an option by its past
outcomes, a bad early outcome leads it to avoid the option and so never gather the evidence that would correct an
unduly negative impression, while a good early outcome keeps the option in use where continued sampling corrects
an unduly positive one, so negative errors are self-sealing and positive errors self-correcting, and beliefs
formed from experience carry a systematic downward bias, with variable options shunned though some are better than
they first seemed**. The play models the documented mechanics: the bad early outcome that triggers avoidance
(Denrell), the rational avoidance that stops the sampling and censors the record (Denrell & March), the untested
option whose true worth is never learned, the continued sampling that corrects only the positive side, the
asymmetrically truncated sample, and the downward bias and endogenous risk aversion it produces (Denrell), as the
load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a prejudiced judge who will not give a fair hearing.

It is a learning-and-sampling asymmetry no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from The Survivors, survivorship bias, where the sample is truncated because the
failures were destroyed before they could be counted, the planes that went down never returning to be seen: there
the censoring is exogenous, done by the world to the data, whereas here it is endogenous, the learner's own
rational decision to stop sampling what burned it, so the missing evidence is not lost to attrition but withheld
by choice, and the bias is the agent's, not the record-keeper's. It is distinct from The Bounce, regression to the
mean, where an extreme result is followed by a milder one and the reversal is misattributed to a reward or a
punishment that did nothing: the hot-stove effect turns on regression too, but its whole point is the asymmetry of
observing it, since a lucky high result is kept in play and seen to regress while an unlucky low result is
abandoned and its regression never witnessed, so The Bounce is the one-sided misreading of a reversal that is
seen, and this is the systematic non-seeing of the reversal on the low side because sampling stopped. It is
distinct from The Open Cage, learned helplessness, where repeated uncontrollable failure teaches an agent that
effort never pays and it stops trying across the board even where trying would now work: there the cessation is
general, a global expectation of no control, whereas here trying does not cease at large but only for the one
option a bad draw condemned, and the agent keeps sampling everything else, so the engine is option-specific
avoidance that seals a particular error, not a learned helplessness that generalises. It is distinct too from
Overfitting's fit to the noise in a given sample and The Streetlight Effect's search where the light is easy, in
that here the defect is neither a model that mistakes noise for signal nor a search drawn to the convenient
place, but a sample the learner itself truncates by declining to revisit what once went badly.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the learner as a
  bigot or a lazy judge, because the source finding it stages is that the bias follows from rational avoidance and
  not from prejudice: avoiding what has burned you is sound, and the asymmetry is a property of learning by
  sampling, not a flaw of character. The loss is authored by no one: it is the good option never given a second
  try and the person never met again after a bad first meeting, and the misfit is that the very prudence that
  stops the agent from repeating a painful experience is what keeps it from ever discovering the experience was a
  fluke.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The ways out exist, forcing
  exploration by sampling what burned you, giving second chances by policy, randomising trials, or borrowing
  others' experience of the option, but none is free: deliberate re-sampling spends real cost on options most of
  which truly are bad, to rescue the few that were only unlucky, and the agent cannot tell in advance which is
  which. The reckoning is that the asymmetry is inherent in learning by adaptive sampling, so wherever an agent
  avoids what it must sample to correct, the negative errors keep sealing themselves.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as
  the house's. The staging is the house's; the hot-stove effect, the sampling asymmetry, the impression-formation
  bias, and the endogenous risk aversion are Denrell's, with Denrell and March for the information-restriction
  form, the cat and the hot stove are Twain's image, and the law of effect is Thorndike's, credited as the
  reinforcement antecedent the effect extends into a bias. The mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding:
  that the bias requires outcomes to be variable and sampling to be the agent's own choice, that it softens where
  outcomes are near-deterministic so a bad draw truly means bad, where sampling is forced or exogenous, or where
  others' experience can be observed, and that most avoided options really are poor, so only the variable and
  unluckily-sampled are misjudged. The scope is held to learning by adaptive sampling under noise, and not
  stretched into a claim that all avoidance is error or all first impressions wrong.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Bad Draw**: carries the trigger (Denrell), the early negative outcome, real or merely unlucky, that turns
  the agent away from an option before its true worth is known.
- **The Flinch**: carries the rational avoidance (Denrell & March), the sound decision not to repeat what hurt,
  which is also the decision that stops the sampling and so censors the record the agent will learn from.
- **The Untested**: carries the sealed negative error (Denrell), the option never sampled again, whose real value
  is never learned, so an impression that was only unlucky stands uncorrected forever.
- **The Second Look**: carries the self-correcting arm (Denrell), the continued sampling of the option that
  looked good, which exposes a lucky first draw to regression and washes the positive error out, the correction
  the low side never gets, so the asymmetry is seen in one piece.
- **The Censored Sample**: carries the truncated record (Denrell & March), the evidence built only from what
  stayed sampled, cut off on the low side, from which the agent forms a picture biased downward.
- **The Downward Bias**: carries the harm and the bound (Denrell), the systematic undervaluation and the risk
  aversion it breeds, variable options shunned though some are better than they first seemed, real where outcomes
  are noisy and sampling is chosen and softening where they are not.
