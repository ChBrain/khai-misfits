# As Fast as the Train: Reference

The research warrant for As Fast as the Train.
The term is the house's; the play runs on the Downs-Thomson paradox, on the equilibrium between two modes,
and on the scale economies in service frequency that transmit the harm, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging a road widening as **a saving that is spent on making the alternative worse**: a lane is added, the
journey time falls exactly as the appraisal said it would, commuters who had been taking the train move to
the road because it is now quicker, the train loses the riders whose fares paid for its frequency, the
operator cuts two departures an hour, the door-to-door journey by train gets longer, and the road refills
until driving is no better than the alternative, which is now worse than it was before anybody touched
anything. The play models the documented mechanics, the appraisal that counts the saving at fixed mode
shares, the switch that is individually correct, the timetable that follows the farebox, the waiting time
that is part of the journey and rises as departures thin, and the equilibrium that equalises the two modes
at a level nobody chose, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a foolish engineer
or a stingy operator.

The governing law is Thomson's: the equilibrium speed of the road is set by the door-to-door speed of the
competing service. It is not a claim that roads do not work. It is a claim about where the road's speed
comes from. As long as commuters can switch, any advantage the road holds over the train is an advantage
that recruits drivers until it is gone, so the road settles at the alternative's speed and cannot settle
anywhere else. **Improve the road and you move drivers; the road's speed is decided by what happens to the
train.**

What makes the harm rather than a wash is the transmission, and it is Mohring's. Waiting is part of a
journey, so a service's quality depends on how often it runs, and how often it runs depends on how many
people are riding it. Ridership does not merely pay for the service, it **is** part of the service. So a
mode shift away from the train does not leave the train where it was: it thins the timetable, lengthens
the wait, and lowers the speed that the road's equilibrium is keyed to. The saving is not lost. It is
converted into a worse alternative and then handed back as a slower road.

What removes any possibility of blame is that every party is responding correctly to a real change. The
appraisal was honest and its arithmetic holds at the mode shares it assumed. The commuter who switched
took a genuinely quicker journey on the day they switched. The operator that cut two departures was
responding to a real fall in demand, and with waiting time in the cost the response is not merely
defensible but efficient: fewer riders do justify fewer buses. The council approved a scheme that
delivered what it promised. **Nobody cut the service. The service followed the riders, and the riders
followed the road.**

And the seat where the damage is decided is occupied by nobody. There is no party whose job is the
equalised journey time. The highway authority owns the road, the operator owns the timetable, the
commuter owns the choice, and the quantity that determines the outcome is a property of all three
together that none of them is asked about, appraises, or is accountable for.

It is distinct from the house's **The Extra Road**, which stages Braess and is the nearest neighbour:
there the quantity is a link added to a network where every traveller is routing on the same mode, and
the harm comes from the equilibrium routing itself, so stipulating a **single mode with no alternative**
leaves Braess entire and removes this play completely. Here there is nothing paradoxical about the routing
at all, and the harm needs a competing service that can lose riders.

It is distinct from the house's **Jevons Paradox**, where efficiency lowers the effective price and use
rises to consume the saving: there the quantity is the induced consumption, and stipulating that **the
number of journeys is fixed and none is induced** removes that harm. Stipulate it here and this harm
survives entirely, because the damage is done by a **shift between modes** at constant total travel, not
by extra travel.

It is distinct from the house's **The Brittle Optimum**, where slack is trimmed as idle waste until the
system shatters: there something is removed from a system that needed it. Here the timetable is not
trimmed against the service's interest, it is matched to demand, and the loss falls on the road rather
than on the operator, who has done nothing wrong and bears none of it.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the highway
  authority as short-sighted, the commuter as selfish or the operator as mean, because each is responding
  correctly to a real change: the scheme delivered the time saving it was appraised for, the switch was to
  a genuinely quicker journey on the day it was made, and the timetable was matched to the demand that
  remained, which with waiting time in the cost is the efficient response and not a retreat. The seat that
  decides the outcome, the equalised journey time across two modes, is occupied by nobody at all. The
  failure is authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that capacity added to
  one mode is spent on degrading the other.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Subsidising the
  frequency is the remedy the scale economies imply and it works, and it is a permanent claim on a budget
  for a benefit that appears as an absence. Reallocating the new lane to the buses converts the scheme
  into a different scheme and forfeits the saving the appraisal was approved on. Pricing the road reaches
  the equilibrium directly, is the answer the economics has given for a century, and is the one that is
  hardest to get agreed. Appraising the scheme with mode shares free rather than fixed is correct, costs
  more, and returns a smaller number to a committee that must still choose.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the transport economics is theirs. The bounds are
  carried with them, and they are wider here than in most of this house. **The paradox is conditional**:
  it requires a competing service with scale economies in frequency and an operator whose supply follows
  demand, so where the service is contractually fixed or generously subsidised the mechanism is blunted
  or absent. **It is contested empirically**: the strongest identified work on induced travel reports no
  effect of transit provision on vehicle travel and concludes against both roads and transit as remedies,
  which is evidence for absorption and not for this particular channel of it, and the play carries that
  rather than hiding it. The experimental support is a laboratory result about a designed environment.
  **The magnitudes those sources report are deliberately not carried here**, because the papers themselves
  could not be read in the pass that cited them: publisher hosts and the article archives are blocked to
  it, so the bibliography, the findings and their directions were verified by search and corroborated
  across independent reports, which is the treatment a citation survives and a reported figure is not.
  **The play makes no claim that roads should not be built**, only that a city with two modes settles the
  road's speed somewhere neither mode's owner decides.
