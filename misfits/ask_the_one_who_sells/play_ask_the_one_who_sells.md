# Play: Ask the One Who Sells

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Ask the One Who Sells.
You go to a doctor because you do not know what is wrong with you or what to do about it, and the doctor does. That is the whole point of the visit: to borrow an expertise you lack. But the doctor is not only your advisor. Under fee-for-service, the doctor is also the supplier, paid for each test ordered and each procedure done. So the one you trust to tell you what care you need is the same one who is paid to provide it, and you cannot check their work, because if you could, you would not have needed to ask. Robert Evans named what follows: supplier-induced demand. Because the provider both advises and sells, and the buyer cannot judge, the provider can recommend more care than a fully informed buyer would have chosen, and the demand for care is set, in part, by the supplier who meets it rather than by the patient who bears it (Evans).
And here is how it shows in the world. Milton Roemer put it in a law: a built bed is a filled bed. Where more hospital beds are built, more hospital days are used, not because more people fall ill but because the capacity, once there, is filled. Regions with more surgeons do more surgery, regions with more scanners do more scans, and the extra procedures do not buy matching gains in health, the wide variation from place to place tracking the supply and not the need (Roemer; McGuire). When fees are cut, providers have been seen to do more, not less, raising the volume to hold their income (McGuire).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The provider is not a fraud, and this is the heart of it. A doctor paid per service, asked what a patient needs, will lean at the margin toward more, and will usually believe it: more testing is more thorough, more treatment is safer, the extra scan rules out the rare disaster, so the induced care feels like diligence and good practice, not like selling. The patient is not a mark: deferring to an expert you cannot second-guess is the rational thing to do, the sound reliance on expertise that medicine is built on. And the payer is not a fool: it pays for real services really rendered. The loss is authored by no one. It is the care an informed buyer would have declined, delivered in good faith and paid for all the same, and the misfit is that the very trust that makes the expert worth consulting is what lets the expert who is also the seller set the demand.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The tilt comes from paying per service, so the remedy is to pay another way: a salary, or a fixed sum per patient enrolled, so that more care no longer means more pay. Break the link between the recommendation and the reward, and the pull to do too much is gone. But the same stroke plants the opposite pull. Paid a flat sum whatever they do, a provider now profits by doing less, by stinting, by turning the difficult patient away, so the cure for too much care is a fresh temptation to too little. Second opinions, guidelines, and review add cost and friction and can still be worked around. Every way of paying wants the wrong amount of care in one direction or the other.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. Supplier-induced demand is real and documented, in Roemer's beds and Wennberg's small-area variation and the target-income response, but its magnitude is genuinely debated, and not all care is induced: most of what a doctor does is needed, and the hard part is that the induced and the needed look alike, because the provider believes in both. The effect is a tendency in a structure, not a verdict on any one doctor or any one visit. Supplier-induced demand is Evans's, the bed-supply law Roemer's, the physician-agency formalisation and the target-income response McGuire's and the variation Wennberg's, and the foundational information asymmetry Arrow's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where you must ask the one who sells whether you need the thing, you will be sold more than you would have chosen, no fraud required, and no way of paying gets the amount exactly right.

## Arc

The bend is this: where the provider of a service is also the buyer's trusted advisor and is paid for each service rendered, and the buyer cannot judge what they need, the provider can recommend more than an informed buyer would choose and profits by doing so, often sincerely believing more is better, so demand is set in part by the supplier who meets it rather than by the buyer who bears it, and the service is over-supplied at rising cost without a matching benefit.
It runs on an expert who both advises and sells.
The provider is at once the buyer's agent, trusted to say what is needed, and the supplier, paid for each service done (Evans).
And the buyer cannot referee. The whole reason they came is that they cannot judge their own need, so they defer to the one who does, who is also the one who is paid (Arrow).
And so the provider can recommend more. Paid per service, and leaning sincerely to the safer, more thorough course, they recommend care an informed buyer would have declined (Evans; McGuire).
And the extra is rendered. Supply drives use, a built bed is a filled bed, so the capacity created is the care delivered and the payer foots the bill (Roemer).
And it is authored by no one. The provider believes in the care, the patient rightly trusts, and the payer pays for real work, so the over-supply is the structure's doing and not a fraud.
And the way out is dear. Pay by salary or head and the pull to do too much is gone, but the pull to do too little takes its place, so the cure trades one error for another (McGuire).
And the whole is held against its limit. The effect is real but debated in size, not all care is induced, and the induced and the needed look alike because the provider believes in both (McGuire).
