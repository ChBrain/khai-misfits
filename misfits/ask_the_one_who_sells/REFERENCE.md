# Ask the One Who Sells: Reference

The research warrant for **Ask the One Who Sells** (play and company not yet staged; this warrant is drafted
first, to prove the science and the distinction before authoring). The title is provisional, a reading of the
phenomenon health economics calls **supplier-induced demand**: where the provider of a service is also the trusted
advisor who tells the buyer what they need, and is paid for each service rendered, and the buyer cannot judge for
themselves, the provider can recommend more than an informed buyer would choose and profits by doing so, often
sincerely believing more is better, so demand is set in part by the supplier who meets it, all cited below, held
with the finding that the effect is real and documented in the small-area variation of medical use but its
magnitude is debated, and the remedy of paying by salary or head trades the risk of too much care for the risk of
too little.

## Line of Work

Staging supplier-induced demand as a **the-seller-sets-the-demand structure**: in medicine, and wherever an expert
both advises and sells, one person holds two roles that pull against each other. As the buyer's agent, the provider
is trusted to recommend the care the buyer needs; as the supplier, paid for each service, the provider profits from
recommending more. And the buyer cannot referee, because the whole reason they came is that they do not know what
they need, so they defer to the one who does, who is also the one who is paid. So the provider can recommend more
care than a fully informed buyer would have chosen, another test, another procedure, another day in a bed that was
built and so is filled, and each such recommendation is profitable and, more often than not, sincerely believed:
more feels safer, more thorough, more careful, so the extra care looks like good practice and not like selling. The
sum is care over-supplied, spending that climbs without a matching gain in health, and sometimes the harm of
treatment the buyer did not need, set in motion by no fraud, only by an incentive and an information gap that make
more the path of least resistance. The governing law is not that the provider is a cheat, but that **where the
provider of a service is also the buyer's trusted advisor and is paid for each service rendered, and the buyer
cannot judge what they need, the provider can recommend more than an informed buyer would choose and profits by
doing so, often sincerely believing more is better, so demand is set in part by the supplier who meets it rather
than by the buyer who bears it, and the service is over-supplied at rising cost without a matching benefit, no
fraud required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the dual role of advisor and supplier (Evans; McGuire),
the information gap that leaves the buyer unable to second-guess (the antecedent in Arrow), the fee that makes more
profitable, the induced care an informed buyer would have declined, the sincere benefit of the doubt that makes it
feel like good medicine, and the salary or capitation that would remove the fee incentive at a cost, as the
load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a greedy doctor bilking a helpless patient.

It is a supply-side agency structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from Moral Hazard, where insuring a risk lowers the care taken against it because the
insured no longer bears the loss: there the driver is the demand side, the insured party consuming more or
guarding less because the price to them has fallen, whereas here the driver is the supply side, the provider
inducing the quantity through an information advantage and a fee, and the buyer is led rather than lured by a low
price. It is distinct from Rent-Seeking, where real resources are spent competing to capture a transfer that
creates nothing: there the effort is pure waste chasing a slice of the pie, whereas here real services are actually
rendered, tests really run and procedures really done, only more of them than an informed buyer would have chosen,
so the harm is over-supply and not unproductive capture. And it is a specific case of the principal-agent problem
the house holds off as too broad to stage whole, cut here to the one instance no staged misfit carries: the agent
who is also the seller, advising the very purchase they profit from, distinct from Moral Hazard's hidden action and
Watermelon Reporting's filtered report, the two agency instances already staged.

## Origin

The phenomenon in practice, and the research spine beneath it.

| **Robert Evans**   | "Supplier-Induced Demand: Some Empirical Evidence and Implications" (in _The Economics of Health and Medical Care_, 1974)    | **The spine**: because the physician is at once the patient's agent and the supplier of care, and the patient cannot judge need, the physician can shift the demand curve outward, recommending care an informed patient would not have chosen; demand is set in part by the supplier. | **Milton Roemer**  | Roemer's Law, "a built bed is a filled bed" (from the 1961 study of hospital bed supply and use)                             | **The field observation**: hospital use rises to meet the beds available, so supply drives utilisation rather than the reverse, the classic empirical sign that demand follows what is built to be sold. | **Thomas McGuire** | "Physician Agency" (_Handbook of Health Economics_, 2000); the target-income and small-area-variation literatures (Wennberg) | **The modern formalisation and evidence**: the physician as an imperfect agent, the target-income response by which volume rises when fees are cut, and the wide regional variation in procedures without matching differences in outcome, with the effect's magnitude honestly debated. ## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the provider as a
  fraud or the buyer as a mark, because the source finding it stages is that the over-supply follows from the dual
  role and the information gap and not from deceit: a provider paid per service and asked what the buyer needs will,
  at the margin, lean toward more, and will usually believe it, since more testing and more treatment feel safer
  and more thorough, so the induced care looks like diligence and not like selling. The loss is authored by no one:
  it is the care an informed buyer would have declined, rendered in good faith and paid for all the same, and the
  misfit is that the very trust that makes the expert worth consulting is what lets the expert who is also the
  seller set the demand.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The way out exists, to break the
  link between the recommendation and the reward by paying the provider a salary or a fixed sum per head rather than
  per service, so that more care no longer pays, but it is not free: the same payment that removes the incentive to
  do too much creates an incentive to do too little, to stint and to turn patients away, and second opinions,
  guidelines, and utilisation review each add cost and friction and can still be gamed. The reckoning is that the
  conflict is inherent in one party both advising and selling, so wherever the buyer must trust the seller to say
  what they need, the demand can be induced.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as
  the house's. The staging is the house's; supplier-induced demand is Evans's, the bed-supply observation is
  Roemer's, the physician-agency formalisation and the target-income response are McGuire's and the small-area
  variation Wennberg's, and the foundational information asymmetry is Arrow's, credited narrowly to that asymmetry
  and not to the moral hazard the same paper also named and that the house stages separately. The mechanism is
  carried as carefully as the finding: that supplier-induced demand is real and documented but its magnitude is
  debated, that not all care is induced and much is genuinely needed, that the provider usually believes the extra
  care is warranted rather than knowing it is not, and that the salary remedy trades the risk of too much care for
  the risk of too little. The scope is held to the case of an expert who both advises and sells under fee-for-service
  and an information gap, and not stretched into a claim that all medical care is induced or all providers self-serving.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element. The play and its company are not yet authored; these are the proposed
load-bearing pieces the warrant commits the staging to carry, one per vector, no filler.

- **The Dual Role**: carries the conflict (Evans; McGuire), the provider who is at once the buyer's trusted advisor,
  meant to recommend what the buyer needs, and the supplier paid for each service, who profits from recommending
  more, two roles in one person that pull against each other.
- **The Information Gap**: carries the asymmetry (Arrow), the reason the buyer cannot referee the recommendation,
  since the whole reason they came is that they do not know what they need, so they defer to the one who does, who
  is also the one who is paid.
- **The Fee**: carries the incentive (Evans), the payment for each service rendered that makes recommending more
  profitable, and, when it is cut, moves the provider to raise the volume to hold their income.
- **The Extra**: carries the induced demand (Evans; Roemer), the care an informed buyer would have declined, the
  extra test, the extra procedure, the bed that was built and so is filled, real service rendered beyond need.
- **The Benefit of the Doubt**: carries the no-villain mechanism (McGuire), the sincere belief that more is safer
  and more thorough, so the induced care feels like good practice and not like selling, the reason no fraud is
  required for the demand to be induced.
- **The Salary**: carries the remedy and the bound (McGuire), the fixed payment by head or by post that removes the
  fee incentive to do too much, costly because it creates the opposite incentive to do too little, and the marker
  of the hedge, since the effect is real but debated in size and the cure trades one risk for another.
