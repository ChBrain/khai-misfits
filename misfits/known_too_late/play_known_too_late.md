# Play: Known Too Late

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Known Too Late.
A new technology enters the world soft, still plastic, its course open to being shaped or redirected or stopped at little cost. And at that early stage almost nothing can be known about what it will do at scale or to the society that takes it up, because those consequences do not yet exist to be measured; they emerge only as the technology is widely developed and used. This is the first horn, the information problem: when the technology can be controlled, not enough is known to warrant controlling it (Collingridge). So the young technology is let grow, reasonably, because there is no evidence yet of a harm that would justify restraining it.
And it grows. Investment sinks into it, other systems come to depend on it, standards and habits and whole industries settle around it, and it hardens. By the time its harmful consequences are finally apparent, plainly measurable, no longer deniable, the technology is so deeply entrenched that changing it is now slow, costly, and resisted by everyone built around it. This is the second horn, the power problem: when enough is known to warrant control, control has become expensive and difficult (Collingridge).
And here is the shape of the trap, which is not either horn but the coupling of the two. The power to steer and the knowledge of where to steer are inversely available across the technology's life: high power and low knowledge early, high knowledge and low power late, and they never meet in the middle. The clay is soft when you cannot yet see the form, and set hard by the time you can. That is the misfit: the harm no one could act on early is the harm no one can cheaply act on late.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The early decision to let the technology grow is the reasonable one, since no harm is yet knowable to act on, and it would be a strange rule that restrained every new thing on the mere chance of an unknown future harm. The later inability to change it is not a failure of will but the real weight of sunk investment and dependency, the cost of unmaking what a world has been built around. No one at either stage is negligent, because the knowledge and the power were simply never in the same hands at the same time. The loss is authored by no one.
And here is the way out, and why it is costly. The one real answer, Collingridge's own, is corrigibility: to build and adopt technologies so as to keep the later decision open, favouring the flexible and reversible over the efficient and committed, so that when the knowledge arrives the power to act has not wholly gone. But this is not free. It forgoes the gains of commitment, standardisation, and scale that lock-in buys, and it slows and complicates deployment; and the foresight that would bring the knowledge earlier, real-time and anticipatory assessment, is itself imperfect and costly and can never fully close the gap (Genus and Stirling; Guston and Sarewitz). The escape works against the grain of the very efficiency that early commitment offers, and none of it is a clean foreknowledge of harm.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The dilemma is a tendency and not an iron law. Its horns can be softened, since some harms are foreseeable through foresight and staged testing, and some entrenchments are reversible; the strength of the trap varies with how deeply a given technology locks in and how legible its harms are. The dilemma of control and its two horns and the corrigibility response are Collingridge's, the responsible-innovation reassessment Genus and Stirling's, the real-time and anticipatory-governance softening Guston and Sarewitz's, and the pacing problem that evidences the second horn Marchant and colleagues'. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how to change it are never in hand at the same time, so the harm is known too late to steer cheaply, and not that nothing can ever be governed or foreseen.

## Arc

The bend is this: when a technology is young it can still be steered but its harms cannot yet be known, and by the time its harms are plain it has grown too entrenched to steer cheaply, so the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how it should be changed are never in hand at the same time.
It runs on a life that hardens as it is understood.
A new technology enters the world soft, still plastic, open to being shaped or stopped at little cost.
And nothing can yet be known of its harms. The consequences do not exist to be measured, so control is possible but unwarranted, and it is let grow (Collingridge).
And it hardens. Investment sinks in, dependency grows, standards settle, and the thing sets into place.
And the knowledge arrives. The harms are at last plain and measurable, no longer deniable, fully in view.
And the wheel will not turn. Control has become slow and costly and resisted by all who are built around it, the power gone just as the knowledge comes (Collingridge).
And it is authored by no one. The early hands act reasonably on a harm not yet knowable, and the late hands meet the real weight of entrenchment, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is to build it to bend. Corrigibility, keeping the later decision open, costly and against the grain of commitment, and the bound held, the dilemma a tendency and its horns softened by foresight (Genus and Stirling; Guston and Sarewitz).

## Stakes

Whether a society can steer a technology to a good end, when the power to steer it and the knowledge of how it should be steered are never in hand at the same time.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the thing set and the harm arrived, and away from the cheap early steering that could have redirected it, because the knowledge of the harm and the power to act on it pass each other in the technology's life and never meet. The structure cannot both leave the early hands the knowledge they need and leave the late hands the power they need, because the knowledge is made only by the deployment that entrenches the thing, so to know is already to have hardened it: the same growth that reveals the harm is the growth that sets the technology beyond cheap reach. The way out, to build it to bend, keeping the later decision open through corrigibility and reversibility, spends a real cost, the gains of commitment and scale forgone and the imperfect price of foresight paid, and it is not the clean foreknowledge of harm anyone would wish for. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the early hands act reasonably on a harm not yet knowable and the late hands meet the real weight of entrenchment, and that the loss is the harm known too late to steer cheaply and not anyone's failure of nerve. And it holds the bound in view, because the dilemma is a tendency and not an iron law, its horns softened by real foresight and by technologies built to stay corrigible (Genus and Stirling), so the whole of the misfit is that the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how to change it are never in hand at the same time, and the clay is set hard by the time its form can be seen.
