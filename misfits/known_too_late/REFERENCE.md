# Known Too Late: Reference

The research warrant for **Known Too Late**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon named the
**Collingridge Dilemma**: when a technology is young it can still be steered but its harms cannot
yet be known, and by the time its harms are plain it has grown so entrenched that steering it is
slow and costly, so the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how it should be changed
are never in hand at the same time, all cited below, held with the finding that the dilemma is a
tendency and not an iron law, its horns softened by real foresight and by technologies built to stay
corrigible.

## Line of Work

Staging the Collingridge Dilemma as a **power-and-knowledge-never-coincide structure**: a new technology enters the world
soft, still plastic, its course open to being shaped, redirected, or stopped at little cost. But at that early stage almost
nothing can be known about how it will be used at scale or what it will do to the society that takes it up, because those
consequences do not exist yet to be measured; they emerge only as the technology is widely developed and deployed. This is
the first horn, the information problem: when the technology can be controlled, not enough is known to warrant controlling
it (Collingridge). So the young technology is let grow, reasonably, because there is no evidence yet of a harm to justify
restraining it. And it grows. Investment sinks into it, other systems come to depend on it, standards and habits and whole
industries settle around it, and it hardens. By the time its harmful consequences are finally apparent, plainly measurable,
no longer deniable, the technology is so deeply entrenched that changing it is now slow, costly, and resisted by everyone
built around it. This is the second horn, the power problem: when enough is known to warrant control, control has become
expensive and difficult (Collingridge). And here is the shape of the trap, which is not either horn but the coupling of the
two: the power to steer and the knowledge of where to steer are inversely available across the technology's life, high power
and low knowledge early, high knowledge and low power late, and they never meet in the middle. The clay is soft when you
cannot see the form, and set hard by the time you can. It is authored by no villain: the early decision to let the
technology grow is reasonable, since no harm is yet knowable to act on; the later inability to change it is not a failure of
will but the real weight of sunk investment and dependency; and no one at either stage is negligent, since the knowledge and
the power were simply never in the same hands at the same time. The governing law is not that anyone was reckless or timid,
but that **when a technology is young it can still be steered but its harms cannot yet be known, and by the time its harms
are plain it has grown too entrenched to steer cheaply, so the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how it
should be changed are never in hand at the same time, and the harm no one could act on early is the harm no one can cheaply
act on late, no villain required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the plastic young technology whose
consequences cannot yet be read, the early window of control spent in reasonable ignorance, the entrenchment that hardens it
against change, the late arrival of the knowledge that would have warranted steering, the resulting impotence of a control
that is now costly and slow, and the one partial answer of building the technology to stay corrigible and reversible so the
window never fully closes (Collingridge; Genus and Stirling), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of
a reckless inventor or a timid regulator who simply failed to look ahead.

It is a power-and-knowledge-never-coincide structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from Path Dependence and Lock-In, where an early and possibly inferior standard locks in as
switching costs compound, so the world is stuck with the worse of two options: there the engine is the compounding cost of
switching around an arbitrary early choice, and the harm is being held to an inferior standard, whereas here the engine is
the inverse timing of knowledge and power, and the harm is a governance failure over consequences that could not be known
when they could have been acted on. Lock-in is one mechanism of the late horn, the thing that makes control costly, but the
Collingridge structure is the wider inversion, and its wound is the unsteered harm and not the suboptimal standard. It is
distinct from Chesterton's Fence, where a safeguard is torn out because its purpose has been forgotten and the quiet harm
returns: there the knowledge existed once and was lost, so the remedy is to recover it before acting, whereas here the
knowledge never existed early to be forgotten, since the harm had not yet come into being, so no recovery of lost
understanding could have helped. And it is distinct from The Horizon Problem, where every plan ends at its instrument's edge
so what lies past it carries no cost and casts no shadow until it arrives: there the future is simply outside the frame,
never entering the plan, whereas here the knowledge of the harm does arrive, fully and inside the frame, but too late in the
technology's life to act on cheaply, the failure one of timing and entrenchment and not of a horizon that hides the future
from view.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the case as a reckless
  inventor who would not look ahead or a timid regulator who would not act, because the source finding it stages is that the
  failure is structural and not a fault of nerve or care: early, no harm is yet knowable to justify restraint, so letting
  the technology grow is the reasonable course; late, the harm is plain but the technology is entrenched in real sunk
  investment and dependency, so changing it is genuinely costly and slow. No one's negligence makes the window close. The
  loss is authored by no one: it is the harm that could not be known when it could have been steered, and cannot be cheaply
  steered once it is known, and the misfit is that the power to change a technology and the knowledge of how to change it are
  never in hand at the same time.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The one real answer, Collingridge's own,
  is corrigibility: to build and adopt technologies so as to keep the later decision open, favouring the flexible and
  reversible over the efficient and committed, so that when the knowledge arrives the power to act has not wholly gone. But
  this is costly, not free: it forgoes the gains of commitment, standardisation, and scale that lock-in buys, and it slows
  and complicates deployment; and anticipatory governance and technology foresight, which bring the knowledge earlier,
  are themselves imperfect and expensive and can never fully close the gap (Genus and Stirling; Guston and Sarewitz). The
  escape works against the grain of the very efficiency that early commitment offers, and none of it is a clean foreknowledge
  of harm.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the dilemma of control and its two horns and the corrigibility response are
  Collingridge's, the responsible-innovation reassessment is Genus and Stirling's, the real-time and anticipatory-governance
  softening of the first horn is Guston and Sarewitz's, and the pacing problem that evidences the second horn is Marchant and
  colleagues'. The mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding: the dilemma is a tendency and not an iron law. Its horns
  can be softened, since some harms are foreseeable through foresight and staged testing, and some entrenchments are
  reversible; the strength of the trap varies with how deeply a given technology locks in and how legible its harms are; and
  the claim is held to the structural inversion of knowledge and power over a technology's life, not stretched into a
  counsel of despair that nothing can ever be governed or that no harm can ever be foreseen.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Clay**: carries the young technology while it is still plastic, its course open to being shaped or redirected or
  stopped at little cost, the soft form before it sets.
- **The Blind Window**: carries the first horn (Collingridge), the early stage where the power to steer is high but the
  knowledge of where to steer is absent, since the consequences do not yet exist to be measured, so control is possible but
  unwarranted.
- **The Setting**: carries the entrenchment (Collingridge; Marchant), the sinking of investment and the growth of
  dependency and standards around the technology that harden it against change, the clay turning to stone.
- **The Reckoning**: carries the late arrival of knowledge, the harmful consequences at last apparent and measurable and no
  longer deniable, the form now plainly seen.
- **The Locked Wheel**: carries the harm and the second horn (Collingridge), the steering that has become slow and costly
  and resisted by all who are built around the technology, the wheel that will no longer turn, the knowledge arrived too
  late to act on cheaply.
- **The Corrigible Hand**: carries the remedy and the bound (Collingridge; Genus and Stirling; Guston and Sarewitz),
  building and adopting the technology to keep the later decision open, flexible and reversible over efficient and committed,
  at the cost of the gains of commitment, and the marker of the hedge, since foresight can soften the first horn in part and
  some entrenchments can still be undone, so the window need not close all the way.
