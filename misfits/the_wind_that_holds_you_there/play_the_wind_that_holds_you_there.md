# Play: The Wind That Holds You There

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Wind That Holds You There.
A sail is a wing. It makes its living going against the wind, which is the whole reason a vessel can go anywhere at all rather than only downwind.
The price of a wing is that it will not work aimed at the wind. There is an arc, perhaps forty-five degrees either side, into which the vessel cannot be steered.
Put a shore under her lee and that arc is aimed at the only way out.

## Arc

The bend is this: the same wind that sets a vessel toward a shore is the wind she must work against to leave it, and her ability to do that falls as the wind rises.
It runs on two facts, neither of them anybody's doing: a sail generates its force by having the wind across it and not on it, so there is a sector no vessel can be steered into; and the keel's resistance to being pushed sideways varies with the square of her speed through the water.
Take the wing first, because the play has to establish that it is **right**. Working to windward is what a sailing rig is for. It is the achievement, not the defect: without it a vessel goes where the wind sends her and nowhere else. A rig that could face the wind directly would be a rig that generated nothing.
Then the sector. The wing's price is an arc, and the arc is not a fault in any particular vessel: it is a property of how lift is made. **The vessel can go anywhere except at it.**
Then the geometry that turns a property into a danger. A shore to leeward is a shore the wind is blowing onto, and to leave it a vessel must go to windward. The shore is charted, fixed, and doing nothing. **Nothing has gone wrong yet and the trap is already complete.**
Then the hinge, which is what makes this a structure and not a hazard. **One variable moves both quantities.** As the wind rises the vessel is set down harder, and her ability to work off falls, by two mechanisms at once. Sail must be reduced, which is correct seamanship and removes the drive. And the seaway stops her: as her way falls, the keel's side force falls with the **square** of it, so the leeway rises to make up the difference. **She is pushed sideways by her own loss of speed**, with nobody steering at the shore and every hand doing the right thing.
Then the two readings, which are both correct at the same time. Carry sail and you have the drive to claw off, and you may lose the rig. Reduce sail and you keep the rig, and you go ashore. The master is not choosing between prudence and boldness. **Each reading is right about the danger it is looking at**, and there is no third position from which one of them is wrong.
Reach for a settlement and there is exactly one, and it was spent before the play opened. **Sea room**: the offing kept days ago, in fair weather, by somebody who did not yet know they would need it and could not have known. That works. It is the only thing that does, and it is not available to anybody standing on the deck in the gale, because it is not a manoeuvre. It is a decision that has already either been taken or not.

## Stakes

Whether a vessel can leave a place her own capability is aimed away from.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether the master is careless, because the production refuses to cast one, and not whether the vessel is badly found, because a well-found vessel is in exactly the same arc, but whether the thing that gives a sailing rig its power can be prevented from also fixing where that power cannot be pointed. The production cannot prevent it. It can only show that the wing is the achievement, that the sector is its price and not its flaw, that the shore is doing nothing at all, that one rising variable moves the danger and the remedy in opposite directions, that the vessel is set ashore by losing way rather than by being steered, that both readings on deck are correct at once, and that the one settlement is an offing kept in fair weather by somebody who could not yet know what it was for.
