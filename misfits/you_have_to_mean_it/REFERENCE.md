# You Have to Mean It: Reference

The research warrant for You Have to Mean It.
The term is the house's; the play runs on the deterrence paradoxes of the intention, anchored on Kavka and
the literature that answered him, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging deterrence as **an arrangement whose only product is a state of mind**: a threat deters only if the
other side believes it will be carried out, belief survives examination only if the intention is real, and a
threat that works is never carried out. So the entire output of the arrangement, across its whole life, is a
settled conditional intention to do something that on the day would be pointless. The play models the
documented mechanics, the signal that cannot verify itself, the reader whose care is what makes a performance
insufficient, the intention that is the deliverable rather than a means to one, the reason that is already
spent by the time the moment arrives, the hand tied in advance because nothing else holds, and the peace that
leaves no evidence, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of statesmen behaving badly.

The governing law is that every reason to carry the threat out is a reason to have intended it, and none of
them survives into the moment when acting is possible. Deterrence has one purpose, and an attack has already
defeated it, so on the morning after the act buys nothing. The threat is rational to make and irrational to
keep, and both parties can see that in advance, which is why the credibility problem is not solved by
resolve but only by removing the capacity to reconsider from the party who would reconsider.

What removes any possibility of blame from the deterred is that its care is the mechanism. A bluff would
serve if nobody examined it, and this is the one party with everything at stake in the examination. It cannot
be asked to look less hard, since looking hard is the whole of its position and the reason deterrence works
at all, so the trap is closed by a participant doing exactly what anybody would want it to do.

What removes it from the threatener is that the provisional version does not exist. An intention held on
condition that it may be reconsidered at the moment is not an intention, and is not read as one, and the
moment at which reconsidering would happen is precisely the moment at which reconsidering is correct. So the
reservation is not a qualification on the state of mind: it is its removal, and it is visible from the other
side.

And the moral rule breaks in the hand. It is wrong to intend what it would be wrong to do, which is sound
almost everywhere and is why intentions are answerable at all. Applied here it condemns the party whose
intention is the reason nothing happened, and it cannot be relaxed for a preventive purpose without licensing
anybody who claims a sufficient purpose, which is the exact move the principle exists to refuse. Neither
keeping it nor amending it is a small adjustment, so the play holds the case rather than resolving it.

It is distinct from the house's **Time Inconsistency**, where the plan optimal today is not optimal to keep
tomorrow, so good outcomes need a hand tied in advance: there the agent is tempted, the later self wants
something the earlier self did not, and the tie is against a preference that shifts. Here nothing shifts and
nobody is tempted. The reason to act is simply gone by the morning it could be acted on, so the tie is
against a correct judgement rather than a weak one, and what is being preserved is not a preference but a
state of mind that the moment would rightly dissolve.

It is distinct from the house's **Exactly How Far**, where a promise firm enough to reassure an ally is a map
of everything that was not promised: there the commitment is bounded and the injury is in the boundary, read
by an ally looking for what it does not cover. Here the commitment has no boundary to read, being total and
conditional, and the difficulty is not what was left out but that the thing promised is monstrous and must be
meant.

It is distinct from the house's **Too Dear to Fake**, where a hidden worth is proved only by a signal
expensive enough that a pretender would not send it: there the proof is an expenditure, something burned in
public, and the signal does its work by costing. Here nothing can be burned that would settle the question,
since any expenditure is available to a competent pretender, so the only thing that passes the examination is
the state of mind itself, which costs nothing to hold and everything to have.

It is distinct from the house's **Someone Will Catch Me**, where a certain rescue removes the reason to build
a footing, so the kindness that always catches you keeps you falling: there the promise is benign, the
promisor wants to keep it, and the harm is what its reliability does to the other party's care. Here the
promise is a threat, the promisor hopes never to keep it, and the harm is what its reliability requires of
the promisor.

It is distinct from the house's **The Arms Race**, where each spends more to get ahead but the prize is rank,
so the spending cancels and all stand higher at no one's gain: there the waste is material and continuous,
and every party is worse off. Here the arrangement can succeed completely, at no material cost after the
posture is built, and the thing spent is not money or position but the moral standing of whoever holds the
intention.

## Origin

The paradox in the literature, and the answers to it.

| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Gregory Kavka**               | "Some Paradoxes of Deterrence", Journal of Philosophy 75 (1978), 285 to 302    | **The spine**: the special deterrent situation, in which an agent must conditionally intend to harm the innocent in order to forestall a great wrong, and in which it can be right to form an intention it would be wrong to act on.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Kavka**                       | the wrongful intentions principle, and its failure in the deterrent case       | **The rule that breaks**: that it is wrong to intend what it would be wrong to do, sound almost everywhere, returning here that the party who prevented the catastrophe did the wrong thing.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| **Kavka**                       | the toxin puzzle (1983), and Moral Paradoxes of Nuclear Deterrence (1987)      | **The mechanism, isolated**: an intention that cannot be formed by an agent who knows the reason to act on it will be gone when the moment comes, stripped of the political case entirely.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| **David Gauthier**              | "Deterrence, Maximization, and Rationality" (Ethics, 1984)                     | **The reply**: whether an agent may rationally form and hold an intention whose execution would not maximise, and what that requires of a theory of rational choice.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Thomas Schelling**            | The Strategy of Conflict (1960), canon                                         | **The strategic frame**: commitment as the removal of one's own options, and the credibility of a threat resting on the inability to reconsider rather than on resolve.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| **The standing dispute**        | the moral status of the deterrent intention                                    | **The live argument**: whether the conditional intention is culpable at all, whether a state's posture is an intention in the sense the principle governs, and whether the paradox dissolves under a different account of intention.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                        |
| **Reinhard Selten**             | "The chain store paradox" (Theory and Decision 9:2, 1978)                      | **The same engine in an economic setting**: a monopolist facing entrants in a known number of towns should not fight in the last, because there is no later entrant to deter, and the argument then unravels backwards to the first, so deterrence is unavailable to a party everybody can see reasoning correctly. The reason to carry the threat out has expired at every point where carrying it out is possible, which is this play's law arriving from the other side. |
| **David Kreps & Robert Wilson** | "Reputation and imperfect information" (Journal of Economic Theory 27:2, 1982) | **The resolution, and what it costs**: a monopolist facing a succession of entrants who observe earlier moves can sustain a reputation for fighting once the entrants hold some doubt about which kind of monopolist they face. The deterrent is restored by the other side not being certain, which is a repair for the sequence and not for this play: a single conditional intention has no succession to build a reputation across.                                     |

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the threatener
  as callous or the deterred as aggressive, because nobody in the sequence has a decision to get right: the
  signal cannot verify itself, so a performance fails against a reader who examines it; the reader has
  everything at stake in examining it and cannot be asked to do less; the provisional intention does not
  exist and would be read as absent if it did; the reason to act is spent by the time the moment arrives, so
  the tie against reconsidering is a tie against a correct judgement; and the moral rule that would settle it
  returns a verdict against the party whose intention prevented the harm. The cost is authored by nobody and
  emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that the only thing the arrangement ever manufactures is a
  state of mind, and the state of mind is the objectionable part.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Meaning it less is read as
  meaning it less, which is the failure. Meaning it while reserving the right to reconsider is not meaning
  it, and the reservation is visible from outside. Tying the hand works and guarantees that if the threat is
  ever tested the act follows from nobody's decision, since reversibility is exactly what the other side
  looks for. Standing down is available, keeps the intention clean, and hands the decision to whoever is
  still willing to make it, which is a real option at a real price rather than a way of having both.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the paradoxes, the puzzle, the replies and four decades
  of argument are theirs. The bounds are carried with them: whether a conditional deterrent intention is
  culpable at all is disputed, and several answers hold that it is not, so the play stages the structure of
  the difficulty rather than endorsing the verdict; whether a state's declared posture is an intention in the
  sense the wrongful intentions principle governs is itself contested, since institutions do not hold
  intentions the way persons do, and the play stages the personal case where the paradox is sharpest;
  Kavka's toxin puzzle is a philosopher's construction rather than an observed case, and is cited as an
  isolation of the mechanism and not as evidence of it; and the empirical question of whether nuclear
  deterrence has in fact prevented war is unsettled and is not a claim this play makes or needs. **The play
  takes no position on any deterrent posture, past or present**, only that a threat which must be meant to
  work, and which works by never being carried out, produces nothing else at all.
- **What the chain store rows add, and what they do not**: they are the same engine reached from economics
  rather than from moral philosophy, which is why they are Origin rows here and not a second play. The
  candidate was put to the switch-off in both directions before being taken: stipulate that carrying the
  threat out is worth doing on the day for its own sake and the monopolist fights in the last town as readily
  as the first, the induction never starts, and the chain store difficulty **vanishes entirely**; stipulate an
  unknown or unbounded horizon, which is the chain store's own distinctive element, and reputation becomes
  sustainable while a single conditional threat is still irrational to keep. The incumbent survives the
  candidate's engine being switched off and the candidate does not survive the incumbent's, which is what
  makes it a row. What the rows do not do is transfer the repair: Kreps and Wilson restore the deterrent
  across a **succession** of entrants who watch each other, and the case this play stages has no succession
  to build a reputation across, so the answer that works there is unavailable here rather than overlooked.
  **The formal statements are deliberately not quoted**, because the papers themselves could not be read in
  the pass that cited them: publisher hosts and the article archive are blocked to it, so the bibliography,
  the results and their directions were verified by search and corroborated across independent reports, which
  is the treatment a citation survives and a theorem statement is not.
