# Play: True of No One

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

True of No One.
A trial is run properly. Thousands of patients, randomised, followed, counted. At the end there is a number: the treatment reduces the bad outcome by so much, and the number is correct. It is not a guess, not a claim, not an opinion. It is the thing the trial was built to measure and the thing it measured.
And it is an average. That word does a great deal of quiet work. The patients in the trial were not interchangeable: some arrived at high risk of the outcome and some at low, some with the biology the drug acts on and some without, and the single figure at the end is what you get when all of that is added up and divided. The trial reports one number because one number is what a trial of that design can validly produce, and this is not a shortcoming to be scolded. Ask it for more and you get subgroup claims that do not replicate, which the literature has spent decades learning to distrust, and rightly (Rothwell).
And the spread inside the average is not gentle. Risk of the outcome is usually skewed across a population, so a minority at genuinely high risk stand to gain a great deal from the treatment while the low-risk majority stand to gain very little. Harms and costs, meanwhile, tend to fall much more evenly, since a side effect does not check your baseline risk before arriving. Put those together and the arithmetic is stark: most of the total benefit in the trial was collected by a minority of its participants, and the average was lifted by them (Kent, Hayward and colleagues).
And then the guideline is written. A panel takes the average and turns it into a recommendation, because a recommendation is what clinical practice can actually use: one rule, statable, followable, auditable. A guideline hedged into fifty conditional branches is not a guideline, and a panel that wrote one would be replaced by one that did not. So the rule goes out in the form the average supports, which is the form that applies to everyone.
And a clinician follows it. In front of them is a person, and the rule says treat, and the rule is drawn from the best evidence there is. Departing from it on a feeling about this particular patient is exactly the thing evidence-based medicine was built to stop, and the clinician who does it is not being sensitive, they are being the thing the whole discipline was a reaction against. So the clinician treats, and is right to, and the patient may be someone for whom the treatment was never going to do much.
And the loss is authored by no one, and it is not small. A low-risk patient takes a drug for years, carries its side effects, pays its costs, and collects a benefit close to nothing, while the guideline that put them there was drawn from a trial where the benefit was real and mostly went to somebody else. Nobody misreported, nobody misapplied, nobody was careless. The average was true of the group and true of hardly anyone in it, and every step from the trial to the prescription was the correct step to take.
And here is what corrects it, and why it costs. Stop asking what the treatment does on average and ask what it does at each level of risk: build a model that predicts a patient's baseline risk, then read the trial's effect within strata of that prediction rather than one variable at a time, and the minority who collect the benefit become visible and separable from the majority who do not (Kent, Hayward and colleagues). It works, and it is expensive. It wants individual participant data rather than published summaries, often pooled across trials that were never designed to be pooled. It wants the risk model externally validated, or it simply relocates the overfitting. And when it is done well it still hands back a probability rather than an answer, so the clinician is left deciding for a person who remains, at the end of all of it, an individual the evidence can only describe in distribution.
And so the misfit is not that trials mislead but that a valid average is the wrong shape for the decision it is used to make. The naming and the account of heterogeneity of treatment effects are Kravitz, Duan, and Braslow's; the finding that outcome risk is skewed so benefit concentrates in a minority, and the risk-stratified analysis that recovers it, are Kent, Hayward, and colleagues'; and the reason the cheap version fails, that conventional one-variable-at-a-time subgroup analysis produces findings that do not hold, is Rothwell's. The house stages the gap between a true average and an individual decision and holds the bound in view: that it needs a genuinely heterogeneous population and a trial unable to resolve who is who, that every actor is doing the disciplined thing, and that it lifts under risk-stratified analysis at a real and recurring price, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that a number can be right about everyone together and wrong about each of them apart.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a treatment's effect varies across a population and a trial can validly estimate only the average of that variation, the average becomes the guideline, the guideline becomes the prescription, and patients who stood to gain almost nothing are treated as though they were the average, carrying the harms while the benefit that justified the rule accrued mostly to a minority, with no one misreporting and no one misapplying.
It runs on a population that differs.
Patients vary in baseline risk of the outcome and in the biology the treatment acts on, so the effect is not one quantity but a distribution across them (Kravitz, Duan and Braslow).
And a trial that can report one number. Randomisation delivers a valid estimate of the average effect and not of any individual's, and asking the design for more yields subgroup claims that do not replicate, so reporting the average is the honest ceiling and not a failure of nerve (Rothwell).
And a spread that is skewed. Outcome risk is unevenly distributed, so a high-risk minority stand to gain substantially while the low-risk majority gain little, and harms fall far more evenly across both (Kent, Hayward and colleagues).
And a rule written from the average. A guideline must be statable and followable to be a guideline at all, so the panel issues one recommendation in the form the evidence supports, which is the form that applies to everyone.
And a clinician who follows it. Departing from the best validated evidence on a feeling about this patient is the very thing the discipline exists to prevent, so applying the rule is the disciplined act and not the lazy one.
And the many are treated for the benefit of the few. A low-risk patient carries years of side effects and cost for a gain near zero, while the benefit that justified the rule was collected mostly by someone else in the trial.
And the loss is authored by no one. No step was misreported, misapplied, or careless, and the average was true of the group and true of hardly any member of it.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs real heterogeneity and a trial unable to resolve it, and lifts under risk-stratified analysis, which wants pooled individual data, external validation, and still returns a probability rather than an answer (Kent, Hayward and colleagues).

## Stakes
