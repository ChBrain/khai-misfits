# Play: Long After the Cause

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Long After the Cause.
A service runs well. Requests come in, the machines answer them, the caches are warm, the queues are short, and there is margin between the load it carries and the load it could carry. Every board is green. And the thing nobody has any reason to know, because the system has never once been there, is that this is not the only place the system will sit. There is a second resting point beneath the first: a state in which the machines are just as up and the processes just as busy and almost nothing gets served, and it is every bit as stable as the good one. The system is not built with two floors on purpose. It simply has them, the way a hill has two hollows, and the whole of ordinary operation happens in the upper one (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And then something small happens. A garbage collection pause on a few machines. A deploy that restarts a tier and leaves its cache empty. A dependency that slows down for ninety seconds. A burst of traffic that is large but not enormous. None of these is a disaster, and in a system with room to spare none of them would be remembered. This one is remembered because it lands while the service is running in its vulnerable band, high enough that the margin is thin, and it pushes latency past the point where requests start to time out (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And here is where everyone does the right thing. A client whose request timed out retries it, because that is what a client is supposed to do with a request that failed and might well succeed on a second attempt: every guide says so, every library ships it by default, and a client that gave up on the first timeout would be the badly written one. A cache that was emptied by the restart goes to the backend for what it does not have, because that is what a cache is for. And the backend accepts the connections and works the queue in front of it, because refusing work it has the capacity to attempt is not something a server is built to do. Not one of these is a mistake. Not one of them would be worth changing if you saw it in isolation (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy).
And together they multiply the work. Each timed-out request comes back as two, then as three. Each cache miss lands on a backend that is now too busy to answer fast enough for the cache to fill, so the cache stays cold and every request keeps going through. The backend is now spending much of its effort on requests whose callers have already given up waiting, work that will be thrown away the moment it finishes, and that dead work crowds out the live work behind it. The service is running at full tilt and delivering a fraction of what it delivered an hour ago, and the harder every part of it tries, the less comes out (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu; Huang, Magnusson and colleagues).
And then the trigger goes away. The pause ends, the deploy finishes, the slow dependency recovers, the burst subsides. On the graph the spike is a bump that finished twenty minutes ago. The load arriving at the front door is back to what it was on an ordinary afternoon, the load the service handled all last week without noticing. And the service does not come back. It sits exactly where it is, pinned, because the loop that is now feeding it does not need the trigger any more: the retries are generated by the timeouts, and the timeouts are generated by the load, and the load is mostly retries. The cause has been gone for twenty minutes and the failure has not moved (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And the reason waiting does not work is a gap in the numbers. The load that tipped the system over and the load at which it could climb back out are not the same load, and they are not close. Going in, it took a spike above the ordinary to cross the line. Coming out, ordinary is nowhere near low enough, because the system must now serve the real requests and the amplified ones together, and only when arriving load falls far below the level that broke it does the loop starve and the system climb back to the upper floor. Between those two levels lie both states at once, and which one the system is in depends on where it has been, not on where the load is now (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And the loss is authored by no one. The client that retried was correct and would be criticized for doing otherwise; the cache that fetched what it lacked was doing its only job; the backend that took the work in front of it was behaving as every server behaves; and the operator watching a passed spike and a flat-lined service is not slow, since the one reading that would explain it, that the system is now holding itself down, is the one reading the graphs do not show. There is no bug to find. Every component would pass every test it has, individually, right now, while the service is down.
And here is what breaks it, and why it costs. Take the load away, hard: shed traffic at the edge, refuse a large share of requests outright, drain the queues of dead work, turn the retries off, and the loop starves and the system climbs back (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy). That is the fix, and it means deliberately refusing users the system could physically have served, which is the one thing every instinct in an operator resists, and it must usually go further and last longer than feels reasonable, because anything short of the lower threshold leaves the loop fed. Or pay standing: carry headroom that sits idle on every quiet day, budget retries so a client can only ever add a bounded fraction of extra work, cap queues so dead work is dropped rather than served. Each of these works and each is a real bill, paid always against a failure that comes rarely, and none of them removes the second floor: they only move how hard it is to fall to it.
And so the misfit is not that systems break under load but that a failure can outlive its cause, kept alive by the correct behaviour of everything inside it. The naming of metastable failure, the vulnerable state, the trigger, and the sustaining effect is Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu's; the survey of how these run in production is Huang, Magnusson and colleagues'; the practitioner account of overload, cascading failure, and the load shedding that alone breaks the spiral is the site reliability literature's; and the ancestor of the whole shape, retransmission feeding the congestion that caused it, is Nagle's and Jacobson and Karels's. The house stages the failure that outlasts its trigger and holds the bound in view: that it needs work amplification and a system already in its vulnerable band, that every actor inside it is behaving correctly, and that it lifts where load is shed, retries are budgeted, or headroom is carried, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that when the work a failure makes is enough to cause the failure, the cause is free to leave.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a system running near its vulnerable band is tipped past a threshold by a transient trigger, and where the response to failure inside it amplifies the work, the feedback loop that results is self-sustaining, so the system settles into a second stable state and stays there after the trigger is gone, and the load at which it could recover lies far below the load that broke it, with every component behaving correctly throughout.
It runs on two stable states, not one.
A system has a healthy operating point and a jammed one, both of them stable, and ordinary operation happens entirely in the first, so the second is never seen and never planned for (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And a vulnerable band. Running near enough to capacity, the system sits where a trigger could cross the threshold, high enough for the margin to be thin though nothing is yet wrong (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And a trigger that passes. A pause, a deploy, a slow dependency, a burst: brief, unremarkable, and gone in minutes, but enough to push latency past the point where requests time out.
And a correct response that multiplies work. The client retries because a failed request should be retried, the cold cache fetches because that is a cache's job, and the backend takes what is in front of it, so each failure returns as more load (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy).
And the loop feeds itself. Timeouts make retries, retries make load, load makes timeouts, and the backend spends its effort on work whose callers have already gone, so full effort yields a fraction of the goodput.
And the cause departs and nothing changes. The spike is over and arriving load is ordinary again, but the loop no longer needs the trigger, so the system stays pinned exactly where it was (Bronson, Aghayev, Charapko and Zhu).
And a gap holds it there. The load that would let it recover lies far below the load that broke it, so between the two thresholds both states exist and which one holds depends on where the system has been, not on the load now.
And the loss is authored by no one. Every component passes its own tests while the service is down, the retry is correct, the fetch is correct, the acceptance is correct, and there is no bug to find.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs work amplification and a vulnerable band, and lifts under load shedding, retry budgets, queue caps, or standing headroom, none of them free and none removing the second state (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy; Huang, Magnusson and colleagues).

## Stakes

Whether a system that has lost nothing and broken nothing can return to serving, once the work its own failure generates is enough to keep that failure going.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward a service that recovers, which every component is trying its hardest to deliver, and toward a service pinned at its lower floor, because the retry that is correct, the fetch that is correct, and the acceptance that is correct together multiply the work past what the capacity can clear. The production cannot make the system recover by waiting, because the loop no longer draws on the trigger that started it: the timeouts that generate the retries are generated by the retries, so the failure and its cause are the one loop seen twice, and removing the original cause removes nothing. The remedy, shedding load hard enough and long enough to starve the loop, spends exactly what the operator most wants to protect, refusing requests the machines could physically have served, and it must usually go further than feels warranted since anything above the lower threshold keeps the loop fed; and the standing alternatives, retry budgets, capped queues, and carried headroom, are real bills paid on every quiet day against a failure that comes rarely, none of which removes the second state and each of which only sets how hard it is to fall there. It can only show that nothing is broken and no one erred, that every component would pass its own tests at the moment the service is down, that the client retrying and the cache fetching and the backend accepting are each doing the one right thing available to them, and that the outage is a loop and not a fault. And it holds the bound in view, because the trap is conditional and not universal: it needs work amplification and a system already running in its vulnerable band, and it lifts under load shedding, budgeted retries, capped queues, or standing headroom (Beyer, Jones, Petoff and Murphy; Huang, Magnusson and colleagues), so the whole of the misfit is that when the work a failure makes is enough to cause the failure, the cause is free to leave.
