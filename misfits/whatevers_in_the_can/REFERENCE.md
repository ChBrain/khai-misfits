# Whatever's in the Can: Reference

The research warrant for **Whatever's in the Can**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls
the **garbage can model of organizational choice**: that in an organized anarchy, an organization with
ambiguous goals, unclear means, and a shifting cast, decisions are not the end of a march from problem
to solution but the chance product of four streams, problems, solutions, participants, and choice
opportunities, each flowing on its own clock, so a choice gets made by whatever happened to be present
when the occasion opened, and the solution attached had little to do with the problem it nominally
answered. It is held with the finding that the dynamic is conditional on the organized-anarchy
conditions and on loose structure, since clear goals, understood means, stable participation, or an
imposed access and timing each pull the process back toward ordered choice.

## Line of Work

Staging the garbage can as a **decisions-couple-by-timing structure**: an organization is expected to
produce decisions, but its goals are ambiguous, its means are not well understood, and who takes part
comes and goes. In such a place, the tidy picture, a problem arises, options are weighed, the best
solution is chosen, does not hold. Instead four things flow independently: problems, the concerns of
people inside and out, looking for somewhere to be aired; solutions, answers that exist before any
question, a pet proposal, a ready framework, a product looking for a use; participants, who drift in and
out as their attention and time allow; and choice opportunities, the occasions on which the organization
is expected to decide (Cohen, March, and Olsen). A choice opportunity is a can into which the
participants who happen to be there tip whatever problems and solutions they are carrying, and the
decision that comes out turns on that mix: on who was in the room and what they brought, not on the fit
of a solution to a problem. So a choice is often made not by resolving anything but by oversight, decided
quickly before problems attach, or by flight, the problems drifting off to a more inviting can and freeing
the choice to be made without them, so the decision and the problem it was meant to address come apart
(Cohen, March, and Olsen). The ordinary expectation is that organizations solve their problems by
choosing; the finding is that choices and problems are only loosely coupled, joined by simultaneity more
than by logic. The governing law is that **in an organized anarchy, decisions are produced by the
temporal coincidence of independent streams of problems, solutions, participants, and choice
opportunities, so a choice attaches whatever solution and problem happened to be present when it opened,
and is often reached by oversight or flight rather than by resolving the problem, which goes unsolved or
migrates elsewhere, with no one deciding irrationally and no one in error**. The play models the
documented mechanics: the four streams and the organized-anarchy conditions (Cohen, March, and Olsen),
the three decision styles of resolution, oversight, and flight (Cohen, March, and Olsen), the reading of
the university as the paradigm case (Cohen and March), and the policy-streams extension in which a window
opens and a waiting solution is coupled to a problem (Kingdon), as the load-bearing vectors of the
structure, and not as a claim that organizations never solve anything by choosing.

It is an organizational-choice structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its
nearest neighbours. It is distinct from The Law of the Instrument, where one mastered tool reshapes every
problem into the shape it can strike: there a single tool drives the fit, applied because it is the tool
in hand, whereas here a solution attaches by the coincidence of streams, present when the can opened, and
no one tool is doing the reshaping, only the timing of arrivals. It is distinct from Everybody's
Business, diffusion of responsibility, where a decision everyone could own is dropped because each assumes
another holds it: there the failure is that no decision is made, whereas here decisions are made,
promptly and often, they simply do not track the problems, coupling to whatever coincided rather than
going unowned. It is distinct from The Colour of the Bike Shed, where a group scrutinises the trivial it
understands and waves through the vital it does not: there the sorting is by triviality and
comprehensibility, whereas here it is by temporal coincidence, a choice loaded with whatever streams met
it regardless of weight. It is distinct from No One in Charge, the tyranny of structurelessness, where a
leaderless group hides power in an informal elite: there the engine is concealed authority, whereas here
there may be a plain chair and plain authority, and the decoupling comes from the independent flow of the
streams and not from who secretly holds the power.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the one
  who brings a ready solution as a schemer or the chair who closes a decision as a shirker, because the
  finding it stages is that each acts reasonably: a person with a good answer looks for a place to use
  it, a person with a concern looks for a forum to raise it, and a chair asked to produce a decision
  produces one from what is in front of it (Cohen, March, and Olsen). The one who would impose structure
  is not a bureaucrat for trying. The loss is authored by no one: it is the independent flow of the four
  streams, so a choice couples to whatever happened to be present, and the problem it was meant to answer
  is left behind by oversight or flight, a decoupling produced by timing and not by anyone's bad judgement.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The coupling can be
  tightened, but not for free: an access structure that decides which problems and solutions may enter
  which choices, a decision structure that sets who may act on what, deadlines that time the streams, all
  can pull the process back toward resolving problems (Cohen, March, and Olsen; March and Olsen), but each
  is costly to design and maintain, narrows the openness that let good unplanned couplings happen too, and
  cannot be fully imposed on a place whose goals stay ambiguous and whose participation stays fluid. The
  reckoning is that where goals are unclear and attention comes and goes, decisions will couple by timing,
  and buying them back toward problem-solving spends structure the organized anarchy resists.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the garbage can model and its four streams and three
  decision styles are Cohen, March, and Olsen's, the broader account of ambiguity is March and Olsen's, the
  university as paradigm case is Cohen and March's, and the policy-window extension is Kingdon's; the
  bounded-rationality neighbour is Simon's and is drawn to mark the line and to support. The mechanism is
  carried as carefully as the finding: that the dynamic needs the organized-anarchy conditions of ambiguous
  goals, unclear technology, and fluid participation, that the streams are genuinely independent, that a
  choice couples to what coincided rather than to what fits, that oversight and flight decide as often as
  resolution, and above all that it is conditional, since clear goals, understood means, stable
  participation, or an imposed structure each pull the process back toward ordered choice. The scope is held
  to decision-making in organized anarchies, and not stretched into a claim that all organizational choice
  is random or that no problem is ever solved by deciding.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Problem**: carries the concern looking for a choice to attach to (Cohen, March, and Olsen), a
  stream that flows on its own, aired where a forum opens and carried off again when none does, independent
  of any solution.
- **The Solution**: carries the answer that exists before its question (Cohen, March, and Olsen; Kingdon),
  a pet proposal, a ready framework, a product looking for a use, waiting for an occasion to be deployed
  whatever the problem of the day.
- **The Can**: carries the choice opportunity itself (Cohen, March, and Olsen), the occasion on which the
  organization is expected to decide, into which the participants present tip whatever problems and
  solutions they carry.
- **The Coincidence**: carries the engine, the temporal coupling by which a decision is produced (Cohen,
  March, and Olsen; Kingdon), so what is decided turns on who was in the room and what they brought when the
  can opened, not on the fit of a solution to a problem.
- **The Decoupling**: carries the phenomenon whole, the problem and the choice coming apart (Cohen, March,
  and Olsen), a decision reached by oversight or flight that attaches a solution unrelated to the problem,
  which goes unsolved or migrates to another can.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge, that the dynamic needs organized anarchy and loose
  structure and lifts under clear goals, understood means, stable participation, or an imposed access and
  timing (Cohen, March, and Olsen; March and Olsen), so the trap is real but bounded and not a claim that
  choice is always chaos.
