# Play: Whatever's in the Can

## Estate

## Name

Whatever's in the Can.
A committee meets and a decision comes out, cleanly, on time: a proposal adopted, a direction set, an item closed and minuted. It looks like the end of a march from problem to solution, the room having weighed the options and chosen the best. But watch what actually happened, and the march is not there. What got decided turned on who happened to be in the room and what they happened to be carrying, and the solution that was adopted had been waiting for an occasion like this one long before the problem of the day arrived.
And here is the shape beneath the meeting. This is an organized anarchy: the organization's goals are ambiguous, its means are not well understood, and who takes part comes and goes with attention and time. In such a place four things flow, and they flow independently. Problems drift about looking for a forum: the concerns of people inside and out, wanting somewhere to be aired. Solutions drift too, and this is the strange part: an answer can exist before its question, a pet proposal, a ready framework, a product looking for a use, waiting for a problem to attach to. Participants come and go. And choice opportunities open: the occasions on which the organization is expected to decide (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And a choice opportunity is a can. When it opens, the participants who happen to be present tip into it whatever problems and solutions they are carrying, and the decision that comes out is a function of that mix: of who was in the room and what they brought, not of how well a solution fits a problem. A solution that had been looking for a use finds a choice to attach to; a problem that was looking for a forum either lands in this can or drifts on to the next. The coupling is by timing, not by logic (Cohen, March, and Olsen; Kingdon).
And so decisions and problems come apart. A choice is often reached not by resolving anything but by oversight, decided quickly before the problems can attach, or by flight, the problems drifting off to a more inviting can and leaving the choice free to be made without them. Either way the decision is made and the problem it was meant to address is not solved: it is still out there, in another can, or in none. The solution that was adopted answers a question that was never really asked here (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And the loss is authored by no one. The one who brought a ready solution is not a schemer: a person with a good answer sensibly looks for a place to use it. The one who raised a concern is not a fool. The chair who closed the decision is not a shirker: asked to produce a decision, it produced one from what was in front of it. Each acted reasonably, and the decoupling is the work of the streams, not of anyone's bad judgement, so the meeting reads as productive and the problems quietly do not get solved.
And here is what tightens the coupling, and why it costs. Structure: an access rule that decides which problems and solutions may enter which choices, a decision rule that sets who may act on what, deadlines that time the streams so the right things meet (Cohen, March, and Olsen; March and Olsen). It helps, and it is dear: it must be designed and maintained, it narrows the openness that let good unplanned couplings happen too, and it cannot be fully imposed on a place whose goals stay ambiguous and whose people keep coming and going. So the coupling can be bought back toward problem-solving, but only with structure the organized anarchy resists.
And so the misfit is not that organizations are chaotic but that choice and problem are only loosely joined. The four streams and the styles of resolution, oversight, and flight are Cohen, March, and Olsen's, the account of ambiguity is March and Olsen's, the university as the plainest case is Cohen and March's, and the policy window where a waiting solution meets a problem is Kingdon's. The house stages the coupling by timing and holds the bound in view: that it needs ambiguous goals, unclear means, and fluid participation, and lifts where those settle or a structure times the streams, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that a decision made from whatever was in the can is a decision fitted to the timing and not to the problem.

## Arc

The bend is this: in an organized anarchy, decisions are produced by the temporal coincidence of independent streams of problems, solutions, participants, and choice opportunities, so a choice attaches whatever solution and problem happened to be present when it opened, and is often reached by oversight or flight rather than by resolving the problem, which goes unsolved or migrates elsewhere, with no one deciding irrationally.
It runs on an organized anarchy.
The organization's goals are ambiguous, its means are not well understood, and its participants come and go, so the tidy march from problem to chosen solution does not hold (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And four streams flow independently. Problems looking for a forum, solutions looking for a use, participants drifting in and out, and choice opportunities opening, each on its own clock and none marching in step with the others (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And a choice opportunity is a can. When it opens, the participants present tip in whatever problems and solutions they carry, so the decision is loaded by who was in the room and what they brought (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And the decision couples by timing. What gets decided turns on the mix that coincided, not on the fit of a solution to a problem, so a waiting solution attaches to the choice that happened to open (Cohen, March, and Olsen; Kingdon).
And problems and choices come apart. The choice is reached by oversight or by flight, the problems decided past or drifting off, so the decision is made and the problem it addressed is left unsolved, in another can or in none (Cohen, March, and Olsen).
And the decoupling is authored by no one. The solution-bearer, the problem-bearer, and the chair each act reasonably, so the meeting reads as productive while the problems quietly go unsolved, a loss with no wrongdoer.
And structure can tighten the coupling, at a cost. Access rules, decision rules, and deadlines can time the streams so the right things meet, but each is costly, narrows the useful openness, and cannot be fully imposed where goals stay ambiguous (Cohen, March, and Olsen; March and Olsen).
And the whole is held against its own limit. The dynamic is conditional: it needs organized anarchy and loose structure, and lifts under clear goals, understood means, stable participation, or an imposed access and timing (Cohen, March, and Olsen).

## Stakes
