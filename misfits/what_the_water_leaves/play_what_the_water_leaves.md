# Play: What the Water Leaves

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

What the Water Leaves.
A field in a dry place, and a ditch bringing water to it from the river. The water is sweet enough to drink and the field needs it, so it is let in, spread, and let to soak, and the grain comes up because of it. What the water carries besides water is a small quantity of dissolved salt, the ordinary amount any river carries, far too little to taste. The crop takes up the water. The sun takes the rest. Neither takes the salt, so the salt stays in the ground, in a quantity too small for anyone to see, and the field is watered again next season, and the season after that.

## Arc

The bend is this: water arrives carrying salt and leaves without it, so watering the field and salting it are one act described twice.
It runs on there being no subtraction.
The One Who Waters does everything right. Irrigating a dry field is necessary, and doing it well is a skill, and the harvest that follows is real and feeds people. Nothing about the practice is careless and nothing about it is optional: a field in a dry place is watered or it is nothing.
And The Salt accumulates, at a rate no one is equipped to notice. A season's deposit is below what a person can see, taste, or measure with anything they have. There is no year in which the ground is meaningfully worse than the year before, which means there is no year in which anyone could have been asked to act.
Underneath, The Water Below rises. Water put on a field that has no way to drain has only one place to go, so the table climbs a little each season, and when it comes close enough to the surface the ground begins to wick it upward and the sun concentrates what arrives. The salt that had been spread thinly through the soil is delivered back to exactly the depth the roots are in.
Then The Hardier Grain arrives, and it is a good answer. Barley bears salt far better than wheat, so sowing barley where wheat has begun to fail is not denial, it is competence, and it works: the yield holds and the field goes on being a field. In the Mesopotamian tallies you can watch it happen, wheat and barley roughly equal at the start and wheat falling away to a fraction over the centuries that follow. The adaptation is real, and it is also the thing that lets the trend run on undiscussed, because a field that still yields is not a field anyone is arguing about.
Until The White Ground, which is the first plainly visible sign and arrives long after the last useful moment: a bloom of white on the surface after a dry morning, on ground that will not now grow the hardier grain either. The interval between the practice and this is generations. Nobody in that chain chose ruin later over yield now. At no point was that choice on the table in a form anyone could recognise.
And every remedy is paid at the wrong end. Wash the salt down and out, and you need more water than the crop needs, in the one kind of place where water is short. Dig drains, and you are maintaining works that produce nothing visible in a good year. Rest the field, and you give up a harvest against a harm that has not appeared. Each is paid now by someone who will not see what it prevented, and if it works, nobody will ever know what it prevented either.

## Stakes

Whether an act that is necessary, skilled and productive can be counted honestly when its cost is deposited rather than spent.
Every plot moves the same way: toward a field that must be watered and is watered well, and away from any season in which the ground is enough worse than last season for anyone to notice. The production cannot make the water arrive without salt, because rivers carry salt, and it cannot make the ground give it back, because only surplus water does that and surplus water is what a dry place does not have. What is left to fight over is not whether to irrigate, everyone agrees the field is nothing unwatered, but who gives up water, land or a season now against a harm that will arrive after they are gone, and how anyone would ever be thanked for it.
