# What the Water Leaves: Reference

The research warrant for What the Water Leaves.
The term is the house's; the play runs on secondary salinisation of irrigated land, read through the
Mesopotamian record set out by Jacobsen and Adams and the modern soil-science account of the same process,
with the long dispute over the historical reading carried alongside it, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging secondary salinisation as **a medium consumed by the act that uses it**: water applied to a field
is taken up by the crop and lost to the air, and the salt it carried stays behind, so every correct
watering leaves the ground fractionally less able to be watered again. The play models the documented
mechanics, the irrigation that is necessary and skilled and produces a real harvest, the residue that
every watering deposits below any threshold a person could notice, the water table rising under a field
that is watered and not drained until it carries salt up into the root zone, the switch to a hardier crop
that is a sensible answer and holds the yield up while the trend continues underneath it, the ground that
crosses into unusable on a timescale no farmer's decision spans, and the corrections that all cost water,
land or a season, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a civilisation destroying
itself.

The governing law is that irrigation water is never pure. It carries dissolved salt, in small quantities,
and the plant takes the water while the sun takes the rest, and neither takes the salt. So the field is a
place where salt arrives and does not leave, and the only thing that removes it is more water than the crop
needs, drained away downward, which is precisely the water a dry place does not have. Watering well and
salting the ground are not two acts that can be separated. They are one act described twice.

What makes it structural rather than a failure of care is the timescale. The deposit from a single season
is far below anything a person could see, taste or measure with the instruments of the time, and the
interval between the practice and the harm runs to generations. Nobody in the sequence is choosing later
ruin over present yield, because at no point is there a choice on the table that anyone could recognise as
that.

Jacobsen and Adams read the record in southern Mesopotamia and found the trend written in the crops: wheat
and barley roughly equal in the earliest tallies, wheat falling to a small fraction over the following
centuries, and barley, which tolerates salt far better, taking the ground. They identified several
episodes, the earliest and most serious running in southern Iraq from about 2400 BC.

The modern extent is measured rather than inferred. Around a tenth of the world's irrigated cropland is
salt-affected on the most recent global assessment, with other estimates ranging far higher, and in some
countries, Iraq among them, close to half the irrigated area is affected. The process has a name in soil
science, secondary salinisation, and a well-understood cause: irrigation without adequate drainage raises
the water table until capillary rise and evaporation concentrate salt where the roots are.

It is distinct from the house's **The Made Ground**, which is the nearest neighbour and whose own warrant
draws the line: there an actor alters the world by living correctly in it and the altered world sets the
next test, so what moves is the criterion of success and, in that warrant's own words, nothing is
consumed. Here something is consumed. The ground is not setting a harder exam, it is being used up, and
the exhaustion is physical and eventually total.

It is distinct from the house's **The Commons**, where a shared stock is drawn down because the cost of
one more taking is split among all: here there is no rivalry and no sharing required, since a farmer
salting only their own field, on their own land, with nobody else drawing on it, produces the whole
structure alone.

It is distinct from the house's **Seed Corn**, where the present is funded by consuming what the future
needed and the trade is deliberate: here nobody trades anything, because the cost is invisible on the
timescale of every decision that produces it, and a farmer who wanted to protect the future would have
nothing to point at.

It is distinct from the house's **The Horizon Problem**, where a plan is complete to its instrument's edge
and silent past it: there a boundary is drawn and the beyond is excluded. Here there is no plan and no
edge, only a physical accumulation that runs continuously through every boundary anyone might draw.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the farmer as
  greedy or short-sighted, because the source structure gives no one a choice to get wrong: irrigating a
  dry field is necessary and skilled, the crop needs the water, the harvest is real, and the salt left by a
  single season is far below what anyone could see or measure. Switching to a hardier grain is not denial
  either, it is a good answer to a real problem, and it works. The failure is authored by nobody and
  emerges from the structure, water that arrives carrying salt and leaves without it, so the misfit is that
  watering well and salting the ground are one act and not two, and no amount of care separates them.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Every remedy is real and
  every one is paid at the wrong end: leaching demands more water than the crop needs, in the one kind of
  place where water is short; drainage means digging and maintaining works that yield nothing anyone can
  point to in a good year; fallow means a season's harvest given up against a harm that has not appeared.
  Each is paid now, by someone who will not live to see what it prevented, and if it works nobody will ever
  know what it prevented either.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the record, the mechanism, the measurements and the
  argument are theirs. **The contested edge is carried rather than smoothed**: Jacobsen and Adams' reading
  of the Mesopotamian record has been disputed since 1985, notably by Powell, who argued that mitigation
  was available and practised, and defended since by others, so the historical case is live. The play does
  not assert that salinisation brought down any civilisation, and the field's own warning is worth keeping:
  the story can be made too neat and too modern, and turned into an environmental morality tale, which is
  precisely the reading the house refuses. What is staged is the mechanism, which is not in dispute and is
  measured on irrigated land today.
