# Play: Nobody Shops Around

## Estate

## Name

Nobody Shops Around.
A good is sold by many sellers, all offering the same thing, in a market that looks the very picture of competition: rivals on every corner, a shopper free to take their custom anywhere. Competition is supposed to hold the price down, because a seller who charges too much loses its customers to one who charges less. And yet the price sits high, the same at every shop, as if a single owner set it. Nothing stops a shopper walking to the next seller. It simply is not worth the walk.
And here is the shape beneath the sameness. To compare prices, a shopper must pay a small cost: a little time, a little effort, a walk to the next shop, a phone held up to check. Now suppose every seller charged the same competitive price. One seller raises its price by a hair, by less than that cost of looking. A shopper standing before it sees the higher price and weighs the walk: searching would save the hair, but searching costs more than the hair, and every other shop, so far as the shopper knows, charges the old price anyway, so there is nothing better out there worth the trip. The shopper stays and pays (Diamond).
And what one seller can do, every seller can, and no rival can take the custom back. A rival that undercuts to win the shopper gains nothing, because a shopper already in a shop will not leave over a hair, and a shopper not yet in the shop will not cross the street to save one. So undercutting buys no customers and cutting price only throws away margin. The one discipline competition had, that a lower price pulls buyers over, is worth nothing once the difference is smaller than the cost of coming, so each seller raises, and none is punished for it (Diamond).
And so the price creeps all the way up. Each small raise sticks, and each is followed, and the only price at which a seller no longer wants to raise is the monopoly price, the one a single owner of the whole market would set. So the market of many competitors settles exactly where a monopoly would, not by collusion and not by any seller charging more than the rest, but by every seller charging the same, each a hair at a time, up to the top. A vanishing cost of looking has produced the full monopoly price (Diamond).
And the loss is authored by no one. The seller that raised a hair no shopper would walk over acted soundly; the rival that declined to undercut for customers it could not win acted soundly; the shopper that stayed rather than pay to look for a saving smaller than the look acted soundly. Each did the sensible thing, and the sum is a price set as if competition did not exist, held there by nothing worse than the small cost of comparing that keeps anyone from comparing.
And here is what restores competition, and why it costs. Make looking free: post the prices, advertise them, give the shopper a way to compare at no cost, and the seller that raises a hair is seen and left, so the discipline returns and the price falls back toward cost (Salop and Stiglitz; Varian). Or let enough shoppers be the kind who search at little cost, and their custom, chasing the lower price, drags a spread of prices back into the market. But transparency must be built and kept, sellers have their own reasons to obscure and complicate what they charge, and any cost of comparing that remains leaves room for the price to lift again.
And so the misfit is not that markets never compete but that the discipline of competition rests on cheap comparison. The unravelling to the monopoly price under a positive search cost is Diamond's, the economics of costly search is Stigler's, and the return of price dispersion when some search cheaply is Salop and Stiglitz's and Varian's. The house stages the collapse of price competition and holds the bound in view: that it needs a positive search cost, a homogeneous good, and prices you must go and find, and lifts where search is free, some shoppers search at no cost, or prices are posted, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that where nobody shops around, many sellers charge the price of one.

## Arc

The bend is this: where many sellers offer the same good and consumers must pay any positive cost to compare prices, no seller loses customers by raising its price below that cost and none gains them by undercutting, so prices ratchet to the monopoly level and competition disciplines nothing, with every seller and every shopper acting rationally.
It runs on a small cost of comparing.
Many sellers offer the same good, and to check the next seller's price a shopper must pay a small cost, a little time or effort or a walk, even a vanishing one (Stigler; Diamond).
And a seller raises a hair. From a competitive price, one seller lifts its price by less than the cost of looking, a raise so small no shopper will pay to walk away from it (Diamond).
And the shopper stays. Facing the higher price, the shopper weighs the walk and declines, since the saving is smaller than the search, so it buys where it stands and the raise sticks (Diamond).
And no rival undercuts. A rival gains no customers by cutting, since a shopper in a shop will not leave over a hair and one outside will not come for one, so the discipline of competition is gone (Diamond).
And every seller raises. What one did, all can, and each does, since none is punished, so the raises repeat across the market, each seller a hair at a time.
And the price creeps to monopoly. The only price at which no seller wants to raise further is the monopoly price, so the many competitors settle exactly where a single owner would (Diamond).
And the loss is authored by no one. The seller, the rival, and the shopper each acted soundly, so the monopoly price is a harm with no culprit, set by no collusion and no one charging more than the rest.
And the whole is held against its own limit. The result needs a positive search cost, a homogeneous good, and unposted prices, and lifts under free search, a fraction of zero-cost searchers, or advertised prices (Salop and Stiglitz; Varian).

## Stakes

Whether competition among many sellers holds the price down, when comparing prices costs even a little.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward a low price, which rivalry among many sellers should deliver, and toward the monopoly price, because a raise smaller than the cost of looking loses no customers and an undercut smaller than the cost of coming wins none, so each seller lifts its price and none is punished.
