# Nobody Shops Around: Reference

The research warrant for **Nobody Shops Around**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls the
**Diamond paradox**: that in a market of many sellers of the same good, if consumers face even a
vanishingly small cost to check the next seller's price, the only equilibrium is every seller charging
the full monopoly price, so a tiny search friction collapses competition entirely and prices jump
discontinuously from the competitive level to the monopoly one. It is held with the finding that the
result is conditional on a positive search cost, a homogeneous good, and prices that must be searched
for, since free search, a fraction of shoppers who search at no cost, or advertised prices each restore
competition and its dispersion.

## Line of Work

Staging the Diamond paradox as a **tiny-search-cost-collapses-competition structure**: a good is sold
by many sellers, all offering the same thing, and a consumer who wants to compare prices must pay a
small cost to do it, a little time, a little effort, a walk to the next shop. Competition is supposed
to hold prices down: if one seller charges more, its customers go elsewhere. But watch what the small
cost does. Suppose every seller charged some competitive price. A single seller now raises its price by
a hair, by less than the cost of searching. A consumer standing in front of that seller sees the higher
price and asks whether to walk: it would save the hair by searching, but searching costs more than the
hair, and every other seller charges the same competitive price anyway, so there is nothing better to
find that is worth the walk. So the consumer stays and pays (Diamond). And what one seller can do, all
can, and no rival can win the customer back by undercutting, since a consumer already in a shop will not
leave over a hair and a consumer not in the shop will not come for one, so the discipline of competition
is gone. Each seller raises again, and again, and the only price at which no seller wants to raise
further is the monopoly price, so the market of many competitors settles at the price a single
monopolist would set (Diamond). The ordinary expectation is that competition among many sellers drives
the price toward cost; the finding is that any positive cost of looking, however small, drives it all
the way to monopoly instead, a discontinuous jump at the instant search stops being free. The governing
law is that **where many sellers offer the same good and consumers must pay any positive cost to
compare prices, no seller loses customers by raising its price below that cost and none gains them by
undercutting, so prices ratchet to the monopoly level and competition disciplines nothing, with every
seller and every shopper acting rationally**. The play models the documented mechanics: the economics
of costly search (Stigler), the unravelling to the monopoly price under a positive search cost
(Diamond), and the return of price dispersion when some consumers search at low or no cost (Salop and
Stiglitz; Varian), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, and not as a claim that markets never
compete on price.

It is a market structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from Priced In, the Grossman-Stiglitz paradox, where everyone reads the
answer off the price so no one pays to find it out and the price stops holding the answer: there the
failure is informational, the price ceasing to aggregate what is known, whereas here the good and its
quality are plain and the failure is the price level itself, driven to monopoly by the cost of
comparing, not by any collapse of what the price conveys. It is distinct from The Price Neither Will
Name, the bilateral-trading impossibility, where a buyer and a seller each hide a private value so a
deal both would gain from goes unmade: there the trade fails to happen at all and the barrier is
two-sided private information, whereas here the trade happens readily and the barrier is a search cost,
the harm not a lost deal but a monopoly price paid on a deal that is made. It is distinct from The
Market for Lemons, adverse selection, where hidden quality drives the good goods out: there the goods
differ and their quality is hidden, whereas here the goods are identical and known, and nothing is
sorted or driven out, only the price lifted by the cost of looking.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the seller
  who raises its price as a gouger or the shopper who stays as lazy, because the finding it stages is
  that both act rationally: a seller that raises by less than the search cost loses no customers and so
  is right to raise, a rival that declines to undercut is right, since it cannot win a customer who will
  not walk for the difference, and a shopper that declines to search is right, since the saving is less
  than the cost of looking (Diamond). The loss is authored by no one: it is a positive search cost, so
  each small raise sticks and each is followed, and the price climbs to monopoly by the sum of choices
  that are individually correct, competition disciplined away by nothing worse than the price of a look.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Competition can be
  restored, but not for free: making search free, by posting and advertising prices, or a price
  comparison that any shopper can read at no cost, brings the competitive price back, and where a
  fraction of consumers already search at low cost the market shows dispersion rather than one monopoly
  price (Salop and Stiglitz; Varian), but building and maintaining that transparency is itself costly,
  sellers have their own reasons to obscure and complicate prices, and any residual cost of comparing
  leaves room for the price to lift. The reckoning is that the discipline of competition rests on cheap
  comparison, and where comparison is not cheap, many sellers can charge the price of one.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the unravelling to the monopoly price under a
  positive search cost is Diamond's, the economics of costly search is Stigler's, and the return of
  price dispersion when search costs differ across consumers is Salop and Stiglitz's and Varian's. The
  mechanism is carried as carefully as the finding: that the result needs a positive search cost, a
  homogeneous good, and prices that must be searched for, that a small raise sticks because no consumer
  will walk for less than the cost of walking, that the induction carries the price all the way to
  monopoly, and above all that it is conditional, since free search, a fraction of zero-cost searchers,
  or advertised prices each restore competition and its dispersion. The scope is held to price
  competition under costly search, and not stretched into a claim that all prices are monopoly prices or
  that competition never works.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Search Cost**: carries the small cost to compare prices (Stigler; Diamond), the time, effort, or
  walk it takes to check the next seller, the friction that does all the work even when it is vanishingly
  small.
- **The Nudge**: carries the unit move (Diamond), a single seller raising its price by less than the
  search cost, a raise so small no consumer will pay to walk away from it.
- **The Stay**: carries the consumer's rational non-response (Diamond), the shopper that declines to
  search because the saving is less than the cost of looking, and so buys where it stands, letting the
  nudge stick.
- **The Creep**: carries the induction (Diamond), every seller able to nudge and none undercut, so the
  raises repeat and the price ratchets from the competitive level toward the monopoly one.
- **The Monopoly Price**: carries the phenomenon whole (Diamond), the unique equilibrium at the full
  monopoly price, many competitors charging what a single monopolist would, a discontinuous jump caused
  by a vanishing friction.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge (Salop and Stiglitz; Varian), that the paradox
  needs a positive search cost, a homogeneous good, and unposted prices, and lifts under free search, a
  fraction of zero-cost searchers, or advertised prices, so the trap is real but bounded.
