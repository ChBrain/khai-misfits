# The Third Option: Reference

The research warrant for **The Third Option**. The title is a reading of the phenomenon named the
**decoy effect**, or asymmetric dominance: adding a third option to a choice set, one that is
clearly worse than one existing option but not clearly worse than the other, shifts choice toward
the option that dominates the decoy, so an alternative chosen by no one changes the choice between
two others it did not need to touch, all cited below, held with the finding that the effect is real
and replicated but its strength varies with how the options are represented and is weaker or absent
under some conditions and elicitation methods.

## Line of Work

Staging the decoy effect as a **dominated-decoy-shifts-choice structure**: a chooser faces two options that trade off
against each other, one better on one dimension, the other better on a second, a cheaper camera against a
better-featured one, a nearer apartment against a bigger one, with no dominance between them and no obvious winner. Then
a third option is added, an asymmetric decoy, worse than one of the two original options on every dimension that matters
but not worse than the other on every dimension, so it is dominated by one option and merely trades off against the
other as the original pair did between themselves. No rational chooser would select the decoy, since it is strictly
worse than an alternative already on offer, and choosers rarely do. But its mere presence changes which of the other two
is chosen: choice shifts markedly toward the option that dominates the decoy, the one the decoy makes look good by
proximity and comparison, even though nothing about that option's own merits has changed and the decoy itself draws
almost no share (Huber, Payne and Puto). The finding violates the principle of regularity, that adding an alternative to
a choice set should never increase the probability of choosing an option already in it, since the new option can only
draw share away, not add to what was there, and it violates independence from irrelevant alternatives, the assumption
that the relative choice between two options should not depend on the presence of a third, especially a dominated one no
one would rationally choose (Huber, Payne and Puto). The mechanism is comparative and not absolute: an option's
attractiveness is judged partly by its position relative to what stands beside it, and a decoy strictly dominated by one
option supplies that option a comparison it wins decisively, a local win that borrows credibility for the wider,
undominated comparison against the other original option, so a chooser who could not easily judge two genuinely
trading-off options gains an easy, comparison-based reason to favor the one the decoy flatters (Huber, Payne and Puto;
the attraction-effect literature that followed). It is authored by no villain: the decoy may be placed on the menu, the
shelf, or the ballot for any reason, a discontinued model, an outdated option, a genuine but poor alternative kept in
range, and need not be placed with intent to manipulate at all, and the chooser who shifts toward the dominating option
is not confused or careless, only using a real and ordinarily reasonable cue, that a clear local win is informative,
in a setting where the cue happens to mislead. The governing law is not that anyone games the menu, but that **adding a
dominated third option to a choice set can shift choice between the other two toward whichever one dominates the decoy,
because comparative ease substitutes for a harder trade-off judgment, so an alternative selected by no one still governs
the choice between two others, no villain required**. The play models the documented mechanics: the two options that
trade off with no easy winner, the decoy added and dominated by one of them, the easy local comparison the decoy
supplies, the choice shifted toward the option that wins it, the option chosen by no one that governed the outcome
anyway, and the conditions under which the shift is strong or weak (Huber, Payne and Puto; later replications and
boundary studies), as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a salesperson planting a rigged
comparison.

It is a dominated-decoy-shifts-choice structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbour. It is distinct from Spoiled for Choice, where past a point a larger assortment overwhelms the chooser, so a
wider range lowers both the odds of choosing at all and the satisfaction of the choice made: there the harm is the sheer
count of options raising the burden of comparison and the hope of a perfect fit past what the chooser can bear, a
paralysis and a regret that grows with the size of the set, whereas here a single added option, dominated and chosen by
no one, reshapes the choice between a small, unchanged pair, the harm not paralysis from too many options but a
preference reversal caused by one specific, badly chosen addition, present even in a set of just three.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the one who adds the third
  option as a manipulator staging a rigged comparison, because the source finding it stages is that the shift needs no
  intent to deceive: a decoy option, a discontinued model, an outdated plan, a weaker candidate, can appear on a menu or a
  ballot for entirely ordinary reasons having nothing to do with steering anyone's choice, and the chooser who shifts
  toward the option the decoy flatters is not gullible but using a real and ordinarily sound cue, that a clear win in one
  comparison is informative, in a setting where that cue happens to mislead. No one need place the decoy on purpose for
  the shift to occur. The shift is authored by no one: it is comparative judgment substituting an easy local win for a
  harder trade-off, and the misfit is that an option no one chooses can still decide the choice between two others.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. A chooser can defeat the effect by
  evaluating each option's absolute merits independent of what stands beside it, or by deliberately excluding dominated
  alternatives from consideration before comparing what remains, but both ask for a discipline that trades away the very
  shortcut, comparative ease, that makes choosing among many attributes tractable at all, and a chooser facing a real menu
  rarely has the leisure or the cause to suspect that one option on it is there to flatter another.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed as the
  house's. The staging is the house's; the demonstration and the name are Huber, Payne and Puto's, and the wider
  replication across choice domains is the attraction-effect literature's. The mechanism is carried as carefully as the
  finding: the effect is real and repeatedly demonstrated, but its magnitude and even its presence depend on how the
  choice is elicited, joint or separate evaluation, the number and structure of the attributes compared, so the house
  stages the shift as a documented and conditional effect and not a claim that any added option always moves a choice.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Trade-Off Pair**: carries the setting (Huber, Payne and Puto), the two options that trade off against each other
  with no easy winner, one better on one dimension and the other better on a second.
- **The Decoy**: carries the addition (Huber, Payne and Puto), the third option, dominated by one of the pair and merely
  trading off against the other, chosen by no one and placed for any ordinary reason.
- **The Local Win**: carries the mechanism (Huber, Payne and Puto; the attraction-effect literature), the easy, clear
  comparison the decoy supplies to the option that dominates it, an informative-seeming win in one comparison.
- **The Shift**: carries the effect (Huber, Payne and Puto), the choice between the original two moving toward the option
  that dominates the decoy, violating regularity and independence from irrelevant alternatives.
- **The Governed Choice**: carries the harm (the attraction-effect literature), the outcome decided by an alternative
  selected by no one, drawn distinct from Spoiled for Choice.
- **The Conditions**: carries the bound (boundary and contested-replication work), the elicitation methods and attribute
  structures under which the effect is strong, weak, or absent, the marker of the hedge, since the effect is real and
  replicated but not unconditional.
