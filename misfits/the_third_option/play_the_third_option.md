# Play: The Third Option

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Third Option.
A chooser faces two options that trade off fairly against each other, a cheaper thing against a better one, a nearer thing against a bigger one, one better on one dimension and the other better on a second, with no dominance between them and no obvious winner. Choosing between them is genuinely hard, since to prefer one is to give something up that the other offered.
Then a third option joins the set, worse than one of the two originals on every dimension that matters but not worse than the other on every dimension, so it trades off against one of them exactly as the pair always did between themselves, while the other original option beats it outright, on every count. No rational chooser would ever pick this third option, since a strictly better alternative already sits beside it, and choosers rarely do (Huber, Payne and Puto).
But its mere presence changes which of the other two gets chosen. Choice shifts markedly toward the option that dominates the decoy, the one the decoy makes look good simply by standing beside it, even though nothing about that option's own merits has changed and the decoy itself draws almost no share of the choice at all. This violates regularity, the plain expectation that adding an option to a set should never raise the odds of choosing something already in it, since a new option can only draw share away and never add to what was there, and it violates the independence the choice between two things ought to have from a third, especially one no rational chooser would take (Huber, Payne and Puto).
The mechanism is comparative rather than absolute. An option's appeal is judged partly by what stands beside it, and a decoy strictly dominated by one option hands that option an easy, decisive win in one local comparison, a win that lends its credibility to the harder, genuinely balanced comparison against the other original option, so a chooser who could not easily judge two fairly trading-off things gains a comparison-based shortcut that favors whichever one the decoy happens to flatter (Huber, Payne and Puto; the attraction-effect literature that followed).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The decoy may be placed on the menu, the shelf, or the ballot for any ordinary reason at all, a discontinued model kept in the catalog, an older plan not yet retired, a weaker candidate who simply chose to run, and needs no intent to steer anyone's choice. And the chooser who shifts toward the dominating option is not gullible or careless: it is using a real and ordinarily sound cue, that a clear win in a direct comparison says something true about quality, in the one setting where that cue happens to mislead, since the comparison it wins so clearly was never the comparison that mattered. No one games the menu on purpose, and no one is fooled by a trick they should have seen. The loss is authored by no one: it is comparative ease substituting for a harder trade-off judgment, and the misfit is that an option no one chooses can still decide the choice between two others.
And here is the way out, and why it is hard to hold to. A chooser can defeat the shift by judging each option on its own absolute merits, set apart from whatever happens to stand beside it, or by deliberately striking any dominated option from consideration before comparing what remains. But both ask for a discipline that trades away the very shortcut, comparative ease, that makes choosing among many attributes bearable at all, and a chooser facing a real menu rarely has cause to suspect that one item on it exists to flatter another.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The effect is real and has been repeatedly demonstrated, but its size, and even its presence, depends on how the choice is put to the chooser, whether options are judged jointly or one at a time, how many attributes are compared and how they are structured, so it is weaker or absent under some elicitation methods (boundary and contested-replication work). The demonstration and the name are Huber, Payne and Puto's, and the wider reach across choice domains is the attraction-effect literature's. The house stages the shift as a documented and conditional effect, and not a claim that any added option always moves a choice.

## Arc

The bend is this: adding a dominated third option to a choice set can shift the choice between the other two toward whichever one dominates the decoy, because comparative ease substitutes for a harder trade-off judgment, so an alternative selected by no one still governs the choice between two others.
It runs on a comparison too easy to resist and too local to matter.
Two options trade off fairly, no dominance between them and no obvious winner.
And a decoy joins. Worse than one option on everything, not worse than the other, chosen by no rational chooser (Huber, Payne and Puto).
And a local win is handed out. The dominating option beats the decoy decisively, a clear comparison it cannot lose.
And the choice shifts. Toward the option the decoy flatters, though nothing about its own merit changed (Huber, Payne and Puto).
And an alternative chosen by no one governs the outcome. The decoy drawing almost no share and deciding it anyway.
And the bound is held. Real and replicated, and weaker or absent under some ways of asking (boundary and contested-replication work).

## Stakes

Whether a choice between two fair options can be trusted, when the mere presence of a third, dominated option that no one will choose can still decide it.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the governed choice and away from an honest trade-off, because a decoy dominated by one option hands that option a comparison it cannot lose, and the comparative ease of that local win substitutes for the harder judgment the original pair actually demanded. The structure cannot both offer a third option for any ordinary reason and keep the choice between the other two untouched, because the offering is the touching: the same presence that lets a chooser see a clear local win is the presence that lends that win's credibility to a comparison it was never part of, so the more decisively one option beats the decoy, the more it is favored in a choice the decoy was never a contender for. The way out, judging each option on its own absolute merits or striking dominated options before comparing, trades away the very comparative ease that makes a crowded menu bearable to choose from at all, and a chooser rarely has cause to suspect one item exists to flatter another. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the decoy is placed for any ordinary reason and the chooser shifts by using a real and usually sound cue, and that the governed choice is authored by no one, comparative ease standing in for a judgment never made. And it holds the bound in view, hedged, because the effect is real and repeatedly demonstrated but its size and even its presence depend on how the choice is elicited (boundary and contested-replication work), so the whole of the misfit is that an option chosen by no one can still decide the choice between two others, present for any reason and deciding anyway.
