# Play: What Everyone Else Does

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

What Everyone Else Does.
When a court has to decide whether a professional was careless, it needs a standard, and it does not have one of its own. A judge cannot determine unaided whether a particular technique was sound, so the law asks the profession: would a responsible body of practitioners have done this? If the answer is yes, there is no negligence. That is not laziness and it is not capture. It is the only honest answer available to a tribunal that knows it is not competent to write clinical guidance from the bench, and it protects careful practitioners from being judged by hindsight and by outcome. It also means the profession's own habit becomes the legal floor, and a floor set by habit cannot be raised by the thing standing on it.

## Arc

The bend is this: the test asks what is done, so what is done is what is required, and nothing in the test can ask whether what is done is good enough.
It runs on a modesty that is entirely justified.
The Court That Defers is right to step back. The alternative is judges deciding contested clinical questions on their own reading, which is worse in every case anybody has thought of, and the deference is bounded rather than absolute: the opinion relied on must be responsible, and since 1997 it must also be capable of withstanding logical analysis. The bounds are real, and they are still bounds on an opinion that comes from inside the profession.
The One Who Did What Others Do is not negligent, and correctly so. A practitioner who acts in accordance with a practice accepted as proper by a responsible body of colleagues has done what any of them would have done, and the law will not punish them merely because another body of opinion takes a contrary view. There is nothing wrong with this and it is the reason the profession can practise at all.
And so The Custom seals itself. A method nobody uses is not required, because no responsible body uses it. A method everybody uses cannot be negligent, because a responsible body uses it. The test therefore reads the profession's average and returns it as the duty, and there is no input anywhere in the mechanism through which the average could be told it is too low.
The One Who Went First pays for the improvement and gets nothing for it. Adopting a safer method costs money, training and time, and confers no legal protection whatever, because the protection comes from what others do rather than from what you do. Meanwhile the colleague who declines is fully covered for as long as enough others also decline. So the incentive runs against being early, and the thing that would eventually make the method compulsory is the very adoption that nobody is rewarded for starting.
And The One Who Was Harmed loses to a practice rather than to a person. The injury is real and the causation is provable, and the answer is that this is how it is done, which is true. There is no individual to blame, no departure to point at, and no moment at which anybody chose wrongly.
It was named in 1932 and did not close. Two tugs lost their barges in a storm without radios, when radios were available and tugs did not generally carry them, and Learned Hand held that a whole calling may have unduly lagged in the adoption of new and available devices: courts must in the end say what is required, and there are precautions so imperative that even their universal disregard will not excuse their omission. The crack is ninety years old. Custom is still the working standard in most of the places this happens.
And the corrections settle into the same shape. A court may reject an opinion that will not withstand logical analysis, which requires a judge to overrule a body of expert evidence and is therefore done rarely and cautiously. Guidelines codify best practice, and then the guideline becomes the custom, including whatever it got wrong, and departing from it becomes the risky act rather than the careful one.

## Stakes

Whether a duty of care can rise when the only thing permitted to define it is the conduct it is supposed to be measuring.
Every plot moves the same way: toward a court properly modest about its own competence, practitioners doing what their colleagues would do, and experts of standing on both sides, and away from any instrument that could ask whether the whole practice is good enough, since the only witnesses to that question are the people whose practice it is. The production cannot make a judge a clinician, and it cannot ask a practitioner to be liable for doing what everybody does. What is left to fight over is not whether courts should defer, they must, but who pays for the years between a method being available and a method being usual, and whether a standard read off the average was ever a standard at all.
