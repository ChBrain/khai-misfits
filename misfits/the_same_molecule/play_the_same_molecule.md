# Play: The Same Molecule

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Same Molecule.
A medicine comes off patent. Several manufacturers make it, a regulator certifies that their versions are interchangeable, and they are.
Buying is done on price, because that is what interchangeable means, and the price falls until it is close to the cost of the glass.
Then one of the two remaining plants stops, and the medicine is gone from every hospital in the country at once.

## Arc

The bend is this: the market can only reward what it can see, the vials are certifiably the same, and the thing that differs between them is not in either vial.

It runs on one relation from regulation and one from the ground. **Equivalence is real**, so two approved versions of an old injectable are the same purchase and there is nothing to choose between them but price; and on the ground, **the line that makes them is not**, since one is a suite installed decades ago and running, and the other is not, and no buyer holds anything that says which.

Take the equivalence first, because the play has to establish that it is an achievement. This is not a loophole and not a fiction. Approval on those terms is why a medicine that cost a fortune under patent now costs almost nothing, and the buyer who awards to the lowest bid is doing exactly what a buyer of an interchangeable good should do. **Every step that made these drugs affordable is a step the play endorses.**

Then the fact that inverts it. **Competition runs at full strength on the visible dimension and at none on the invisible one.** The margin on an old sterile injectable is thin, the manufacturer has to find its costs somewhere, and the only place left is the place the customer cannot inspect: the age of the fill line, the depth of the redundancy, the size of the maintenance budget, the number of suites that could take over. None of that is on the label, in the bid, or in the contract, so none of it is paid for.

Then the sharper half, which is why the usual remedies do not reach it. **The failure is an absence and not a defect.** Every vial that arrived was good. There is no bad unit to observe, no claim to make, no batch to refuse, and so nothing for a market to learn from and select against. What was economised was the probability of there being a vial at all next year, and that is not a property of any good anyone bought. And the market is too thin to lose one participant: sterile capacity is approved line by line and product by product, so the survivors are not permitted to make more of the thing that ran out.

And nobody has done anything wrong. The regulator certified two products as equivalent, which they are. The buyer took the lower price, which is the point of having two. The manufacturer bid what it could meet and met it. The line was adequate at its last inspection. The exit was a decision not to spend money on a product that had stopped earning any.

Reach for a settlement and each one gives back the thing that was wanted. Stockpile it, and you have bought months and a cost and an expiry date. Qualify a second source, and you have started something that finishes in years. Pay a premium for reliability, and you have paid it alone, into a plant your competitors also draw on, for a property you cannot verify you received. Write a failure-to-supply penalty, and you have priced the penalty, which is not the shortage. Build a manufacturer that does not need the margin, which is a real answer and exists, and it is one more supplier competing in the same market on the same terms.

## Triggers

**[They Are the Same Drug](plot_they_are_the_same_drug.md)**
Equivalence established as an achievement rather than a loophole: two approved versions of an old injectable, interchangeable in fact and not merely in law, which is why the medicine is affordable at all. Closes when it is clear there is nothing to choose between them but price, and that this is correct.

**[The Lowest Bid Wins](plot_the_lowest_bid_wins.md)**
Cue: the award. Closes when the price has fallen to near the cost of making it and the document that moved a year's volume is seen to contain no line, no clause and no column describing the plant it will come off.

**[The Cheapest Place to Save](plot_the_cheapest_place_to_save.md)**
Cue: the margin, met. Closes when the economising is located: not in the product, which is inspected, but in the line, the redundancy and the maintenance, none of which the customer can see, and when it is clear this was adequate rather than reckless.

**[The Line Goes Down](plot_the_line_goes_down.md)**
Cue: a quality finding, and a suite out of service. Closes when the absence is national rather than local, because there were only ever two or three makers and the others' capacity is approved to make something else, and when the maker who stopped has no reason to return.

**[And Somebody Still Needs It Today](plot_and_somebody_still_needs_it_today.md)**
Cue: the settlements. Closes when each is found to give back what it was for: a stockpile with an expiry, a second source measured in years, a premium paid alone for something unverifiable, a penalty that prices the penalty, and a non-profit maker inside the same market.

## Stakes

Whether a market can be trusted with a good whose most important property it cannot see.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether the competition was wrong, because it is what put these medicines within reach, and not whether anyone cheated, because nobody did, but whether a mechanism that prices only what is visible can be left in charge of something that must simply keep existing. The production cannot settle it. It can only show that the equivalence is real, that the award has no line for the plant, that the economising went where nobody looks, that the failure arrives as an absence with no defective unit anywhere, and that every escape costs years, money, or the thing it was meant to save.
