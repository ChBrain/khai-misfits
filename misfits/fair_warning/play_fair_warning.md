# Play: Fair Warning

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Fair Warning.
There are two things everyone knows about announcing a phase-out, and both of them are true. The first is that you announce it early, because a rule that lands overnight strands the workers, the towns and the investors who built their lives around the thing being ended, and a government that does that to people has done something wrong quite apart from whatever it achieved. The second is that you make it credible, because a deadline nobody believes in changes nobody's plans, and a policy that changes nobody's plans is not a policy. Give people time, and mean it. That is not a compromise between fairness and effectiveness; it is what both of them separately require.
And here is what those two correct instincts do together.
The owner of a coal seam or an oil field is not deciding whether to sell. The stuff does not spoil. It is deciding **when**, and the only reason to leave it where it is another year is that it will be worth more next year than this year, net of what it costs to lift (Hotelling). That is the whole calculation, and it is not a clever calculation, it is the ordinary one.
And an announcement that demand will be cut in fifteen years is an announcement that the price in fifteen years will be lower. Which means the return to waiting has just fallen. Which means the optimal thing to do with the stock is to bring it up sooner.
And so the field goes into production in the year the date is set. Not out of spite, not to beat the deadline, not in defiance of anything: in correct answer to it. The owner has been told, credibly and in good time, that its asset is being wound down, and it has responded the way any holder of a depleting asset responds to news that the buyer is leaving (Sinn).
And nobody in it is a villain. The legislator picked fifteen years because five would be cruel and thirty would be meaningless. The campaigner pressing for a firmer date and an earlier one is pressing for exactly the right thing, since vagueness is how phase-outs die quietly and credibility is the property that makes them bite. And the owner is not defying the policy. It is believing it. Which is what the policy asked to be.
And now the cruelty, which is unusually exact. The remedy for a policy that fails because nobody believes it is to make it more credible. That is the standard move, it is correct almost everywhere else, and here it is the accelerant. Every increment of credibility the campaigner wins is an increment of certainty in the owner's ledger about what the price will do, and the ledger responds by moving the extraction forward. The lever a government can actually reach for is the one that makes it worse.
And the carbon arrives sooner. If the stock is eventually lifted anyway, then the announcement did not change the total at all: it changed the schedule, in the wrong direction, and the atmosphere is a stock problem where the schedule is most of what matters.
And the corrections that reach the mechanism all cost the same specific thing. Buy the reserves and retire them; tax the stock rather than the flow; build a demand cartel comprehensive enough that no unregulated buyer is left. Every one of them requires paying the owner for wealth the policy is extinguishing, which is a transfer to precisely the party the public believes should bear the loss, and the cartel has to be very nearly universal or the leakage restores the arbitrage it was built to close.
And so the misfit is not that resource owners are greedy but that a warning given fairly and made believable is, to an asset holder, a price forecast, and a price forecast is an instruction about when to sell. The green paradox is Sinn's; the rule the owner is following is Hotelling's, held near to mark the mechanism and not to claim the modern result. The house stages the notice, the certainty, the revaluation, the acceleration, the carbon arriving early and the correction that has to be bought, and holds the bound in view: that it needs an exhaustible stock and an owner able to accelerate, that its magnitude is contested and turns on extraction costs and on how much of the stock is ultimately economic, and that it lifts where supply-side instruments accompany the demand-side ones, so the whole of it is that telling someone honestly when you will stop buying is telling them exactly when to sell.

## Arc

The bend is this: where an owner's only real choice about an exhaustible stock is when to sell it, a credible announcement that future demand will be cut lowers the value of holding it, so the announcement brings the extraction forward and the harm arrives sooner than if nothing had been said, with nobody defying the policy and the policy's own credibility as the mechanism.
It runs on a warning that has to be both early and believed.
Early, so that workers, towns and investors are not stranded overnight; believed, because a deadline nobody credits changes nobody's plans, and neither property is optional.
And an owner whose question is when and not whether. The resource does not spoil, so the only return to leaving it in the ground is the expected rise in its net price (Hotelling).
And an announcement that is therefore a price forecast. Demand cut in fifteen years means a lower price in fifteen years, which means the return to waiting has just fallen.
And extraction that moves forward in correct answer. The field goes into production in the year the date is set, not in defiance of the policy but in belief of it (Sinn).
And a remedy that is the accelerant. Firmer dates and greater credibility are the standard cure for a policy nobody heeds, and here each increment of certainty moves the ledger further forward.
And carbon arriving sooner, and perhaps only sooner. Where the stock is lifted anyway the announcement changed the schedule and not the total, in the wrong direction, on a problem where the schedule is most of what matters.
And corrections that all cost the same thing. Buy and retire the reserves, tax the stock, or build a near-universal demand cartel: each pays the owner for wealth the policy is extinguishing.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs an exhaustible stock and a forward-looking owner able to accelerate, its magnitude is contested, and it lifts where supply-side instruments ride alongside the demand-side ones.
