# The Case It Destroys: Reference

The research warrant for The Case It Destroys.
The term is the house's; the play runs on the growth-and-underinvestment archetype from system dynamics,
from Forrester's method through Senge's naming and the People Express record, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging growth and underinvestment as a **loop that eats its own evidence**: a limit that investment could
remove is left in place, and the performance that follows destroys the demand that would have made the
case for investing. The play models the documented mechanics, the growth that arrives faster than capacity
can be built, the standard that quietly floats down under that pressure so that what was a lapse becomes
the going rate, the relaxed pressure to expand that follows from a standard nobody is failing any more,
the service that degrades because the capacity was never added, the demand that falls away because the
service degraded, and the falling demand read back into the room as proof that the capacity would have
stood idle, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of miserliness or short sight.

The governing law is that where a capacity decision is justified by observed demand, and observed demand is
suppressed by the absence of that capacity, the decision destroys its own evidence. The case for investing
is made of exactly the customers the underinvestment drives away, so the longer the decision is deferred
the better it looks, and it looks best just before the business fails.

The hardest part to stage is that nobody in the loop is wrong at the moment they act. The investment was
declined on numbers that were real. The standard fell because a growing operation genuinely cannot hold the
old one, and lowering it was the honest thing to record. Senge's account of People Express turns on exactly
this: the underinvestment was very difficult for the managers to discern at the time, because every
individual reading was defensible and only the loop was fatal.

It is distinct from the house's **Limits to Growth**, where a reinforcing engine meets a balancing limit
that is real and fixed: there the ceiling exists whatever anyone does, and the misfit is arriving at it.
Here the ceiling is removable, and it becomes real only because it was not removed.

It is distinct from the house's **The New Normal**, where a standard drifts until a margin is gone and
something breaks: there the eroding standard is the phenomenon and the outcome is a failure. Here the
eroding standard is one link, and the outcome is a capital decision that manufactures its own
justification. The New Normal ends in a bang; this ends in a chart that looks fine.

It is distinct from the house's **Shifting the Burden**, where a symptomatic fix relieves the pressure and
the fundamental capacity atrophies from disuse: there something is applied and works. Here nothing is
applied at all, and the capacity was never built rather than allowed to waste.

## Origin

The phenomenon in practice, and the research spine beneath it.

| Source                      | Key Work / Event                                              | Scope                                                                                                                                        |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Forrester**               | _Industrial Dynamics_ (1961) and the system dynamics method   | **The instrument**: the feedback structure that makes a self-justifying decision legible as a loop rather than a mistake.                    |
| **Senge**                   | _The Fifth Discipline_ (1990)                                 | **The naming**: growth and underinvestment set out as an archetype, with the eroding service standard as its hinge.                          |

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the executive
  as mean or the analyst as blinkered, because the source finding is precisely that each reading was sound:
  the demand numbers were real numbers, the standard was lowered because a growing operation genuinely could
  not hold the old one and recording the truth is not a failing, and declining to build capacity for
  customers who are visibly leaving is ordinary prudence. The underinvestment is authored by no meanness and
  emerges from the structure, a decision judged by evidence it is itself suppressing, so the misfit is that
  the case for the investment is made of the customers the underinvestment has already driven away.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Breaking the loop means
  holding the old standard against a growing load, which is to accept being visibly in breach of it, and
  then investing ahead of demand that is falling, on the argument that it is falling because you did not
  invest. That case is made against every number on the page and can only be won on a reading of the
  structure, so the correction is a commitment defended without evidence, in the period when the evidence
  says the opposite.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the archetype, the eroding-goal hinge and the People
  Express reading are theirs. The bound is carried with it: an archetype is a lens rather than a law, a
  single company's collapse has more causes than one loop, and the play stages the structure that made the
  underinvestment hard to see rather than a claim that it alone brought the airline down.
