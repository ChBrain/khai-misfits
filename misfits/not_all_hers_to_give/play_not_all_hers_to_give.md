## Name

Not All Hers to Give.
A pregnancy looks like one project shared by two bodies pursuing one goal: a healthy birth. The fetus grows, the mother's body carries it, and every hormone and nutrient that crosses between them looks like cooperation, because in the largest sense it is. But the two are not identically invested in the outcome. The fetus is related to itself completely, so its genes gain everything a bit of extra growth or an extra week in the womb can buy. The mother is related to this fetus by only one half, and by that same one half to every other offspring she carries now or will carry later. The two ledgers are not the same ledger, and the difference is where the conflict begins (Trivers; Hamilton).
And here is what the difference in ledgers means for what is worth taking or giving. A resource that costs the mother one unit of investment in a future offspring is worth, to her, exactly one unit of gain in the present one, since she is related by half to both, so she should give until the two balance. But that same resource, to the present offspring's own genes, is worth a full unit of gain against a cost measured only in what it does to a future sibling it is related to by half again, so the offspring is selected to keep asking past the point the mother is selected to keep giving, wanting more than she is selected to provide, not from any excess or any illness but from the arithmetic of relatedness alone (Trivers).
And the fetus's own genome does not speak with one voice on the matter. At certain loci, a copy of a gene inherited from the father presses for growth and resource extraction, and the copy of the same gene inherited from the mother would, left active, press for restraint, so evolution has silenced the more restrained copy at those loci and left only the pressing one switched on. The logic is that the father's copy, if the mother has mated with more than one male across her reproductive life, may share nothing with the half sibling that resource would otherwise be spared for, having no stake at all in a future child it may not be related to, while the mother's copy, related to that future sibling through the mother herself, has a stake in holding some back. So each parent's copy is imprinted, switched off or on by which parent it came from, and the offspring becomes, at these loci, a small parliament of two, one member with nothing to lose by asking for everything now (Haig; Haig and Moore).
And the pressing is not abstract. The placenta, built largely from the fetus's own tissue, secretes hormones that raise the mother's blood sugar and shift her blood pressure, drawing more glucose and more blood flow across to the fetus than her body would otherwise supply. Genes expressed from the paternal copy at growth-promoting loci, such as the one coding for insulin-like growth factor 2, are seen doing exactly this in the laboratory: switched off, growth falls; switched on alone, growth rises, the mechanism visible at the molecular level and not merely inferred (DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis; Haig).
And the mother's body answers. She raises her own insulin output to hold her blood sugar against the fetal push, and her vasculature adjusts to the higher demand on her circulation, a cover that in most pregnancies meets the press with room to spare, the ordinary business of gestation and no sign of any conflict at all. Neither side is at fault in any of it: the paternal copy presses because pressing is what its calculus rewards, the maternal copy is silenced because a mixed signal would only blunt the press without stopping it, and the mother covers because covering is the sound response to a fetus that is, after all, hers to carry. The loss, when it comes, is authored by no one (Haig).
And where the press outruns what the cover can meet, real pathology follows and not a metaphor. Gestational diabetes is the mother's insulin response exceeded by the fetal pull on her blood sugar; preeclampsia is read by the same theory as the fetal placenta driving the mother's blood pressure higher than her vasculature can safely sustain, both arising with no toxin, no infection, and no one to blame, the price of a tug fought out in hormones rather than in words (Haig). Screening for both is real medicine and it is not free: a glucose tolerance test, a blood pressure check, insulin or early delivery when the overshoot is severe, each catching the acute risk without touching the imprint that produced it, since the silencing at each locus is fixed in the genome and not a habit a clinic visit can break.
And so the misfit is not that pregnancy is adversarial but that a difference in relatedness, run twice, once between mother and fetus and once between the fetus's own two gene copies, is enough to press a demand past what any single party would set on its own, with no side defecting and no design at fault. The general parent-offspring conflict from asymmetric relatedness is Trivers's, built on Hamilton's rule; its extension into an intragenomic tug of war between imprinted paternal and maternal gene copies is Haig's and Haig and Moore's, and the molecular demonstration that a single locus's parent-of-origin expression moves fetal growth is DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis's. The house stages the tug and holds the bound in view: that it needs a viviparous kin system with loci under imprinting control, that the press and the cover are each sound by their own gene's calculus, and that it lifts toward convergence wherever a mother's every offspring shares the same father, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that a resource is never quite as free to give as it looks, once you ask whose future it was also owed to.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a fetus is related to itself completely but a mother is related by half to it and by half to every other offspring she carries now or later, and where the fetus's own paternally and maternally inherited gene copies are imprinted to different interests at the same loci, the press for resources runs past what the mother is selected to give, and where it runs past what she can cover, real pathology follows, with no side at fault.
It runs on two unequal ledgers.
The fetus is related to itself fully; the mother is related by half to this fetus and by that same half to every offspring she will ever have, present or future (Trivers; Hamilton).
And a demand set by the wider ledger. A resource the mother would give until her cost to a future offspring balances her gain to this one is, by the fetus's own calculus, worth taking well past that balance, since the fetus weighs only what it costs a future sibling it is related to by half again (Trivers).
And an imprint that splits the offspring's own voice. At growth loci, the paternally inherited copy, unrelated to a possible future half sibling by a different father, presses for extraction, and evolution silences the more restrained maternal copy at those loci rather than let it blunt the press (Haig; Haig and Moore).
And a press carried in hormones. The placenta, built from the fetus's own tissue, raises the mother's blood sugar and blood pressure, a paternally expressed growth gene demonstrably driving the pull in the laboratory (DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis).
And a cover that usually holds. The mother raises her insulin and adjusts her vasculature to meet the press, ordinary gestation and no sign of conflict, ordinary because the cover is, in most pregnancies, enough (Haig).
And the loss is authored by no one. The paternal copy presses because pressing is what its calculus rewards, the mother covers because covering is sound, and where the press outruns the cover, gestational diabetes or preeclampsia follows with no toxin and no one to blame.
And the whole is held against its own limit. The trap needs a viviparous kin system with imprinted loci and lifts toward convergence wherever a mother's every offspring shares one father, screening catching the acute risk though never removing the imprint that presses it (Haig and Moore; DeChiara, Robertson and Efstratiadis).
