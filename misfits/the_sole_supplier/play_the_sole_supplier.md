# Play: The Sole Supplier

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Sole Supplier.
A bureau provides a public service, and it is the only one that provides it. There is no rival agency to compare it against, no market price to read, no second quote to hold up beside its own. And there is a deeper asymmetry beneath that one: only the bureau knows what the service truly costs to produce. The sponsor that funds it, a legislature or an executive, holds the purse and looks, on paper, to hold the power. But it cannot see the cost, and it has nowhere else to buy.
And here is what the bureau's chief is rewarded for. Not profit, which it does not keep, but the budget itself: a larger budget means more staff to lead, more salary, more perquisites, more prestige, more patronage and discretion, so the size of the budget is the measure of the chief's success (Niskanen; Tullock). The chief is not a thief. It simply does well by doing big, and growing the bureau is doing well.
And so the chief maximizes the budget, and the monopoly and the hidden cost let it. Because the sponsor cannot cost the service and cannot buy it anywhere else, the bureau presents its output and its funding as a single package: this much service for this much money, take it or leave it. The sponsor cannot pick the efficient amount at the efficient price out of the bundle, because the bundle is all there is, so it funds the whole thing or gets nothing (Niskanen).
And the sponsor funds it. It wants the service, it cannot price it, and the alternative is none at all, so it approves the package, and the bureau captures the surplus as budget: more of the service than is worth its cost, or the right amount at an inflated cost, the difference kept as staff and standing. Government over-supplies, not because anyone stole and not because the sponsor was foolish, but because the monopoly of supply and the hidden cost handed the terms to the bureau (Niskanen).
And the loss is authored by no one and falls on the public. The chief grew the budget, which is its reward and its job; the sponsor funded a service it wanted and could not cost, which is reasonable; and the public pays for a government larger and costlier than it needed to be, a bill with no thief behind it. The bureau reads as diligent and well-run, its budget growing year on year as a sign of importance, and the over-supply hides inside the impossibility of costing it.
And here is what checks it, and why it costs. Make the cost visible: audit the books, compare the bureau against other jurisdictions, contract the service out to read a market price, and the sponsor's leverage returns, since a cost it can see is a budget it can trim. Or reward the chief for efficiency rather than size, and it will stop maximizing the budget (Niskanen; Migue and Belanger). But none is free: the bureau knows its own costs far better than any auditor, comparison is imperfect where services differ, contracting out is impossible for some public goods, and the chief maximizing not the whole budget but the slack within it hides the padding deeper still. The sponsor's power over the purse is only ever as real as its power to cost the service.
And so the misfit is not that government is bloat but that a monopoly of supply and cost lets the supplier set the terms. The budget-maximizing bureau and its take-it-or-leave-it offer are Niskanen's, the bureaucrat's growth incentives are Tullock's and Downs's, the discretionary-slack variant is Migue and Belanger's, and the tempering of the magnitude is Blais and Dion's. The house stages the extraction under hidden cost and holds the bound in view: that it needs a monopoly bureau, an under-informed sponsor, and a reward in budget, that its size is contested, and that it lifts where costs can be seen, a rival compared, or efficiency rewarded, so the trap is real and common and bounded, and the whole of it is that whoever alone can supply a thing and alone knows its cost sets the price of it, even to the sponsor who holds the purse.

## Arc

The bend is this: where a bureau is the sole supplier of a service to a sponsor who cannot observe its cost, and the bureau is rewarded by the size of its budget, it offers output and funding as one take-it-or-leave-it package and extracts a budget above the efficient level, so government over-supplies, with no one embezzling and the sponsor unable to correct it.
It runs on a monopoly of supply.
A bureau is the only provider of its service, so the sponsor that funds it has no rival to compare against and no market price to read (Niskanen; Downs).
And a hidden cost beneath it. Only the bureau knows what the service truly costs to produce, so the sponsor cannot tell an efficient budget from an inflated one (Niskanen).
And a reward measured in budget. The chief is rewarded not by profit but by the size of the budget, which brings staff, salary, standing, and discretion, so it maximizes the budget and takes nothing it should not (Niskanen; Tullock).
And a take-it-or-leave-it offer. Being the sole supplier the sponsor cannot cost, the bureau bundles output and funding, so the sponsor funds the whole package or gets nothing, unable to buy just the efficient amount (Niskanen).
And the sponsor funds it. Wanting the service, unable to price it, and with nowhere else to buy, the sponsor approves the bundle, and the bureau captures the surplus as budget (Niskanen).
And government over-supplies. More of the service than its cost warrants, or the right amount at an inflated cost, so the state grows past the efficient size, the difference kept as budget.
And the loss is authored by no one. The chief grew the budget it is rewarded to grow, the sponsor funded a service it could not cost, and the public pays for a bigger government, a bill with no thief.
And the whole is held against its own limit. It needs a monopoly bureau, an under-informed sponsor, and budget-maximizing preferences, its magnitude contested, and lifts under cost visibility, a rival to compare, or a chief rewarded for efficiency (Migue and Belanger; Blais and Dion).

## Stakes

Whether a sponsor's power over the purse holds a bureau's budget to the efficient level, when the bureau alone can supply the service and alone knows its cost.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward a funded service, which the sponsor genuinely wants, and toward an over-supplied one, because the bureau, rewarded by the size of its budget and facing a sponsor who cannot cost or shop, bundles output and funding so the surplus is captured rather than trimmed. The production cannot make the sponsor's purse discipline the budget, because the power of the purse rests on the ability to cost the service, and that is the one thing the sponsor lacks: the monopoly of supply and the hidden cost are the one leverage seen twice, the funding the sponsor grants and the surplus the bureau keeps. The remedy, making the cost visible by audit, yardstick, or contracting out, or rewarding the chief for efficiency rather than size, spends real effort and is not certain, since the bureau knows its own costs best, comparison is imperfect where services differ, and some public goods cannot be bought on a market. It can only show that no one embezzled and no one blundered, that the chief who grew the budget and the sponsor who funded the service each acted soundly, that the over-supply is an extraction and not a theft, and that the public pays for a bigger government by the sum of reasonable choices. And it holds the bound in view, because the effect is conditional and its size contested: it needs a monopoly bureau, an under-informed sponsor, and budget-maximizing preferences, and lifts under cost visibility, a rival to compare, or a chief rewarded for efficiency (Migue and Belanger; Blais and Dion), so the whole of the misfit is that whoever alone can supply a thing and alone knows its cost sets the price of it, even to the sponsor who holds the purse.
