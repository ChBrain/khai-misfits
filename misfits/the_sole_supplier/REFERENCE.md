# The Sole Supplier: Reference

The research warrant for **The Sole Supplier**, a staging of the phenomenon the literature calls the
**budget-maximizing bureau** (Niskanen): that a government bureau is the only supplier of its service to
a sponsor who cannot see what the service truly costs, so the bureau, whose chief is rewarded not with
profit but with the budget itself, presents its output and its funding as a single take-it-or-leave-it
package and extracts a budget above the efficient level, and the sponsor's information disadvantage
cannot trim it, so government over-supplies at no one's fault. It is held with the finding that the
effect is conditional on a monopoly bureau, an under-informed sponsor, and budget-maximizing
preferences, and that its magnitude is contested, lifting where costs can be seen, a rival can be
compared, or the chief maximizes something other than the total budget.

## Line of Work

Staging the budget-maximizing bureau as a **monopoly-supplier-extracts-under-hidden-cost structure**: a
bureau provides a public service, and it is the only provider of it, so the sponsor that funds it, a
legislature or an executive, has no rival to compare it against and no market price to read. Worse, the
sponsor cannot see what the service really costs to produce, because only the bureau knows its own cost
function. And the bureau's chief is rewarded not by any profit, which it may not keep, but by the size
of the budget itself, since a larger budget brings more staff, more salary, more perquisites, more
prestige, more patronage and discretion, all the things a bureau chief values (Niskanen; Tullock). So
the chief maximizes the budget, and because the bureau is a monopoly supplier facing a sponsor who
cannot cost the service, it presents its output and its funding as a single package, take it or leave
it, so the sponsor can fund the whole thing or get nothing, and cannot buy only the efficient amount at
the efficient price. Facing a service it genuinely wants and cannot cost, the sponsor funds the package,
and the bureau captures the surplus as budget, delivering more of the service than is worth its cost, or
the right amount at an inflated cost, so government over-supplies (Niskanen). The ordinary expectation
is that a sponsor holds the purse and so holds the power; the finding is that the monopoly of supply and
the hidden cost hand the power to the bureau. The governing law is that **where a bureau is the sole
supplier of a service to a sponsor who cannot observe its cost, and the bureau is rewarded by the size
of its budget, it offers output and funding as one take-it-or-leave-it package and extracts a budget
above the efficient level, so government over-supplies, with no one embezzling and the sponsor unable to
correct it**. The play models the documented mechanics: the bureaucrat's reward in budget rather than
profit (Niskanen; Tullock), the bilateral monopoly between bureau and sponsor under hidden cost
(Niskanen), the take-it-or-leave-it offer that captures the surplus (Niskanen), and the variant in which
the chief maximizes discretionary slack rather than total budget (Migue and Belanger), as the
load-bearing vectors of the structure, and not as a claim that all public spending is waste.

It is a public-choice structure no staged misfit holds, and it is carefully distinct from its nearest
neighbours. It is distinct from Rent-Seeking, where real resources are burned competing to capture a
transfer: there the waste is the competition itself, many parties spending to win a prize, whereas here
there is no competition at all, a single monopoly bureau facing one under-informed sponsor, and the
waste is an over-supply extracted through a bilateral-monopoly information gap, not a race. It is
distinct from The Iron Triangle, where a committee, an agency, and a lobby supply one another's needs in
a closed ring: there the engine is a three-way alliance capturing a policy, whereas here it is a
two-party bargain, one bureau and one sponsor, and the over-supply comes from the bureau's monopoly of
supply and cost, not from a coalition. It is distinct from The High Water Mark, where each crisis lifts
spending and the spending stays when the crisis passes: there the ratchet is triggered by events and
persists, whereas here the over-supply is a standing feature of the bureau-sponsor relationship,
present without any crisis, driven by budget-maximizing under hidden cost. It is distinct from Ask the
One Who Sells, supplier-induced demand, where a paid expert advises more than a buyer would choose:
there the provider recommends to an individual buyer who cannot judge need, whereas here the bureau
bargains a whole budget with a sponsor who cannot judge cost, the lever the size of the funding package
and not the advice on a course of treatment.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the bureau
  chief as a crook or the sponsor as a dupe, because the finding it stages is that both act rationally: a
  chief rewarded by the size of the budget is right to grow it, since that is where its salary, staff,
  standing, and discretion come from, and it embezzles nothing; and a sponsor that funds a service it
  genuinely wants and cannot independently cost is doing the reasonable thing with the information it has
  (Niskanen; Tullock). The auditor who would open the books is not a zealot. The loss is authored by no
  one: it is a monopoly of supply plus a hidden cost plus a reward measured in budget, so the bureau
  extracts an over-supply through a take-it-or-leave-it offer the sponsor cannot counter, government
  grown past the efficient size by the sum of sound choices.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. The over-supply can be
  checked, but not for free: making the cost visible, by audit, by yardstick comparison with other
  jurisdictions, or by contracting the service out to read a market price, restores the sponsor's leverage,
  and a chief who could be rewarded for efficiency rather than size would not maximise the budget, but each
  of these is costly and resisted, the bureau knows its own costs far better than any auditor, cross-agency
  comparison is imperfect where services differ, and contracting out is not possible for every public good
  (Niskanen; Migue and Belanger). The reckoning is that the sponsor's power over the purse is only as real
  as its ability to cost the service, and where it cannot cost it, the monopoly supplier sets the terms.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the budget-maximising bureau, the bilateral monopoly
  under hidden cost, and the take-it-or-leave-it offer are Niskanen's, the bureaucrat's growth incentives
  are Tullock's and Downs's, and the discretionary-slack variant is Migue and Belanger's; the empirical
  appraisal that tempers the magnitude is Blais and Dion's and is drawn to mark the hedge. The mechanism is
  carried as carefully as the finding: that the effect needs a monopoly bureau, a sponsor who cannot observe
  cost, and a reward in budget, that the over-supply is an extraction through an offer the sponsor cannot
  counter and not a theft, and above all that it is conditional and its magnitude contested, since cost
  visibility, a rival to compare, or a chief who maximises slack rather than total budget each changes the
  case, and the pure doubling result is not settled fact. The scope is held to monopoly bureaus funded under
  hidden cost, and not stretched into a claim that all government is bloat or all public spending waste.

## Encoding

Source to constraint, per element, one per load-bearing vector, no filler.

- **The Monopoly**: carries the bureau as sole supplier of its service to the sponsor (Niskanen; Downs), so
  there is no rival to compare against and no market price to read, the structural fact that hands the bureau
  its leverage.
- **The Hidden Cost**: carries the information asymmetry (Niskanen), the bureau alone knowing what the
  service truly costs to produce and the sponsor unable to observe the cost function, so the sponsor cannot
  tell an efficient budget from an inflated one.
- **The Budget**: carries the objective (Niskanen; Tullock), the funding pool the chief maximizes because it
  brings staff, salary, perquisites, prestige, and discretion, the reward measured in size rather than profit
  and pursued without theft.
- **The Offer**: carries the mechanism (Niskanen), the take-it-or-leave-it package of output and funding, so
  the sponsor funds the whole thing or gets nothing and cannot buy only the efficient amount at the efficient
  price, the surplus captured in the bundling.
- **The Oversupply**: carries the phenomenon whole (Niskanen), the bureau delivering more of the service than
  its cost warrants or the right amount at an inflated cost, government grown past the efficient size, the
  surplus captured as budget and the cost borne by the public, with no one corrupt.
- **The Boundary**: carries the condition and the hedge (Migue and Belanger; Blais and Dion), that the effect
  needs a monopoly bureau, an under-informed sponsor, and budget-maximizing preferences, and lifts under cost
  visibility, a rival to compare, yardstick competition, contracting out, or a chief who maximizes slack
  rather than total budget, with the magnitude of the over-supply contested and carried lightly.
