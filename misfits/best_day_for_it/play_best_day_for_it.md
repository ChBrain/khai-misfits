# Play: Best Day for It

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Best Day for It.
Bright, and blowing hard, and the whole fleet is running. It is the best day of the month for generating and the worst day of the month for getting paid, and those are the same fact.
Start with the developer, who builds where the resource is best. That is not a failing. That is the job, and a developer that deliberately chose poorer ground to be less correlated with its rivals would be destroying its own returns in order to raise theirs. But every other developer of the same technology is looking at the same resource maps and reaching the same conclusion, so the output of the class is not merely similar. It is very nearly simultaneous, and nobody chose that and nobody can undo it alone.
And the plant bids zero. Also not a failing. A plant with no fuel has no marginal cost to recover, so zero is the correct bid and any other number would be a lie about its economics.
And the operator dispatches cheapest first, because that is what a merit order is for, and an operator doing otherwise would be raising costs on purpose.
Put those three correct behaviours together and the market clears at the bottom of the stack in exactly the hours the fleet is producing, which are exactly the hours it has to earn in (Sensfuss, Ragwitz and Genoese).
So the revenue per megawatt-hour falls as a function of how much of the technology has been built. Not because it got worse. Because there is more of it. And it would go on falling even if the technology got cheaper every single year, because the cost and the value are independent quantities and only one of them is being driven by the deployment (Hirth).
And nobody can see it in the numbers that matter politically. Installed capacity is up and to the right. Levelised cost is down and to the right. Both charts are honest and neither can show this, because a levelised cost averages over output without ever asking when the output arrives, and comparing that average across an intermittent technology and a dispatchable one is not comparing like with like (Joskow; Ueckerdt, Hirth, Luderer and Edenhofer).
And now the remedies, which is where it turns.
Storage, transmission across weather regimes, demand shifting: all three break the correlation, all three work, and all three are paid for by the system while the benefit lands on every generator of the class alike. So no developer will fund one, because funding it would raise its rivals' revenue exactly as much as its own, and a board that approved that would be asked why.
And the remedy that does pay the developer is a long fixed contract at a guaranteed price. It makes the project bankable. It works by removing the price signal from the party best placed to respond to it. So the instrument that finances the build is the instrument that suppresses the reason to build the thing that would fix it.
And nobody in it is doing anything wrong at any point.
And so the misfit is that a technology's success is the direct cause of its declining worth, through three correct behaviours and two remedies that fail in opposite directions. The market-value decline and the value factor are Lion Hirth's; the merit-order effect measured on a live market is Frank Sensfuss, Mario Ragwitz and Massimo Genoese's; the argument that levelised cost cannot compare an intermittent technology with a dispatchable one is Paul Joskow's; the system-cost framing is Falko Ueckerdt, Lion Hirth, Gunnar Luderer and Ottmar Edenhofer's. The house stages the correlated fleet, the zero bid, the value factor, the wrong metric, the unfunded fix, the contract that hides it and the bound, and holds the bound in view: that the trap needs units sharing both zero marginal cost and a common weather driver, that the decline is a function of penetration rather than a fixed property, and that it is mitigable by flexibility elsewhere in the system, so it is a reason to count value and not a reason to stop building, and the whole of it is that the better the day is for the thing you built, the less anybody has to pay you for it.

## Arc

The bend is this: where every unit of a generating technology has zero marginal cost and produces in the same hours as every other unit of it, the fleet suppresses the price precisely in the hours it earns, so revenue per unit falls with its own deployment independently of any fall in its cost, and the remedies that break the correlation are paid for by the system and benefit every generator alike.
It runs on a correlation nobody chose.
Every developer of the technology reads the same resource maps and builds on the same kind of ground, so the class's output is very nearly simultaneous, and no single builder can decorrelate itself without destroying its own returns to raise its rivals'.
And a bid of zero that is simply true. A plant with no fuel has no marginal cost to recover, and an operator dispatching cheapest first is doing exactly what a merit order is for (Sensfuss, Ragwitz and Genoese).
And a price at the bottom of the stack in exactly the earning hours, produced by three correct behaviours and no misconduct anywhere.
And revenue per unit falling with deployment. Not because the technology got worse but because there is more of it, and it would fall even if the technology got cheaper every year (Hirth).
And two charts that cannot show it. Installed capacity and levelised cost both improve, both honestly, and neither asks when the output arrives (Joskow; Ueckerdt, Hirth, Luderer and Edenhofer).
And a fix nobody will fund. Storage, transmission and demand shifting each break the correlation and each raise every rival's revenue as much as the funder's.
And a fix that is funded and makes it worse. A guaranteed price makes the project bankable by removing the signal from the party best placed to act on it.
And the whole is held against its own limit. The decline is a function of penetration and not a fixed property, it is mitigable by flexibility elsewhere, and it is a reason to count value rather than to stop building (Hirth).
