# Widest When You Need It: Reference

The research warrant for Widest When You Need It.
The term is the house's; the play runs on market microstructure and on what a standing two-sided quote costs the
person who has no choice about taking it, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging the quoted market as **a defence that is strongest when it is least affordable**: an asset is sellable
because somebody stands ready on both sides at prices posted in advance, to whoever arrives next, and that
standing quote is what a market is rather than a service laid over one. The gap between the two prices is not a
margin. In the model that established it, a risk-neutral quoter facing a stream of traders of whom some are
better informed must post a positive gap **even at zero transaction costs and zero expected profit**, because it
loses on every fill against the informed, cannot tell them apart at the moment of trade, and can recoup only from
everybody else. So the gap is set by the odds that the next seller knows something, and those odds are highest on
the day the reason to sell is shared, which is the day the greatest number of holders need to trade. The play
models the documented mechanics, the quote that makes an asset sellable, the spread that is a defence rather than
a fee, the widening that is the only instrument available, the capacity consumed by the trades the quote invites,
the depth measured honestly on an ordinary day, and the seller with a date rather than a view, as the
load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a tale of a dealer withdrawing out of greed.

The governing law is that the spread is the quoter's only defence against the counterparty who knows more, so it
must be widest exactly when the greatest number of people need to trade.

**Nobody in the sequence falls short, and that is what puts the failure outside anybody's conduct.** The quoter
stays on the screen and honours what is shown. The informed seller does the research that makes prices mean
anything. The risk officer measures a real market accurately. The forced seller follows a mandate. The central
bank restores the function.

What removes any possibility of blame from the quoter is that the alternatives are unavailable rather than
unattractive. It cannot ask an arriving seller what they know, since an honest answer would be against the
seller's own interest and cannot be verified. It cannot decline selectively, because declining to whoever looks
informed is declining to whoever is selling, which is not a market. And it cannot hold the gap fixed while the
odds move, because that is an open invitation to the one counterparty it loses to, after which it is gone and
there is no quote at all. Widening is what keeps the price on the screen.

What removes it from the informed seller is that the transfer is the point. A market in which nobody researched
anything would have prices that meant nothing, research is costly, and the quoter's loss to this party is exactly
what pays for it. The house stages that argument separately and does not relitigate it here.

**And the two peaks arrive together for one reason, which is where the structure becomes concrete.** A day when
many holders want to sell is by definition a day when the reason to sell is shared, so it is the day on which any
given seller is most likely to be acting on something. Independently, the capacity behind the quote is consumed
by the trades the quote invites, and on a one-way day nothing comes back. It has been observed in the deepest
market in the world: dealer capacity declined exactly as demand for it soared, spreads widened, liquidity
evaporated, and past a level of balance-sheet utilisation liquidity was worse than yield volatility alone
predicted.

**Then the number everybody sized against turns out to be about a different day.** Depth observed when flow runs
both ways describes a market in which the quoter's odds are ordinary. On the day they are not ordinary, the
quantity estimated and the quantity wanted are different quantities, and no amount of care collapses them.
Assuming worse is available and costs return on every day it is not needed, which somebody also answers for, so
the honest number is the one that gets used and the one that misleads.

It is distinct from the house's **The Weather We Make**, which stages endogenous risk, where a shared risk model
forces sales that validate the risk it measured, so the engine is a feedback through position limits. Remove that
engine, by stipulating no risk model anywhere and no forced deleveraging at all, and this misfit stands entire:
the gap widens on an information argument that needs no model, no limit and no feedback, and it would widen in a
market of unconstrained participants.

It is distinct from the house's **The Market for Lemons**, where the seller knows the quality of the particular
good and the market unravels toward no trade. Here the good is homogeneous and its quality is not in question:
what one party knows is where the **common** value is going, which is a different asymmetry with a different
outcome. Remove the lemons engine, by stipulating goods of perfectly observable quality, and this stands, because
the market does not unravel and instead stays open at a price that moves against whoever is not informed.

It is distinct from the house's **The Bank Run**, where a belief about what others will do is self-fulfilling and
the institution is solvent until the belief arrives. Remove that engine, by stipulating no coordination failure
and no belief-driven feedback whatever, and this stands: the asymmetry is real rather than believed, and the gap
would widen for a single seller on a quiet day carrying genuine news.

It is distinct from the house's **The Winning Bid**, which is the nearest relative and stages the winner's curse,
where the bidder who wins a common-value auction is the one whose estimate was most flattered. The logic is
cousin to this one, since the quoter also transacts precisely when it is on the wrong side. The difference is
where the cost lands: there it falls on the winner of a contest, who chose to enter it; here the quoter prices
the problem away **in advance** and the cost is carried by a third party who is not competing for anything and
has no view at all.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the quoter as
  withdrawing out of greed or the forced seller as reckless, because nobody in the sequence has a decision to get
  wrong: the standing quote is what makes an asset sellable rather than a slice taken from something that would
  happen anyway; the gap is positive in a model built with no costs and no profit in it; the quoter cannot ask,
  cannot verify, cannot decline selectively, and cannot hold the gap fixed without being picked off into
  insolvency, after which there is no quote at all; the informed seller is doing the thing prices depend on and
  cannot be identified at the point of trade; the risk officer's measurement is true, current and about a real
  market; the forced seller has a date rather than a view, and having no information is exactly what makes it
  indistinguishable from having some; and the central bank's rescue is correct and is not free. The failure is
  authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that the defence is strongest when it is
  least affordable.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Affirmative obligations can
  compel a quote and cannot compel a narrow one without bankrupting whoever is quoting, which ends with fewer
  quoters. Circuit breakers stop the trade, which is the spread going to infinity by another route. More
  competition among quoters compresses the calm spread and is thinnest under stress by the same argument that
  produces the stress. Holding a larger cash buffer is real protection that costs return every day it is not
  needed, and the seat holding it answers for that too. Central clearing and all-to-all trading change who
  intermediates rather than removing the asymmetry. A central bank backstop works, moves the cost to a party that
  was not in the trade, and becomes part of what the next calm measures. And knowing the whole structure, which
  the field does and has documented at length, leaves a redemption to meet on Friday and a screen.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never claimed
  as the house's. The staging is the house's; the microstructure model, the market-turmoil reviews and the
  official analyses are theirs. The bounds are carried with them: **which constraint bound dealers hardest in
  March 2020, regulatory balance-sheet cost or risk appetite or operational capacity, is genuinely contested**,
  and the play carries that argument rather than settling it; the model is a model and abstracts from inventory
  risk, competition among quoters and much else that matters in practice; one episode in one market is offered
  as an observation in the hardest case rather than as a general rate, and the size of the effect elsewhere is
  not established here; the account treats dealer markets, electronic market making and designated-quoter
  arrangements as one structure, which is its own simplification, since obligations and economics differ sharply
  between them; and the play makes no claim about whether liquidity provision has improved or worsened over
  time, which its own sources treat as a separate and disputed question. **The play makes no claim that any firm
  behaved improperly, that any intervention was wrong, that any rule should change, and it gives no investment,
  trading or risk-management advice of any kind**, only that the price is posted every day and is at its worst on
  the day it is taken.
