# Safe Enough to Fight: Reference

The research warrant for Safe Enough to Fight.
The term is the house's; the play runs on the stability-instability paradox, on the proportionality that
makes a very large threat unbelievable for a small stake, and on the quantitative and regional evidence
for and against it, all cited below.

## Line of Work

Staging deterrence as **a protection that opens the ground beneath it**: two states acquire the ability to
destroy each other, the largest war between them becomes genuinely unthinkable and stays unthinkable for
decades, and the space below that ceiling fills with border incursions, proxy wars and limited campaigns
that neither would have risked when a war between them could still be won. The play models the documented
mechanics, the ceiling that is real and holds, the response that exists at full strength and cannot be
used for a small stake, the claimant with a genuine grievance who calculates correctly that this will not
be answered with that, the government that declines to escalate and is right to, and the district or the
third country where the fighting actually happens, as the load-bearing vectors of the structure, not as a
tale of a reckless aggressor or a weak government.

The governing law is Snyder's, and he offered it as a restatement of what the strategic literature
already understood rather than as a discovery: the greater the stability of the balance of terror at the
strategic level, the lower the stability of the overall balance at its lower levels of violence. The
ceiling does not merely fail to cover the ground below it. **It is what clears the ground below it**,
because the same fact that makes the largest war unthinkable is what makes the largest threat
unbelievable for anything small.

What makes it structural is proportionality rather than capacity, and this is the distinction the whole
play turns on. Nothing here depends on a state being unable to commit, or unable to communicate, or
unable to strike. The weapons exist, are ready, and could be used for any provocation whatever. What
cannot be done is **want** to use them over a border district, and everybody involved can see that,
because the arithmetic is not hidden: a response out of all proportion to a provocation is not believed
however sincerely it is issued, and a threat that is not believed does not deter. So the deterrent is
present at every rung and effective only at the top one.

What removes any possibility of blame is that each party is calculating correctly, on a real interest.
The states that built the ceiling built something that worked and has kept working. The claimant acting
below the line usually has a claim of long standing that it has had no other way to press, and its
reading of what will not be answered is accurate. The government that declines to escalate is protecting
its own population from annihilation over a district, which is not weakness but the only defensible
choice available to it. **Nobody in the sequence is being reckless, and the recklessness is the sequence.**

And the harm settles where the decisions were not taken. The two principals are the ones the ceiling
protects; the fighting happens on a line of control, in a border province, or in a third country that
joined neither side's calculation, so the parties whose safety was purchased and the parties who pay for
it are not the same parties.

It is distinct from the house's **The Spent Margin**, which stages risk compensation and is the dedup
this candidate was slated owing: there the quantity is a **felt margin** handed to one actor by a safety
measure and re-spent, mostly without deliberation, inside that actor's own experience. Stipulate risk
compensation entirely off, so that no actor adjusts its own behaviour in response to its own protection,
and this harm survives intact, because the adjustment here is not to one's own safety but to **the
opponent's loss of a usable answer**. The claimant is not emboldened by feeling protected. It is
un-deterred by a threat it has correctly judged will not be made.

It is distinct from the house's **Moral Hazard**, where the loss is transferred to somebody else and the
covered party takes risk it would not otherwise take: stipulate that every party bears the whole of its
own loss and the harm survives, because the two states do bear their own losses in the limited war, and
what makes the limited war available is that it **cannot become total** rather than that somebody else
pays for it.

It is distinct from the house's **You Have to Mean It**, which stages the deterrence paradox of intending
what it would be irrational to perform: there the quantity is the ability to bind oneself to the later
act. Stipulate perfect precommitment, so that any state can bind itself to anything and be believed, and
this harm survives, because **no state would choose to bind itself to annihilation over a district**, and
the adversary's confidence rests on that choice rather than on any inability to make the commitment.

It is distinct from the house's **Exactly How Far**, where a guarantee's necessary vagueness is read by
three audiences at once and the adversary reads it for what it does not cover: that structure needs a
guarantor, an ally and an adversary. Stipulate two parties only, with no alliance and no guarantee to
anybody, and this harm survives entirely, because two nuclear-armed neighbours with a disputed border
need no third party for the ceiling to clear the ground.

And it is distinct from the house's **The Balloon Effect**, where enforcement displaces the activity it
suppresses to where the enforcer is not: there the quantity is the **coverage** of the enforcement, a
matter of where the resource was placed. Stipulate unlimited coverage, the deterrent present at every rung
at once, which it already is, and the harm is untouched. **Presence is not credibility**, and what fails
here is not the reach of the threat but the belief in it.

It runs against the house's **The Bank Run**, on the same axis and with the opposite sign. There a credible
guarantee removes a self-fulfilling run and the depositor who stays put does nothing more dangerous for being
covered, so the cover is pure gain. Here the cover is equally credible and equally effective at the level it
covers, and its completeness at that level is exactly what opens the level beneath it, so the same thickening
of the shield runs the other way.

**What sets the sign on this axis is whether the cover changes the behaviour it insures**, which is the
discriminator that warrant already states. This play is the limiting case of it. The behaviour is not permitted
by the cover the way a bank's risk-taking is permitted by a backstop, and it is not recruited over a generation
the way a floodplain's settlement is recruited by a levee: it is **read off the cover directly**, by both
parties, as arithmetic, because a shield that makes the largest war unthinkable is a shield that makes the
largest threat unusable for anything smaller.

## Restrictions

What the house refuses to model, and how it credits what it stages.

- **No villains**: the elements are reduced to load-bearing vectors. The play refuses to cast the state
  acting below the line as a reckless aggressor or the state declining to escalate as weak, because each
  is calculating correctly on a real interest: the claim being pressed is usually of long standing and
  has had no other route, the reading of what will not be answered is accurate, and a government that
  refused to trade its population for a district is making the only defensible choice available to it.
  The ceiling itself was built by people trying to prevent a war, and it prevented one. The failure is
  authored by nobody and emerges from the structure, so the misfit is that the protection which removes
  the largest war is what makes the smaller ones survivable enough to start.
- **No hero**: the play refuses to resolve the misfit into a costless correction. Building credible
  intermediate options, which is the answer the strategy literature actually gave, works by putting back
  the usable violence the ceiling removed, and every rung added to the ladder is a rung somebody can
  climb. Conventional rearmament reaches the low end and is expensive, permanent, and read by the other
  side as preparation. Declaring that any incursion will be answered at full strength is available, free,
  and not believed. And settling the underlying claim is the real remedy, which is to say the one the
  parties have already failed at for as long as the claim has existed.
- **Credit, not claim**: the research above is other people's work, packaged and attributed here, never
  claimed as the house's. The staging is the house's; the strategic studies and the quantitative
  international relations are theirs. The bounds are carried with them and they are unusually wide.
  **The paradox is contested**, and not at the margins: the leading regional study reads the same events
  as driven by conventional imbalance and revisionist aims rather than by the shield, the quantitative
  work reports findings that cut both ways, and the whole literature rests on a small number of dyads
  observed once. **It is a claim about a tendency and not about any particular war**, so no episode
  should be read out of this play as explained by it. **The magnitudes and the identification strategies
  those sources report are deliberately not carried here**, because the works themselves could not be
  read in the pass that cited them: publisher hosts and the article archives are blocked to it, so the
  bibliography, the findings and their directions were verified by search and corroborated across
  independent reports, which is the treatment a citation survives and a reported figure is not. **The
  play makes no claim about whether anybody should hold these weapons**, which is a question it is not
  equipped to answer and does not raise.
