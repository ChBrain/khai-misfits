# Play: Too Sure to Be True

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Too Sure to Be True.
Many observers judge the same question independently, each honestly, each carrying its own small error. And if they are truly independent, their answers should scatter: a spread of near misses around the truth, some high, some low, rarely two exactly alike. That spread is what independence looks like. It is the signature of many separate minds each making its own mistake.
But suppose a shared systematic error is present, unseen. A line-up built so that one face stands out. An instrument miscalibrated the same way for every reading. An assumption every observer holds in common. Then the judgments do not scatter. They fall into line, and the agreement becomes near perfect, because the same tilt lies under every one of them. And here is the paradox, set down formally by Gunn and colleagues: past a point, each new voice added to a unanimous chorus should lower confidence in the conclusion, not raise it, because a perfect agreement is better explained by the systematic error than by the truth, since truth plus honest independent noise would have produced some dissent (Gunn, Chapeau-Blondeau, McDonnell, Davis, Allison and Abbott).
And here is the trap. The naive reading runs the other way. Each observer, seeing its own judgment confirmed by the next, and the next, takes the agreement as corroboration and grows more certain the more unanimous the verdict becomes. So the confidence rises exactly as the ground for it falls away, and the collective grows too sure to be true, the very perfection of the agreement the mark against it, and no one in the chorus able to hear it from inside.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. Each observer judges honestly and independently and in good faith, and no one colludes. The shared error that aligns them, the biased line-up, the miscalibrated instrument, the common assumption, is unseen and unintended, planted by no one. And reading agreement as corroboration is the ordinary and sensible thing, the correct rule when the errors really are independent, which is the case the reader cannot tell apart from within. No one's dishonesty makes the verdict too clean. The loss is authored by no one: it is the false certainty placed in a conclusion whose very unanimity indicts it, and the misfit is that the perfect agreement which should reassure should instead alarm.
And here is the way out, and why it is costly. The escape is to seek dissent on purpose, to test whether the judgments were truly independent, to hunt for the common cause: a red team, a second instrument, a line-up rebuilt, and to weight the verdict by the prior chance of a systematic error rather than count the agreeing voices (Gunn et al.). But this spends effort and time and the comfort of a clean consensus, it can be run on only some questions and never removes uncertainty entirely, and it asks the judge to distrust the very agreement that feels like proof. The escape works against the grain of the intuition that more agreement is always more evidence, and none of it is the reassurance a unanimous verdict seems to offer.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The alarm is a Bayesian weight on the prior probability of hidden correlation, and not a rule that unanimity is ever proof of error. Genuine strong evidence can be near-unanimous, and a real signal will rightly command wide agreement, so the paradox is not a licence to distrust every consensus but a caution that agreement past what independent error would produce is a flag to check for a common cause. The model and the identification line-up are Gunn and colleagues', the ancient rule that a unanimous conviction acquits is the Talmud's, the jury theorem whose independence assumption is the hinge is Condorcet's, and the principle that systematic errors correlate while independent errors scatter is metrology's. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that agreement too perfect for independent error to have produced should alarm, and not that consensus is generally worthless or the majority usually wrong.

## Arc

The bend is this: when independent honest judgments should scatter, their perfect agreement is more likely a sign of a shared systematic error than of the truth, so the very consensus that should reassure should instead alarm.
It runs on a scatter that should have been there and is not.
Many observers judge the same question independently, each carrying its own honest error.
And truly independent, their answers should scatter, a spread of near misses around the truth (Condorcet's assumption).
And a shared error lies unseen beneath them. A biased line-up, a miscalibrated instrument, a common assumption, tilting every judgment the same way.
And the judgments fall into line. The agreement becomes near perfect, too perfect for independent error to have produced.
And the reading grows more certain. Each agreeing voice taken as corroboration, so confidence rises as the ground for it falls (Gunn et al.).
And it is authored by no one. Each observer judges honestly and the common cause is unseen, so the loss is the structure's and not a fault.
And the way out is to seek dissent. To test independence and hunt the common cause, costly and against the grain, and the bound held, the alarm a Bayesian weight and not a proof of error (Gunn et al.).
