# Play: Don't Look

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Don't Look.
The statement is in your inbox, the login is a tap away, the result is ready at the desk, and you could know your own situation in a moment and for nothing. That is the strange part: the information is free, it is yours, and looking would cost you almost nothing. But you have a fair idea the news is bad, and learning bad news hurts, so you do not open the statement, you do not log in, you leave the envelope where it lies. It is not laziness and it is not stupidity: it is a flinch, the same flinch that turns the eye from a blow it sees coming. Dan Galai and Orly Sade gave it a name, the ostrich effect, first seen in investors who would rather not receive news of their losses. Niklas Karlsson, George Loewenstein and Duane Seppi showed it plainly: people check the value of their portfolios far less often when markets are falling than when they are rising, so the looking tracks not how much the news matters but how much it is likely to hurt.
And here is why the flinch is rational, in its own coin. To look away buys something real and immediate: the pain of the bad news is spared, at least for now, and nothing is felt to be lost in the sparing. Free information is not free to receive, because what it carries is a cost of its own, the bad feeling, the pressure to act, the fact that once known a thing cannot be unknown (Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein; Sweeny and colleagues). So at the moment of choice, not-looking is the comfortable move and looking is the painful one, and the comfortable move wins.
And here is the turn. What the flinch defers is not the bad news itself but only the knowing of it, and the thing the news is about does not wait. While the eye is averted the problem goes unwatched, and a problem unwatched is a problem free to grow, so the debt swells, the symptom worsens, the small fixable trouble becomes a large one, and all of it in the dark the not-looking made. Worse, the attention is lowest exactly when it would matter most, because it is precisely when the news is bad that the situation most needs tending, and precisely when the news is bad that the eye turns away. The looking and the needing to look pull apart at the very moment they should meet.
And here is why no one in it is a villain. The one who looks away is not careless and not a coward: sparing yourself a present pain is a real and human thing, and the news genuinely will hurt. The loss is authored by no one. It is the problem that grew unwatched because looking hurt, the trouble that was still small enough to fix while the eye was turned away, and the misfit is that the very moment the news matters most is the moment it is hardest to face.
And here is the way out, and why it is so hard. The flinch wins because it is left to the moment, so the remedy is to take the choice out of the moment: to commit in advance to look, to set the monitoring on a schedule, to make the news arrive whether or not it is sought, to make not-looking harder than looking. But the forced look is not free and not always right. To be made to stare at bad news you can do nothing about is distress for no gain, some news is genuinely better not known, and even where looking would help, the pull to look away returns the next time the news turns bad. The comfort of not-looking is immediate and its cost is deferred and diffuse, so wherever the looking is left to the will in the moment, the flinch keeps winning.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The ostrich effect is real and documented, in Galai and Sade's prices and Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi's monitoring, but it is of varying and debated magnitude and it is not universal: some watch their situation more closely under stress, not less, the meerkat to the ostrich, and not all not-looking is a flinch, since some information is genuinely not worth its cost or cannot be acted on and declining it is the sound rational ignorance the house stages elsewhere. The name is Galai and Sade's, the selective-attention evidence Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi's, the information-avoidance frame Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein's, and the psychology of when the eye turns away Sweeny and colleagues'. The house stages the structure and holds the bound in view: that where you can learn your own likely-bad situation for nothing, you may well not look, and least of all when the looking matters most.

## Arc

The bend is this: where an agent can obtain information about its own situation at little or no cost but the news is likely to be bad, it avoids the information to spare itself the anticipated pain of learning it, so it monitors least exactly when the news matters most, and a problem it could still act on goes unwatched while it remains addressable, the avoidance locally comforting and globally costly.
It runs on a look that would cost almost nothing and is not taken.
The information is free and to hand: the statement, the login, the result, yours to read in a moment (Galai and Sade).
And the news is likely bad. You have a fair idea what the looking would show, and it is not good.
And so the eye turns away. Learning bad news hurts, and the anticipated pain makes the free information costly to receive, so you do not look, and least of all when the news is worst (Golman, Hagmann and Loewenstein; Karlsson, Loewenstein and Seppi).
And the thing does not wait. What is deferred is the knowing, not the news, so while the eye is averted the problem grows, still small enough to act on and going untended.
And the attention fails when it is needed most. It is when the news is bad that the situation most needs tending, and just then that the looking stops, so the two pull apart.
And it is authored by no one. The avoider spares a real pain and is no coward, and the loss is the unwatched problem's growth, not anyone's fault (Sweeny and colleagues).
And the whole is held against its limit. The effect is real but debated in size and not universal, some watch more under stress, and not all not-looking is a flinch, some being sound rational ignorance.
