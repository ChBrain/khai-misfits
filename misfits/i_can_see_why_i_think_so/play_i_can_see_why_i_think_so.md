# Play: I Can See Why I Think So

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

I Can See Why I Think So.
An analyst forms an estimate, and forming it is the same act as assembling everything behind it.
A colleague, asked, sends an estimate of their own: one number, competent, and arriving alone.
The analyst moves a short way toward it, which is exactly what a person does with a claim they cannot inspect, and the midpoint would have been closer.

## Arc

The bend is this: the two things being weighed are not the same kind of object, and only one of them can be looked at.

Take the judge's own estimate first, because the play has to establish that nothing is being hidden from them by anybody. Forming an opinion **is** assembling the case for it, so a person's own estimate arrives with its reasons attached, permanently and for free. They can turn it over, find the weak step, and say why they landed where they did.

Then take what the adviser sent. A number. **The reasoning stayed where it was made**, because a conclusion is what an opinion is, and it is what was asked for, and sending the working is a different and longer piece of work nobody commissioned. The adviser is competent, is not being lazy, and has done what a second opinion is.

Then the weighting, which is the part that looks like a flaw and is not. The judge is asked to weigh a supported claim against a bare one, and weights the supported one more. **Doing otherwise would mean acting on the strength of reasoning they have not seen**, which is not caution and not modesty but a guess about something invisible. The discounting survives when the judge rates the adviser highly, so it is not a verdict on anybody.

Then the arithmetic, which is where the loss lives. Where two estimates carry independent errors, **their average is closer to the truth than a typical one of them**, and that is a property of errors rather than a claim about who is right. It holds even when the judge really is the better forecaster. So the move that would have paid is one the judge has no reason on the table to make, because the case for it is not about this adviser at all.

And nothing in the ordinary run of the work corrects it. **The average is a counterfactual nobody scores.** The judge sees what their revised estimate did and never what the midpoint would have done, so the evidence that would teach the lesson is the evidence the procedure does not produce. A person can do this for a career and accumulate no experience of it.

Reach for a settlement and each one gives back the thing that was wanted. Ask for the reasoning, and the second opinion becomes a second piece of work, which is what an opinion was got instead of. Average by rule, and you give up the cases where the judge does know more, which are the cases a judge is employed for. Take more advisers, and the same discounting runs on each of them. Explain the effect to the judge, and you have asked them to weight something they still cannot see.

## Stakes

Whether two estimates can be combined by somebody who can only inspect one of them.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether the judge is proud, because the discounting survives when they rate the adviser highly, and not whether the adviser was careless, because a conclusion is what an opinion is, but whether a comparison can be made between an object that carries its reasons and an object that has lost them. The production cannot settle it. It can only show that the judge's own case is visible and the adviser's is not, that weighting them equally means guessing at something invisible, that the midpoint would have paid, and that nobody computes the midpoint afterwards to find out.
