# Play: The Vote Takes Four Minutes

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

The Vote Takes Four Minutes.
The agenda was posted, the doors were open, the gallery was free to anybody who came, and the minutes are accurate.
Item seven is called, one member speaks for ninety seconds, nobody dissents, and the vote is recorded.
Everything in that account is true, and nothing in it is where the matter was settled.

## Arc

The bend is this: the rule attaches to an occasion and the deciding is not an occasion.

A statute defines a meeting, usually by a quorum and a matter within the body's remit, and requires that one to be held where anybody may watch. **That definition is what makes the rule enforceable**, and it is also the whole of its reach. Every other way in which five people speak to one another is outside it, not because anybody drafted around it but because no drafter can enumerate them.

Take the rule first, because the play has to establish that nothing failed there. The session was noticed and open. The agenda was published. The vote was taken and recorded. No exemption was invoked and none was abused. **The gathering the statute defines is exactly the gathering that happened in public**, and if the question is whether the law was obeyed, the answer is that it was, completely.

Then the fact that moves the deliberation. **A position stated in a durable record is a different object from a position stated among colleagues.** The record is read later, by constituents, by employers, by the people who will judge a career, and it is read without the room's context. So a member who would say "I am not sure about the third option, talk me out of it" among five people will not say it into a transcript, and the reticence is an accurate reading of what a record does rather than a want of courage. The measured version is a central bank's: after it began publishing full transcripts of its meetings, its members were less willing to voice dissent in them.

Then the venue. What the statute leaves undefined does not stay empty, because the work still has to be done: the briefing, the one-to-one, the call, the two members who meet the officer separately. **None of that is a conspiracy and most of it is how a body informs itself.** By the time the doors open, the positions are known, the objections have been heard and answered, and the item takes four minutes because there is nothing left that anybody needs to say aloud.

And the watcher is left with the hardest part, which is that **what they are shown is not false**. The session is a real session and the vote is the real vote. Nothing is concealed and nothing is misreported. What is missing is the exchange of reasons, which happened, in full, somewhere the rule does not reach. **A citizen watching a ratification cannot tell it from a body that simply agrees**, and neither can the minutes.

Reach for a settlement and each one gives back the thing that was wanted. Widen the definition to catch serial contact, and it catches the ordinary conversation between two colleagues, so a body that may not confer arrives less prepared. Require the deliberation to be conducted in public and not merely held there, and you have asked a rule to police the state of mind a member walks in with. Publish more, transcripts as well as minutes, and the measured evidence says the dissent thins further. Exempt preliminary discussion, and you have conceded the thing the requirement was for.

## Stakes

Whether a citizen can watch a body decide.
Every plot moves the same question forward: not whether the law was obeyed, because it was, and not whether anybody schemed, because nobody did, but whether a duty that attaches to an occasion can hold a decision in place. The production cannot settle it. It can only show that the rule worked, that the record is what makes a stated position expensive, that the deliberation went somewhere lawful, that the session is real, and that every widening of the rule reaches something the rule should not have.
