# Play: Back to Even

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Back to Even.
An investor looks at a portfolio and sorts it, without quite meaning to, by a single figure: the price paid for each holding. The ones now worth more than was paid are winners, the ones worth less are losers, and faced with which to sell, the investor sells the winners and keeps the losers. Ask why, and the answers sound reasonable: take a gain while you have it, and do not sell a good company just because it is down, wait for it to come back. But the pattern is the exact reverse of what returns and taxes would counsel, and it is remarkably consistent. Odean, going through tens of thousands of brokerage accounts, found investors sold their winners at a far higher rate than their losers, and, tellingly, the winners they sold went on to outperform the losers they kept. The disposition to sell winners and ride losers was not shrewd. It cost return, and by realising gains rather than losses it raised the tax bill besides (Odean).
And here is the shape beneath the habit. The price paid becomes a reference point, and value is felt not in the abstract but as a gain or a loss measured from it. Above the line, in the domain of gains, people are risk-averse: a sure gain in hand beats the gamble of holding for more, so the winner is cashed. Below the line, in the domain of losses, people are risk-seeking: the gamble of holding for a recovery beats accepting a sure loss, so the loser is held. It is one asymmetry, the value function concave over gains and convex over losses, seen on its two sides, and it produces both halves of the behaviour at once (Tversky and Kahneman; Shefrin and Statman).
And here is what makes the loser so hard to let go. To sell it is to realise the loss, to close the account at a loss and admit it, and admitting a loss is painful in a way a paper loss is not: while the holding is kept, the loss is only on paper and the hope of a rebound is alive, but the moment it is sold the loss is real and the mistake is owned. So the investor resolves to sell it once it is back to even, once it returns to the price paid, and waits on a level the market has no reason to honour, while the holding may fall further still. The purchase price, which is sunk and should bear nothing on whether the holding is worth keeping now, becomes the one thing the decision waits on (Shefrin and Statman).
And here is the cost, and why it wears as bad luck. Each move looked reasonable in isolation: the gain was real and taken, the loser was a good company held through a dip. But over time the winners sold climb on out of reach and the losers kept sink further, and the account falls behind the very market it holds, bleeding return and, through the tax, a little more. The loss is authored by no one and blamed on the market, because no single trade was foolish, only their pattern, the systematic cutting of gains and riding of losses, and a pattern is invisible from inside any one of its parts.
And here is the way out, and why it is hard to hold. Judge each holding by its prospects and not by what you paid: let the winners run while their outlook is good, cut the losers whose outlook is poor, and take the loss where it should be taken. Set a rule that closes positions on their merits, and lean on the tax calendar, which at year-end turns the effect around by making losers worth selling for the deduction (Odean; Shefrin and Statman). But the fix asks exactly the thing the reference point resists: to drop the purchase price from the decision, and dropping it means admitting the losses it was hiding, one painful realisation at a time. And it is not a blanket sell, because holding a fallen holding is not always wrong, a price sometimes truly does recover, so the discipline is to judge, not to reflexively cut.
And so the misfit is not that anyone is careless but that a figure that should not matter, what you paid, rules the sale, cutting gains short and riding losses long. The disposition effect and its roots in prospect theory, mental accounting, and regret are Shefrin and Statman's, the account evidence that the sold winners beat the held losers is Odean's, and the reference-dependent value function, risk-averse over gains and risk-seeking over losses, is Tversky and Kahneman's. The house stages the asymmetry and holds the bound in view: that the pull to sell winners and ride losers is real and costly and common, and that a rule, a tax calendar, and the sense to judge a holding on its future can loosen it, though never without the sting of admitting, at last, a loss.

## Arc

The bend is this: because the price paid becomes the reference point and people are risk-averse over gains but risk-seeking over losses, and realising a loss is painful, investors sell holdings that have risen and hold holdings that have fallen, so gains are cut short and losses are ridden in hope of a rebound, and returns and taxes both suffer.
It runs on a portfolio sorted by the price paid.
Each holding is a winner if it is worth more than was paid and a loser if it is worth less, the purchase price the line between them, a figure sunk and irrelevant to the future yet kept as the reference point (Shefrin and Statman).
And value turns across that line. Above it, in gains, people are risk-averse and prefer the sure thing, so the winner is cashed; below it, in losses, people are risk-seeking and prefer the gamble, so the loser is held, one reflected value function producing both (Tversky and Kahneman).
And the loser is hard to close. Realising a loss means admitting it, painful in a way a paper loss is not, so the investor resolves to sell only once it is back to even and waits on a price the market need not return to (Shefrin and Statman).
And the account bleeds. The winners sold climb on and the losers kept sink further, so the portfolio falls behind the market, and realising gains not losses worsens the tax (Odean).
And the loss is authored by no one. No single trade was foolish, only the pattern of cutting gains and riding losses, so the cost wears as bad luck and is blamed on the market rather than the habit.
And a rule can loosen it, at a price. Judge holdings by prospects not entry price, cut losers on their merits, lean on year-end tax-loss selling (Odean; Shefrin and Statman), each asking the investor to drop the purchase price and admit the losses it hid.
And the whole is held against its limit. The effect is robust but bounded, reversed at year-end and lessened by rules and sophistication, and holding a fallen asset is not always wrong, only the systematic asymmetry is.

## Stakes

Whether a portfolio can be sold on its future, when a figure from its past, the price paid, has become the line the decision turns on.
Every plot but the last two moves the same way: toward the sale ruled by the purchase price, and away from the sale ruled by prospects, because above the line the sure gain tempts and below it the gamble on a rebound tempts, and the loss, once realised, must be admitted. The production cannot make the winner worth holding and the loser worth cutting while the purchase price is the reference point, because the value sought and the loss ridden are the one reflected value function seen on its two sides: the same asymmetry that cashes the winner rides the loser. The forces that undo it, a rule that closes on prospects, the discipline to realise a loss, the tax calendar that reverses the effect at year-end, each ask the investor to drop the purchase price and admit the losses it was hiding, and none is free, since the sting of admitting a loss is paid each time and the judgement of prospects can be wrong. It can only show that no one is careless and no one is duped, that the investor who takes the gain and holds the down holding is following the shape of value and the fear of a realised loss, that the cost is a pattern and not a run of bad luck, and that the account bleeds because a sunk figure ruled the sale. And it holds the bound in view, because the effect is robust but not a law of the ledger, reversed at year-end and lessened by rules and sophistication (Odean), and holding a fallen holding is not always the error, so the whole of the misfit is that what you paid, which cannot change and should not weigh, is exactly what keeps the winner from running and the loser from being let go.
