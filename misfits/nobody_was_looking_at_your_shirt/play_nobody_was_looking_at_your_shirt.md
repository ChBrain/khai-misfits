# Play: Nobody Was Looking at Your Shirt

## Estate

[khai-misfits](https://github.com/ChBrain/khai-misfits): the house that holds this production and answers for the whole run.

## Name

Nobody Was Looking at Your Shirt.
A person does something mildly conspicuous, wears a shirt they would rather not be seen in, stumbles, gives a wrong answer aloud, and to that person the moment is vivid, the whole of their own attention for a stretch of time. Asked, or simply left to guess, how many of the people around them noticed or will remember it, the person's estimate comes out far too high: because the thing is so salient to them, they anchor on that salience and adjust insufficiently for the fact that everyone else in the room is the center of their own attention and not of anyone else's, an egocentric anchor carried outward onto a perspective it does not fit (Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky, "The Spotlight Effect in Social Judgment: An Egocentric Bias in Estimates of the Salience of One's Own Actions and Appearance", 2000).
When the actual count is taken, in the classic demonstration a student wearing a conspicuous shirt into a room of peers and then guessing how many noticed it, the true number who noticed or later recalled it comes in well under half of what the wearer guessed, the gap between the felt spotlight and the room's actual, ordinary attention laid bare.
The mechanism is not a failure of the people who fail to notice, nor a delusion on the part of the one who feels watched. Each person in the room is, in fact, absorbed in their own concerns, their own appearance, their own conversation, and has only a fraction of their attention left over for anyone else, so the felt spotlight is real to the one who feels it and simply does not exist from where anyone else is standing.
No one here is a villain. The felt spotlight is a real anchoring effect and not vanity, and the room's inattention is not coldness or indifference, only the same ordinary self-absorption the anchored person is themselves feeling, projected outward and simply not returned. No one is preening and no one is failing to care; the gap between the felt spotlight and the room's real attention is authored by no one.
And the bias is bounded in a way that keeps it honest. It shrinks, though it does not vanish, once a person is given feedback about how little was actually noticed, and it shrinks somewhat further with familiarity, a close friend or a familiar face noticing more than a stranger would, but even a familiar observer notices and recalls far less than the one at the center of the moment fears (Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky, 2000; the wider egocentric-bias and self-focus literature that followed).

## Arc

The bend is this: the salience of one's own action or appearance to oneself is anchored on and insufficiently adjusted when estimating how salient it is to anyone else, so a person is judged, by their own account, to be far more watched and remembered than they in fact are.
It runs on a spotlight only one person can see.
A conspicuous moment happens, and it fills the one who lives it, vivid and whole.
And an estimate is anchored. How many noticed, guessed from how much it fills the mind that lived it.
And the room is glimpsed as it actually is. Each person there absorbed in their own concerns, attention mostly elsewhere.
And the count comes in. Far below the guess, the gap between the felt spotlight and the room's real attention laid bare.
And a familiar eye is met. Noticing somewhat more than a stranger, and still far short of what was feared.
And the estimate is adjusted. Smaller with feedback, though never all the way to what the room actually gave it.

## Stakes

Whether a person can ever see themselves as the room around them actually sees them, when the very vividness of their own conspicuous moment is what makes an accurate guess about anyone else's attention impossible to form from the inside.
Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the gap and away from an accurate estimate, because the Self's anchor is honestly set by what actually fills their own mind, and the Crowd's inattention is honestly the same self-absorption turned elsewhere. The structure cannot both let a moment be genuinely vivid to the one living it and guarantee that vividness translates into an accurate guess about anyone else's notice, because the vividness is the distortion: a mind that must judge another's attention has nothing to go on but its own, and its own is, by the nature of the moment, the loudest thing in the room to only one person in it. The remedies offered, a familiar eye that notices somewhat more, and feedback that corrects the guess somewhat, narrow the gap without closing it, since even someone who knows the Self well notices and recalls far less than feared, and a single corrected guess does not recalibrate every future conspicuous moment. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the felt spotlight is a real anchoring effect and the room's inattention is ordinary self-absorption and not coldness, and that the gap between them is authored by no one. And it holds the bound in view, hedged, because this is a documented bias that shrinks, though it does not vanish, with feedback and with familiarity, and not a claim that others never notice anything (Gilovich, Medvec and Savitsky, 2000; the wider egocentric-bias and self-focus literature that followed), so the whole of the misfit is that the spotlight a person feels on themselves is the truest thing in the room to them and, to everyone else standing in it, very nearly nothing at all.
