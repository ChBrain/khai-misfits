# Play: Larger in the Looking

## Estate

## Name

Larger in the Looking.
A person is asked, or asks itself, how much some single thing bears on how well its life goes. Would you be happier with a higher income, in a warmer climate, in this house, after this news, if you moved. It is a fair question, and to answer it the mind does the only thing it can: it brings the thing to attention and weighs it.
But a thing brought to attention looms large. The very act of attending to a factor magnifies its apparent importance, so what the mind returns is not a measure of how much the factor will figure in a life but of how much it fills the mind while the mind is fixed on it, and those are not the same thing (Kahneman).
So the judge overweights it. It predicts the factor will matter far more than it will, because in the judging the factor is the whole of the frame, while in the living it is one of many things and mostly out of focus: the higher income earned but not attended most hours, the fine climate stepped through on the way to the day's real concerns (Schkade and Kahneman).
And the gap is documented and wide. Midwesterners and Californians report about the same satisfaction with their lives, yet both expect the Californians to be happier, because asked to compare they fix on the climate, which is salient and favors California, and neglect the hundred other things that fill a life the same in either place (Schkade and Kahneman). And the belief that money greatly raises happiness is inflated the same way, income looming large under the attention fixed on it and moving the moods of actual days far less than the fixed attention predicts (Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz and Stone). It is the same focalism by which a person overpredicts how long a good or bad event will color its feelings, fixing on the event and forgetting the rest of the life that will go on around it (Wilson, Wheatley, Meyers, Gilbert and Axsom).
And here is why no one in it is a villain. No one deceives. The one who asks the question, a survey, a seller, a self weighing a large choice, directs attention to the factor innocently, because to weigh a factor at all one must bring it to mind. And the judge that overweights is doing the only thing a mind can do to gauge importance, bringing the thing to attention, where it looms. The error is authored by no one: it is attention magnifying what it lands on, and the misfit is that a factor's weight, judged while it is the thing in mind, is larger than the weight it will carry in a life where it is one of many things and rarely thought of.
And here is the way out, and why it is hard to take. One can widen the frame, attend to how a day is actually spent rather than to the factor alone, or recall that the factor will soon fall out of attention and be adapted to. But each asks the mind to hold in view exactly what the focused question crowds out, the whole diffuse rest of a life, which is many and hard to bring to attention against one salient thing. The escape works against the grain of how importance is estimated, which is by attending, and a mind cannot attend to everything at once.
And here is the bound that keeps it honest. The factor is not nothing. Income does raise reported life satisfaction somewhat, a warm climate is a real good, and the illusion is a documented bias in the judgments made while the factor is salient, not a proof that the factor never matters or that such judgments are worthless. What the looking inflates is the weight given the single attended thing against everything else. The demonstration and the name are Schkade and Kahneman's, the income case is Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz and Stone's, the focalism mechanism is Wilson, Wheatley, Meyers, Gilbert and Axsom's, and the aphorism is Kahneman's. The house stages the overweighting under attention and holds the bound in view: that the factor has a real but smaller effect than the focused judgment gives it.

## Arc

The bend is this: the act of attending to a factor magnifies its apparent importance, so a factor's contribution to well-being is overweighted whenever it is the thing being thought about, and the judgment of how much it matters is larger than how much it will matter in a life where it is one of many things and rarely in focus.
It runs on a mind that magnifies whatever it looks at.
A question is put, how much does this one thing bear on how well a life goes.
And the factor comes to mind, brought to attention to be weighed, which is the only way a mind can weigh it.
And it looms. The very attending magnifying it, the estimate of how much it fills the mind and not the life (Kahneman).
And the judgment overweights it. The factor the whole of the frame in the judging, predicted to matter far more than it will (Schkade and Kahneman).
And the rest of the life is neglected. The many diffuse things that fill the days the same, and the adaptation to come, all out of focus.
And the difference is small. The factor mattering far less in the living than in the looking, real but smaller, the bound held (Kahneman and colleagues).

## Stakes

Every plot but the last moves the same way: toward the overweight and away from the true weight, because to gauge a factor's importance the mind must bring it to attention, and a factor in attention fills the frame, so the estimate is of the frame and not the life. It can only show that no one is a villain, that the asker asks innocently and the judge does the only thing a mind can to gauge importance, and that the error is attention magnifying what it lands on, authored by no one.
