# Play: After You

## Estate

## Name

After You.
Picture a grand new marketplace, built and ready, its stalls swept and its doors open. It would be worth a great deal once it was full, buyers browsing a hundred stalls, sellers meeting a thousand buyers, each side there precisely because the other is. But this morning it is empty. A seller stands at the door and looks in: no buyers. Why pay to stock a stall no one will browse? A buyer stands at the same door and looks in: no sellers. Why cross the town to shop where there is nothing to buy? Neither is being difficult. For the seller the market is genuinely worth little until the buyers are there, and for the buyer it is worth little until the sellers are, because the whole value of the place is the meeting, and the meeting has not happened. So each waits for the other, politely, rationally, after you, and the doors stay open on an empty floor (Rochet and Tirole).
And here is what the two sides have done, which is nothing, and why the nothing is the trap. A platform like this joins two distinct groups, and each group's reason to join is not its own numbers but the other's: a seller wants buyers, not more sellers, and a buyer wants sellers, not more buyers. This is a cross-side pull, the value to one side sitting entirely in the other side's presence (Rochet and Tirole). So the first mover on either side pays now for a value that does not yet exist, and no one wants to be the first to an empty room. Each side's wait is the correct reading of what the place is worth today, and the two correct waits compose into a standstill neither intends, a chicken and egg with no first term (Caillaud and Jullien).
And here is the cost, and why it is so easily misread. The market never opens. The cross-side value that would have formed, had both sides been present at once, never forms, and a platform that would have served everyone well is written off as one nobody wanted. But the demand was real and the sellers were willing; the only thing missing was the coordination to get both sides through the door at the same time. The loss is authored by no one, because no side was wrong to wait, and it hides as a simple failure of demand when it is nothing of the kind: it is a failure to begin.
And here is the lever that opens it, and its price. The platform is not helpless, because it sets not one price but two, the charge to each side separately, and it can bend that structure. It can cut one side's price to nothing, or below, subsidise the sellers to stock the stalls, or let the buyers in free, to build one side first, and then recoup from the other side that now has a reason to come. This is divide and conquer, and it is the platform's real tool (Caillaud and Jullien; Rochet and Tirole). But the subsidy is a real cost carried against a launch that is still uncertain: spend it on the wrong side, or on users who take the free credits and leave, and the market still does not open.
And here is the deeper turn, the one that keeps the whole thing honest. The empty market is not a fate. It is one of two outcomes the very same platform can reach, and what chooses between them is what the two sides expect. If each side believes the other will come, each comes, and the belief is true; if each believes the other will not, each stays away, and that belief is true too. Both are self-fulfilling, so the same doors, the same stalls, the same town, open to a full market or an empty one according to nothing but expectation (Caillaud and Jullien; Katz and Shapiro). A first arrival can tip it: a marquee seller whose name draws the buyers, a seeded side that makes the floor look alive, and once one side is credibly there the other's belief turns, the cross-side pull reinforces itself, and the room fills.
And so the misfit is not that the market cannot exist but that it cannot begin itself. The two-sided platform with its cross-side externality and its price structure is Rochet and Tirole's, the chicken and egg and the divide-and-conquer remedy and the decisive role of expectations are Caillaud and Jullien's, and the network externality with self-fulfilling expectations that the two-sided case extends is Katz and Shapiro's. The house stages the standstill and holds the escape in view: that the failure is the bad equilibrium of a coordination problem, real and costly and common, and reachable of the good one only by paying to make two rational waiters believe, at the same moment, that the other will go first.

## Arc

The bend is this: a platform creates value only by joining two distinct sides whose benefit from participating depends on the other side's participation, so each side rationally waits for the other to commit first, and with both waiting the cross-side value never forms and a platform that would have served everyone fails to launch, no side wrong to wait and nothing scarce but the coordination itself.
It runs on a platform that joins two distinct sides.
A marketplace, an exchange, an intermediary is worth nothing in itself and everything as a meeting-place, its whole value the bringing together of two groups who need each other, buyers and sellers, riders and drivers, cardholders and merchants (Rochet and Tirole).
And it turns on where each side's value lies. Each side's benefit from joining rises with the other side's participation and not with its own, a cross-side externality, so the platform is worth a great deal when full and almost nothing when empty (Rochet and Tirole).
And so each side waits. Facing a platform empty on the other side, the first mover would pay now for a value that does not yet exist, so declining to go first is the correct reading of present worth, not stubbornness (Caillaud and Jullien).
And the two waits compose. Each side's rational wait meets the other's, and with both waiting the cross-side value never forms, a chicken and egg with no first term, and the platform that would have served everyone fails to launch (Caillaud and Jullien).
And the loss hides as a lack of demand. The market is written off as one nobody wanted, when the demand was real and only the coordination was missing, so the harm is a launch that never happened and it leaves no mark, authored by no one because no side was wrong to wait.
And the platform holds one lever. It sets not a price but a price structure, the charge to each side, and can subsidise one side below cost to build it and recoup from the other, divide and conquer, a real cost carried against an uncertain launch (Caillaud and Jullien; Rochet and Tirole).
And the whole turns on expectation. The failure is one of two equilibria, and what selects between them is what each side believes about the other: believe the other comes and you come, believe it stays away and you stay away, both beliefs self-fulfilling, so the same platform reaches the full outcome or the empty one by expectation alone (Caillaud and Jullien; Katz and Shapiro).
A first arrival can tip it, and it is bought not given. A marquee participant or a seeded side present before the other, absorbing the risk of the empty room, turns the other side's belief, and the cross-side pull reinforces itself until the room fills, the escape real but paid for and never guaranteed.

## Stakes

Whether a market that would serve everyone can ever begin, when each side is worth joining only once the other has already joined.
Every plot but the last two moves the same way: toward the meeting that would create the value, and away from making it, because for each side alone there is no reason to be first into an empty room. The production cannot make the first move worthwhile while the platform is empty, because the value of joining is the other side's presence, and the other side is not yet there: the worth and the emptiness are the one cross-side pull seen from either door. The levers that open it, subsidising one side to build it, courting a marquee whose presence draws the rest, and above all shifting what each side expects the other to do, each spend a real cost against an uncertain launch, and the coordination they buy is a belief that can fail to hold. It can only show that no side schemed and no side blundered, that the seller who will not stock and the buyer who will not shop are each reading the empty floor correctly, that the market's failure is a coordination not a lack of demand, and that the value which never formed leaves no mark because the meeting that would have created it never happened. And it holds the escape in view, because the empty market is not a fate but the bad equilibrium of a coordination problem, and the same platform fills or stays empty by what its two sides expect (Caillaud and Jullien; Katz and Shapiro), so the whole difference between the full room and the empty one is a coordination bought at a price and never assured.
